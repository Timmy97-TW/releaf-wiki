Results

Abstract

From engineered cells to precise plant protection.

ReLEAF brings together synthetic biology, plant experimentation, optogenetic control, continuous monitoring and membrane-based bioreactor engineering. We tested the major steps our proposed system needs: producing our protectants, evaluating their effect on plants, controlling biological activity with light, monitoring bacterial growth, releasing protectant through our bioreactor, and retaining engineered Bacillus subtilis inside it. Every stage below carries the question it had to answer and how far our own measurements reach, including the two places where they stop short: the light plate grows cells and gives a readable band but no graded response to green intensity, and the ACC deaminase ELISA has a design and a correction factor but no concentration yet.

Stress detection and prediction

Asks

Can sensor and weather data be turned into a decision about when to produce and when to apply?

Our planned interface puts a variety of inputs through AI, which assembles instructions for farmers on protectant application while actively monitoring conditions inside the machine. Our AIoT system is also what produces our protectant, activating the green light during optimal application windows.

ReLEAF's bioreactor is built to stand beside a rice field, and the person responsible for it there grows rice. That sets five jobs for the software before any model exists: show what the culture is doing while it runs, raise an alarm when a reading or the culture drifts, say how much biomass there will be in a few hours, say whether the protectant will be ready when the crop wants it, and answer in plain language. The bioreactor's sensors and valves are wired to a board that logs every reading, and the models learn from those readings; a sensor beside the crop adds leaf temperature and leaf wetness, which together say when the weather is letting disease into the field. The full build, the interface and the model code are on Digital Twin; what follows is what the models have been scored on.

What it reads

Inside the bioreactor the twin reads optical density, pH, flow rate and four pressures live, and it commands the pinch and Luer valves from the same interface. Outside it, a field rig reads leaf temperature, leaf wetness, air temperature and humidity, and a weather forecast supplies temperature, humidity, rain and wind. The models that sit on top of those two streams are a sensor alarm on the instrument and process axes and a batch forecast two to twelve hours ahead.

The alarm

A week can be lost before the next visit, so an instrument that is quietly failing has to be caught while its readings still look plausible. An autoencoder trained on the healthy first 120 hours of the 434-hour run scores two axes separately: a sensor axis on the reference beam, which never passes through the culture, and a process axis on the specific growth rate.

Forecasting

Along with this, we tested different forecasting methods for predicting bacterial growth and conditions.

The test ran on 102 hours of perfusion, 2 to 6 September 2026, with B. subtilis 168 secreting ACC deaminase constitutively, over which OD600 rose from 0.84 to 3.77. A Kalman filter, a local linear fit, a global linear fit and an exponential fit were each compared against persistence, which predicts no change at all. A forecast was made every hour from 12 h, each using only the data up to that point.

Global linear weighs early data as heavily as recent data, so it points the wrong way when growth changes speed. Exponential assumes growth keeps accelerating, which it does not over 102 hours of perfusion, so it overshoots. Local linear follows the recent level and slope; the Kalman filter also weighs each reading against the noise, so it follows real changes without chasing scatter. One caution holds for all of them: most of the run sits above the range where optical density is linear in cell count [5], so what is being forecast is the photometer reading. The comparison between models is unaffected, because every model forecasts the same signal.

Optogenetic control

Asks

Can the light plate deliver a controlled signal for optogenetic activation?

ReLEAF depends on light as the external control signal connecting environmental prediction to biological production. We therefore tested the light plate to determine whether it could provide reproducible illumination across the culture area.

For our light plate apparatus we measured both OD600 and Western blot at two-hour intervals from 2 to 8 h, and once more at 24 h. Twelve tubes of B. subtilis WB800 carrying the CcaSR system were run on the plate at 2%, 12% and 100% light intensity. Each green intensity had three tubes, and each red intensity had one. At every time point, 100 µL of each tube went to OD600 and 100 µL to Western blot. The Western blot covers 0, 2, 4, 6 and 8 h, and the 24 h sample was read for OD600.

Growth under light

OD600 was read at 2, 4, 6, 8 and 24 h, and all six panels share the same axes. The top row shows red 2%, 12% and 100%, one tube each, and the bottom row green 2%, 12% and 100%, three tubes each.

Through 8 h all six conditions grew alike, OD600 0.09 to 0.29, with more spread among green tubes at 6 h. At 24 h red 2%, 12% and 100% reached 1.026, 0.969 and 1.026, and green 100% reached 1.017. Green 2% at 0.073 and green 12% at 0.034 stayed at or near the blank. Because green 100% grew normally, this is not a simple dose effect of green light on growth, and these two 24 h readings require verification. OD600 reflects turbidity instead of specific protein output.

Protein over time

Western blotting was used to identify and measure ACCD produced after B. subtilis is exposed to different intensities of red and green light. Cultures were used in three ways: put directly into the plate and sampled every two hours from 0 to 8 h; put directly into the plate and sampled only at 13 and 16 h under green or red light at 100%; and grown overnight first, then moved into the plate under green light at 100% and sampled after 3, 6 and 8 h.

The first blot used wild-type AcdS, level 2 construct ABDI. The other two used the codon-optimised AcdS 2.1 variant, level 2 construct ABDQ, as will the remaining experiments. The reason behind this change was that the original AcdS did not expand well in liquid culture, so we had to repeat electroporation and the steps around it every time. AcdS 2.1 expands better and does not have this issue.

What it settles

The light plate held twelve tubes under matched red and green conditions, grew B. subtilis in all of them, and gave a readable ACCD signal on a Western blot, so it can serve as ReLEAF's light-control platform.

What the results do not yet show is a clean, graded response of ACCD to green light intensity: the red 100% reference was also strong at 8 h, and the 24 h OD600 of green 2% and 12% is unexplained. Before we rely on light intensity as a control signal, we need to repeat the 24 h point, quantify the blot against a loading control, and add a WB800 lane without the plasmid.

The plate did give the project one thing it uses elsewhere. A logistic fit to the 19 September run on this plate is the prior behind the growth rate registered on 1 October and scored on run 6 in section 2, which is the one prediction on this page written down before the experiment that tested it.

Protectant production

Asks

Can our engineered B. subtilis produce the intended protectants?

Following stress detection and optogenetic signal delivery, the next stage of ReLEAF is biological production. Our system uses engineered B. subtilis, strains 168 and WB800, as the microbial chassis for producing plant-protective molecules in response to a controlled light signal. To make the system easier to construct, troubleshoot and adapt, we organised our genetic design into four functional modules.

Module 1 · PCB production — supplies the chromophore phycocyanobilin that CcaS-m3 needs in order to sense light

PtagC+RBS Ho1–pcyA L3S2P21

Module 2 · Light sensing — once loaded with PCB, CcaS-m3 detects green light and autophosphorylates

PliaG RBS0 ccaS-m3 B0015

Module 3 · Transduction — CcaR takes the phosphoryl group from activated CcaS and switches on the output promoter

Pveg RBS0 ccaR B0015

Module 4 · Light-responsive expression — turns green-light signalling into expression of the AcdS protectant

PcpcG2-veg EEBD–RBS0 Csn–AcdS or SamyQ–AcdS 2.1 6×His B0015

The system comes in two versions. Both share the first three modules and differ only in the light-responsive expression module: ccaSR–Csn–AcdS–Histag, and ccaSR–SamyQ–AcdS 2.1–Histag.

A fifth module, the production module, constitutively expresses the stress-mitigation proteins so that their maximal secretion and detection can be evaluated. It runs Pveg, EEBD–RBS0, a protectant coding sequence and B0015. The coding options are AcdS, LEA14 and BoPep4, each fused to a Csn or SamyQ secretion peptide and a 6×His tag, in wild-type, truncated and codon-optimised forms.

Every module was assembled with the SubtiToolKit Golden Gate system [3]. Part-by-part detail, sequences and registry numbers are on Parts.

The four modules

Colony PCR was used as a rapid initial screen to identify transformants carrying inserts of the expected size before further verification. After transformation, individual colonies were picked with a sterile tip, streaked onto an agar plate for later recovery, and the remaining cells were resuspended in 2× Q-amp master mix and sterile water. Primers flanking the cloning site were used, so colonies with the insert yield a band of the expected size while colonies carrying the empty vector yield a shorter product or none at all.

The products were separated by agarose gel electrophoresis and compared against a 3k or 10k bp DNA ladder. Colonies giving a band of the expected size were selected for overnight culture and plasmid extraction, followed by further verification to confirm the construct.

Complete systems

After colony PCR identified candidate clones, we grew the positive colonies in liquid culture overnight and purified their plasmids. For the two complete systems the purified plasmid was then used as template for a second round of PCR. Compared with colony PCR this step is more precise: a clean template gives more reliable amplification and fewer false positives, so band sizes can be interpreted with greater confidence.

Selected four-module constructs were then amplified using specific primers designed for cross-module verification. Each primer pair binds in one module and extends into the neighbouring one, so that it spans the junction between them: module 1 to 2, module 2 to 3, and module 3 to 4. A product of the expected size is obtained only if the two modules are joined directly, so these reactions confirm both that the intended DNA fragments are present and that the modules were assembled correctly and in the right order.

Together, colony PCR, cross-module PCR and sequencing provided multiple levels of evidence that the genetic constructs were assembled as intended before being introduced into downstream expression experiments.

Production cassettes

The production module drops the light switch and expresses each protectant constitutively, so that maximal secretion and detection can be evaluated without waiting on the optogenetic circuit. Eight cassettes were built across three protectants: ACC deaminase, LEA14 and BoPep4, each with a Csn or SamyQ secretion peptide.

The gel images for Pveg–EEBD–RBS0–SamyQ–BoPep4–Histag–B0015 and for the 15–23 truncation are unavailable, but both constructs were successfully assembled and verified by gel electrophoresis.

Electroporation

To identify transformation efficiency under various electroporation conditions, two B. subtilis strains, 168 and WB800, were evaluated at different field strengths. Cells were recovered after the pulse and plated on selective LB or LBE agar, and the colonies on each plate were counted.

For strain 168 carrying Pveg–EEBD–RBS0–SamyQ–ACCD v2t1–B0015, 2.1 kV yielded 21 colonies against 8 at 2.3 kV, so recovery and viability were better at the lower field strength.

For WB800 carrying Pveg–SamyQ:ACCD v2t1, plating at a 1:2,000,000 dilution yielded high colony density at both 2.1 kV, 434 and 430 colonies, and 2.3 kV, 403 and 388. Increasing the voltage to 2.5 kV cut viability sharply to 15 colonies through excessive field strength and cell lysis, and plating at 2.1 kV without the dilution gave 16. On this evidence 2.1 kV is the optimal electroporation voltage for transforming WB800 with this construct.

The pattern is not the same for every construct. WB800 carrying pSTK–EXP2–CcaSR v1–AcdS v2t1 peaked at 2.3 kV with 370 colonies, against 268 at 2.1 kV and 233 at 2.5 kV, so 2.3 kV was taken as optimal there. For Pveg–BoPep4 15–23 the useful range was lower still: 1.5 kV yielded 6 colonies while 1.1 kV yielded 15, and the NC4 version of the same construct yielded 11 at 1.1 kV. Lower voltages appear to improve recovery and transformation yield for these constructs.

Protein expression

Three readouts sit behind the protein work, and each answers a different question. Western blot separates proteins by size, transfers them to a membrane and identifies them by antibody, so knowing our protectant's molecular weight we can tell whether the secreted components contain it. The DNPH assay uses 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine, which reacts with carbonyl groups to produce a brightly coloured derivative, and it gives us the enzyme activity of what our engineered B. subtilis secretes. ELISA captures the target on a microplate and detects it through an enzyme-conjugated antibody, and the signal is proportional to the amount present, so it reports a concentration against a standard curve.

The construct we focus on is ACC deaminase, because it carries the result of our codon optimisation. For the comparison we have two constructs that differ in both the secretory signal peptide, CSN against SamyQ, and the ACC deaminase gene sequence, wild type against codon-optimised v2t1. We quantify ACC deaminase in culture supernatants by anti-His ELISA to compare the expression and secretion of the two gene sequences.

The variance in signal peptide does influence the measured result, and due to time constraints we were unable to remove it with new cloning. To isolate the effect of codon optimisation, the signal peptide contribution is accounted for using published quantitative data. Documented values are limited and come from different studies: the native CSN signal peptide mediated 33.8% secretion of its cognate cargo in an E. coli system [9], and reached near 72% of the best signal peptide within a 173-member B. subtilis library for cutinase [10], while SamyQ has been reported to reach around 90% secretion of a heterologous α-amylase in B. subtilis [11]. With no direct comparison between the two peptides on ACC deaminase, the codon optimisation effect will be presented as a range, with a correction factor of 2.66 between the two peptides.

The ELISA has been run and the concentrations are not reported here. The standard curve, the interpolated supernatant concentrations and the fold difference between SamyQ:AcdS v2t1 and CSN:AcdS are being finalised by the team, and this chapter will carry them when they are. What exists on this page in the meantime is the design above, the correction factor, and the one piece of direct evidence that the secretion tag works at all: the fractionation blot in section 7, where ACC deaminase appears in the supernatant lanes and in neither pellet lane.

The chromoprotein reporter

AmilCP was undertaken as a stand-in reporter to quantifiably visualise the successful secretion of a desired construct from our engineered B. subtilis under our light plate apparatus and bioreactor. With chromoproteins we are able to use absorbance, since AmilCP gives a blue-purple hue, which is a quantifiable and relatively direct route compared with biochemical and protein expression assays. Enzymatic assays such as DNPH required substrates, some of which are explosive and dangerous to work with, on top of extra data and resources. Fluorescence-based reporters were not possible as we lacked the materials. Western blot is only semi-quantitative, indicating protein levels rather than numerical protein quantity, and ELISA, though quantitative, is set at a high price point for a single reaction, which was not feasible for our sample numbers.

Despite repeated experiments, and alongside the successful production of the construct within the bioreactor culture, the medium showed no purple colour. Spectrophotometric measurement of the supernatant after centrifugation also showed no absorbance peak at the expected wavelength, so no usable data could be obtained.

The likely explanation is a property of the chassis rather than the construct. Secreted proteins in B. subtilis cross the membrane unfolded and fold on the outside, and AmilCP's beta-barrel has to fold quickly to hold its chromophore, so a reporter that depends on fast folding is a poor match for this secretion route. We record the attempt because it decided the rest of the section: with the chromoprotein route closed, ACC deaminase and the DNPH assay became the way we justify activity.

Continuous culture monitoring

Asks

Can the inline photometer continuously monitor changes in culture density?

To monitor culture growth without repeatedly removing samples from the bioreactor, we developed an inline dual-beam photometer and evaluated its performance through long-term monitoring and independent turbidity calibration.

The 434-hour run

We first tested whether the photometer could operate continuously over an extended period.

From 21 July to 8 August 2026 it logged wild-type B. subtilis 168 at 22 °C with the hollow-fibre module fitted, every ten minutes for 434.2 hours, giving 2,671 valid rows out of 2,720 logged with no sample drawn and nobody at the bench.

The extended run shows that the photometer can collect culture-density measurements over long periods without repeated manual sampling, which is the thing a field bioreactor needs it to do. It also shows the limit. The optical density channel became unusable at 243.0 h, after the reference beam had already begun to drift at about 220 h, so the instrument degrades over a run of this length. That failure is what the sensor alarm in section 2 was built on, and it was caught 26.7 h early. Two further limits hold: the run was not sampled, so nothing independent confirms what the trace says the culture was doing, and no cell count was taken alongside it.

Titania

To determine whether the photometer responded predictably to increasing turbidity, we first calibrated it using TiO₂ suspensions. TiO₂ provided a nonliving turbidity standard, allowing us to characterise the optical response independently of changes in microbial growth.

Cumulative TiO₂ additions into a stirred 400 mL loop gave 45 paired points between the inline photometer and the BioDrop, from 2 to 60 mg. Up to an optical density of 1.2 the photometer follows the BioDrop with a slope of 1.018 and R² 0.995, and over all of its own points it fits a straight line at R² 0.987 against the BioDrop's 0.991. Above about 30 mg our readings are the steadier of the two. What the calibration establishes is a linear response to turbidity, and turbidity is not a cell count: converting one to the other needs a paired series against plate counts, which we have not run.

Silica

We next repeated the calibration using SiO₂ as a second particulate standard. Testing a second material allowed us to evaluate whether the relationship observed with TiO₂ was reproducible with a different source of turbidity.

Silica is also what the iGEM interlaboratory protocol asks for [5]. Titania was the standard we had on the bench in August; running the protocol's own material in October put our instrument on the scale the rest of iGEM uses.

Silica was added in 2.5 mg steps from 2.5 to 100 mg in 500 mL of 1% SDS, read at every step on the BioDrop and on both photometer builds, and logged inline every five seconds for 4 h 27 min. Across 40 steps the second build fits a straight line at R² 0.992 with a residual standard deviation of 0.016 OD, against R² 0.974 for the first build and 0.979 for the BioDrop, whose residual spread is ten times ours. The linear relationship seen with titania therefore holds with a second material, and holds better on the instrument we would deploy.

The run carries its own internal check. At 137.6 min the suspension was diluted and then restored, which tests proportionality without the dose being known: 2.3610 before with a standard deviation of 0.0033 over 29 readings, 0.7943 after over 38 readings, and 2.3665 restored over 16. The ratio across the dilution is 2.97 one way and 2.98 the other, and the restored reading returns within 0.2% of the original. A reading of 2.36 therefore carries the same proportional meaning as one of 0.79, which is the property the twin needs in order to compare one reading against another.

Protectant function

Asks

Do our protectants actually improve plant stress response?

To determine whether the protectants actually benefit plants, we used Arabidopsis thaliana as our plant model. We compared plants under normal conditions, stress conditions, and stress conditions supplemented with protectant. Our plant-validation experiments were designed to move from highly controlled agar-based testing toward a hydroponic system that more closely reflects our intended application pathway.

Two agents appear below, and they are not the same thing. ACC deaminase is the enzyme our own circuit secretes. CH bio peptide is a commercially available biostimulant, used here as a reference for what a working protectant looks like in this assay; nothing about its performance is a claim about ours.

Salinity on agar

We first evaluated protectant performance under controlled salt stress. The general framework crossed salt against protectant in four conditions: neither, salt alone, protectant alone, and both. Depending on the trial, different salt concentrations and protectant timings were evaluated. By comparing salt-stressed plants with and without protectant treatment, we could determine whether the protectant reduced the negative effects of salinity on plant growth.

Before any protectant was tested, the stress itself had to bite. A salt ladder at day 6 gives primary root lengths of 74.95, 34.77, 11.00 and 1.36 mm across 0, 75, 100 and 150 mM NaCl, nine seedlings per group, with non-overlapping spreads. That is the dose response every rescue experiment below is measured against.

ACC deaminase was then applied at three timings against that ladder, and this is the one experiment on this page where a protectant moves a plant measurement above its matched control.

Every ACC deaminase timing sits above the untreated control at the matching salt level, and the timing decides by how much. Normalised to fresh weight, panel (b), co-treatment reaches 3.2 against the untreated 1.0 with no salt and 2.4 against 1.85 at 75 mM, and by 100 mM it has fallen back to the untreated level. Pre-treatment is the steadier of the two: it stays above the control at all three salt levels, and in the unnormalised panel (c) it is the only timing whose chlorophyll rises as the salt rises, from 1.9 to 3.3 times the control.

Three cautions belong with the figure. The marked comparisons run within a treatment group across salt levels, not between a treatment and its control, so the four-star marks say that salt changed each group and not that the protectant beat the control. The replicates are three per bar inside one experiment, so this is one run and not a repeat. And the 100 mM post-treatment group was not determined, which leaves the most severe condition without its full timing comparison.

The second run of the same design is the reason the section is marked partial rather than measured. It points the same way, with every protectant timing above the untreated control, and its scatter is wide enough that no comparison in it would survive a test: single points sit four and ten times above their own bar mean. Until the two runs are reconciled, the claim this page is entitled to is that ACC deaminase raised chlorophyll under salt in one well-controlled experiment.

Heat on agar

We next evaluated plant response under controlled heat stress. Plants were divided into control and heat-stressed groups, with and without protectant treatment, allowing us to determine whether the protectants improved heat tolerance or recovery.

Seven-day-old seedlings were transferred onto sterilised agar plates identified as the four experimental groups: no peptide added, pre-treatment, co-treatment and post-treatment. Pre-treatment peptide was applied immediately after transfer on the seventh day; co-treatment a day later, alongside placing all heat-stress groups into the heat chamber; after five days at 37 °C all plates were returned to the 22 °C growth chamber, and the post-treatment group received peptide at that point. This interval design allowed the groups to separate on growth parameters under the imposed heat stress.

The heat work is at a different stage from the salt work. The peptide reference gives a clear chlorophyll separation by treatment timing; the ACC deaminase heat trial has its plates and its phenotype, and the chlorophyll and fresh weight numbers that would put it beside the salt result are still being worked up by the team.

Hydroponics

To move beyond agar-based proof of concept, we tested protectant performance in our self-made standardised hydroponic platform. The hydroponic system allowed plant roots to interact directly with the treatment solution and provided a closer approximation of how protectants could eventually be delivered through irrigation or nutrient circulation.

CH peptide demonstrates strong rescue effectiveness in 13-day-old Arabidopsis under 100 mM NaCl. Plants received seven days of 100 mM salinity stress, and CH peptide was applied for three days at four different times: not applied, three days before salinity, simultaneously with salinity, and three days after salinity was applied.

Co-treatment again carries the effect, at 0.203 g of fresh weight against 0.085 g untreated, and after-treatment sits below the control at 0.049 g. One point per bar is plotted, so these are single measurements and the ordering is what they support, not a tested difference. The heat version of this experiment in the hydroponic platform has been run and its numbers are not worked up.

The assays

Plant phenotype provides whole-organism evidence, and we also wanted to evaluate the biological activity of our protectant directly. For ACC deaminase we used an enzymatic activity assay to determine whether the produced protein retained measurable function.

Three assays stand behind that sentence and they are at three different stages. The DNPH assay, which reads ACC deaminase activity through the coloured hydrazone of the α-ketobutyrate it releases, is the activity route the team chose once the chromoprotein reporter failed, and it is running. The indophenol blue assay reads the other product, ammonia, and gives a second handle on the same reaction. The anti-His ELISA in section 4 reports how much enzyme is there, which is what turns an activity reading into a specific activity.

None of the three has a number on this page yet. Until they do, the honest statement of what section 6 shows is that ACC deaminase applied to a salt-stressed plant raised its chlorophyll content in one experiment, and that nothing here yet says how much active enzyme was in what was applied.

Whole-system validation

Asks

Can the bioreactor produce and release ACCD?

After validating individual components, we tested whether ACCD produced inside the microbial culture could be detected in the bioreactor output pathway.

The reactor is a perfusion loop: a PES hollow-fibre cartridge of 0.2 µm rating [7] with about 200 mL of lumen volume, a medium reservoir, and a peristaltic pump circulating between them. Cells live in the lumen, protein passes the membrane into the shell, and the shell side is what a farmer would eventually draw from. The engineering of it, and the hydraulics that go with it, are on Hardware.

Growth in the reactor

Version 1, on 6 June, put 300 mL of LB and 20 mL of overnight culture through the loop at 94 mL/min and stopped early when medium leaked at the pump. It ended at OD600 0.304 in the loop against 0.480 in a serum-bottle control, and a regression on the lumen series gives a mean OD of 0.142 with a slope of −0.0024 OD/h at p = 0.759. The culture in the fibre lumen did not grow at 22 °C while the control at the same temperature did, so the difference was the reactor and not the temperature.

Oxygen starvation, cumulative shear and adhesion to the fibre walls are all still live explanations and none has been eliminated. Version 2, on 14 June, added magnetic stirring at 200 rpm, a sampler between the reservoir and the pump, and ran at 37 °C. It reached plateau at OD600 0.8 to 0.9 in about two hours, against a shake flask reaching 0.6 to 0.8 in about four and a half hours. Under stirring at 37 °C the reactor beat the flask on both time and final density. The team recorded the lesson as: stirring is essential. It also explains the version 1 result.

The 102.4 hour run

Run 4, started on 1 September with B. subtilis 168 secreting ACC deaminase from a constitutive promoter on the full-length hollow fibre module, was logged in two files: 3.2 h up to OD600 0.24, then 10.2 h unrecorded, then 102.4 h without an interruption. That second file is the one place on this page where growth, crossflow and transmembrane pressure are recorded together as a continuous series.

OD600 rises from about 0.8 to 3.8 across the run. Crossflow is held near 371 mL/min, with brief dips where the dashboard logs an event, and transmembrane pressure stays inside a band of about ±0.1 bar throughout, which suggests the membrane was not fouling on the timescale of the run. Two cautions apply. The fit is a model fitted to one run and not a prediction tested against a second one, and the OD axis is the inline photometer's own reading, which section 5 establishes as linear in turbidity rather than tied to a cell count. What the run does close is whether the loop can be left alone: it can, for four days.

The October runs

Two runs in October put WB800 in the loop for the first time, both secreting ACC deaminase from a constitutive promoter. The first, 8 to 10 October, is not a growth curve: the inline reading climbs from 0.10 to 0.30 over nine hours and then falls away while the culture is still there, with a sixth of its windows carrying more than 20% bubbles and the reference beam moving by a factor of 4.8. The second, 14 to 16 October, halved the permeate flux to 36 LMH and gave the photometer an acceptance gate, and it ran 41.8 h without a restart.

OD600 rose from 0.14 to 2.71 by hour 37 of the second run. Above about 2.7 the sample beam fell to a tenth of the reference, so the last four hours are flagged and unused. Fitted between 1.5 and 6.5 h the specific growth rate is 0.286 ± 0.010 h−1, a doubling time of 145 min, and that is the figure registered on 1 October and scored in section 2.

The second run is also the first continuous transmembrane pressure log this page has across a whole run. The pressure the module needed beyond what clean water needs rose from 2.0 to 3.8 kPa over 42 h, slow enough that nothing had to be cleaned. Both runs are tabulated with the four earlier ones on Digital Twin. Neither was paired with strain 168 at the same settings, so the two strains have still not been compared against each other, and no sample was drawn from either for a cell count.

Protectant release

We operated the bioreactor using constitutive ACCD expression and collected samples from different points in the system.

Release has two halves: the protein has to leave the cell, and it has to cross the membrane. The first half has one piece of evidence and it is positive. On 2 August a Western blot of B. subtilis carrying the ACCD construct was run in three fractions side by side, total pellet, soluble pellet and supernatant. Bands sit between 35 and 40 kDa in the supernatant lanes and the two pellet lanes are effectively blank. That is the only fractionation evidence in the archive that the Csn secretion tag does what it was chosen to do, and the whole reactor design assumes it.

The expected molecular weight of ACCD was approximately 42 kDa. A faint band was observed near this expected size, supporting the presence of ACCD in the sampled bioreactor output pathway. This result provides preliminary evidence that ACCD produced inside the bioreactor could be released into the output pathway under the tested conditions. The weak band intensity suggests that the total ACCD yield was likely low, and indicates that protein production, membrane transport or recovery efficiency may not yet be optimised.

The engineering record reads the same blot the other way, as a band that is not clearly detectable above background. This page adopts neither reading. What would settle it is the computed mass of the fusion, a positive control lane and a re-read against both, and until that exists the honest position is that the membrane crossing is not demonstrated.

Safety and containment

In our trials, we ensured that the bacteria remained safely contained inside the bioreactor through shell-side plating. The hollow fibre membrane physically blocks bacteria from leaving the lumen side, since the pore size of the membrane, 0.2 µm, is much smaller than the cell size of B. subtilis.

To validate this, a simple test was conducted. While the bioreactor was running, we collected medium from the lumen and shell sides and plated both. After 24 h at 37 °C the samples were compared with each other. No detectable B. subtilis colonies were observed in the shell-side sample under the tested conditions. This provides preliminary evidence that the hollow-fibre membrane retained bacterial cells during the tested run.

Two platings are the entire containment record: this one, and 2 mL of shell-side liquid spread during the 6 June run, which also grew nothing. There is one plating per run and no replicate within a run, and for the June plate the incubation time and temperature were not recorded. The geometry argument is also weaker than it sounds. Our own pages give B. subtilis as a rod of 1 to 2 µm in the containment argument and as a 0.8 by 3 µm rod in the fouling model, and a 0.2 µm microfiltration membrane passes debris and vesicles in the 20 to 200 nm range along with the protein. The barrier works at the scale of a cell, and one plate with no colonies is consistent with retention at a detection limit nobody recorded.

What the chain does not close

What this page claims
  • Fourteen constructs assembled, each with a colony PCR band at a stated size, and the two complete systems junction-checked across all three module boundaries before sequencing.
  • An electroporation window measured rather than assumed: 2.1 kV for WB800 with the constitutive ACC deaminase cassette, 2.3 kV for the CcaSR version, and about 1.1 kV for the BoPep4 constructs.
  • A light plate that holds twelve tubes under matched red and green settings, grows B. subtilis in all of them, and gives a readable ACCD band at every time point from 2 to 16 h.
  • An inline photometer that ran 434 h unattended at ten-minute intervals and produced 2,671 valid rows.
  • A turbidity response linear against a commercial spectrophotometer on two different particulate standards: slope 1.018 at R² 0.995 on titania, and R² 0.992 with a residual spread a tenth of the reference instrument's on silica.
  • A sensor alarm that flagged a failing photometer 26.7 h before its channel became unusable.
  • A salt dose response in Arabidopsis at day 6, 74.95 to 1.36 mm of root across 0 to 150 mM NaCl, nine seedlings per group.
  • ACC deaminase raising relative chlorophyll content above the untreated control at every salt level, in one experiment of three replicates per group, with pre-treatment the steadiest timing and co-treatment the strongest without salt.
  • ACC deaminase in the supernatant lanes and absent from both pellet lanes, 2 August, which is one blot's worth of support for the secretion tag.
  • A reactor that holds four days of continuous perfusion, and a growth rate registered before the run it was tested on: 0.286 ± 0.010 h−1, inside the interval written down on 1 October.
  • Two shell-side platings that grew no colonies.
What it cannot claim yet
  • That light intensity controls output. Bands rise with time under green light and not in order of green intensity, and red 100% is as strong at 8 h. Closed by: a dose series quantified against a loading control, with a plasmid-free WB800 lane.
  • The 24-hour growth readings at green 2% and 12%. Both sit at the blank while green 100% grows normally, which no dose effect explains. Closed by: repeating the 24 h point.
  • A secreted yield. The anti-His ELISA is designed, the signal peptide correction factor of 2.66 is worked out, and no concentration is reported. Closed by: the standard curve and the interpolated supernatant concentrations.
  • Any ACC deaminase activity. The DNPH and indophenol blue assays are running and neither has a number here. Closed by: either assay on the purified fusion, with the ELISA beside it to give a specific activity.
  • That a photometer reading is a cell count. Both calibrations give linearity in turbidity, not biomass. Closed by: a paired OD600 series against plate counts.
  • That the ACC deaminase salt result repeats. A second run of the same design points the same way with scatter wide enough that nothing in it would test. Closed by: a third run at the replication of the first.
  • That LEA14 or BoPep4 does anything to a plant. Both are built and sequenced and neither has been applied to one. Closed by: expressing, purifying and applying them.
  • That protectant crossed the membrane. The reactor blot has two opposite readings on record. Closed by: computing the fusion mass, then re-reading the blot with a positive control lane.
  • A strain comparison. WB800 has been in the reactor twice and never beside strain 168 at the same settings, and the permeate flux changed between the two October runs. Closed by: running the comparison.
  • A containment figure. Two plates, no replicate within a run, no log reduction value, and no citation for the cell dimension the argument uses. Closed by: plating shell fluid at every sampling point of the next run.
  • An end-to-end run. No forecast has driven a light, no light has driven a graded expression change, and no reactor output has reached a plant. Closed by: the items above it, in order.

The single experiment that would move this project furthest is the graded induction curve in section 3, because the light switch is the one link the rest of the chain is built around and several items on the right wait on it.
