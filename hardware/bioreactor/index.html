ReLEAF Perfusion Bioreactor

Overview

A closed loop for continuous production and physical containment.

The ReLEAF perfusion bioreactor is a closed culture system designed to grow engineered Bacillus subtilis continuously while physically retaining the cells. Culture circulates through the lumen side of a hollow‑fiber membrane, and smaller soluble products can cross into a collection stream.

Part2

Purpose

2.1 Shift from Direct Release

The original design placed the engineered bacteria, a transgenic plant growth‑promoting rhizobacterium, directly into the soil. Once released, their spread, expression and dose are harder to control, which risks unintended GMO release.

Only the protectant they produce is needed, so we moved the cells into a contained bioreactor, from which only the product is designed to leave.

2.2 Choice of a Hollow‑Fiber Bioreactor

B. subtilis natively secretes proteins into the surrounding medium, so the product can be separated from the cells.

The hollow‑fiber membrane makes that separation the containment barrier as well: its pores are designed to pass smaller soluble products to the shell side while retaining whole cells on the lumen side.

2.3 Compact, On‑Site Operation

A compact bioreactor can sit near the farm, so protectants are produced locally and applied closer to when they are needed, with less reliance on centralized production and transport.

Part3

Hardware Highlights

3.1 Hollow‑Fiber Containment

The 0.2 µm hollow‑fiber membrane separates the culture from the product stream by size (6.1).

3.2 Perfusion Culture

The culture circulates continuously. Circulation is designed to admit fresh medium and remove product while the culture stays in the closed loop.

3.3 Integrated Monitoring

In‑line OD600 from our self‑built photometer is logged with flow rate and pressure, so a run is followed with no sample drawn.

3.4 Layout of the Loop

The system has two fluid paths, which meet only at the membrane wall.

Lumen side

Culture loop

Medium reservoir → peristaltic pump → in‑line photometer → fiber lumens → reservoir. The path holds the organism and is closed for the run; the only access is the dual Luer valve.

  • Reservoir1 L medium; 300 mL working volume in a 500 mL vessel, first three runs
  • Inoculum20 mL multi‑overnight B. subtilis culture
  • Flow rate94 mL/min
  • Stirring200 rpm magnetic
Shell side

Protectant harvest

The space around the fibers. It fills by diffusion across the fiber wall, is drawn off through the shell ports, and carries protectant to delivery.

  • MembranePES (polyethersulfone) hollow fiber
  • Pore rating0.2 µm
  • Lumen volume~200 mL
  • Area150 cm², eight fibers
  • Deliverysoil or hydroponic line

3.5 Software

The bioreactor is run from one controller interface, the Digital Twin, which logs every sensor reading on the loop and commands the valves. Its models have so far run on recorded data after each run, and a person approves every decision.

      Prototype 3 – Ongoing

      The in‑line photometer in the loop

      1. DDesignA full growth curve, no samples drawn
      2. BBuildPhotometer in the culture loop

      The in‑line photometer monitors optical density in the loop: culture passes through a flow cuvette with a 2 mm path, where OD600 is read without drawing a sample. Its own cycles are on the photometer page.

      Design

      We wanted a full growth curve from one uninterrupted run. Every manual OD600 reading removes culture and risks contamination; in‑line measurement is designed to remove that risk.

      Build

      The photometer runs on an Arduino UNO Q serving a web dashboard on the local network, so a long run can be followed without opening the system (photometer page, 4.5).

      Part6

      Containment & Safety

      In agricultural fields, engineered organisms must not enter the wild. The bioreactor is designed to keep engineered B. subtilis inside while protectants leave through the membrane.

      6.1 Physical Containment by Size

      The membrane's 0.2 µm pore is smaller than the reported diameter of B. subtilis cells (0.25–1.0 µm), so whole cells are intended to stay on the lumen side while smaller soluble products cross to the shell side.

      To test whether whole cells crossed, medium was collected from the lumen and shell sides after 24 h at 37 °C.

      On LB agar the lumen‑side sample grew colonies and the shell‑side sample grew none, so culturable B. subtilis remained on the lumen side under the conditions tested. The second plating on record, 2 mL of shell‑side medium (6 June 2026), also grew none (photometer page, 3.1).

      6.2 Leak Prevention

      A closed bioreactor can still leak. Two monitoring mechanisms are fitted: one for liquid escaping the system, one for faults within the membrane.

      External Leaks: Droplet Sensor

      A droplet sensor beneath the setup detects liquid escaping from the tubing or connections; the software is designed to raise an alert.

      Internal Faults: Transmembrane Pressure

      The system is designed to monitor TMP, where a sudden or abnormal change can indicate a leak, breach or blockage before it is visible.

      Neither system has been tested with a deliberately induced leak or breach (7.1).

      Part7

      Limitations & Future Plans

      7.1 Current Limitations

      1. Protectant Transfer Rate

        No shell‑side concentration or transfer rate has been measured; the blot shows only that it crosses.

      2. Long‑Term Membrane Integrity

        The two platings test the barrier at one time point. One fouling trend is now on record, 42 h on 14 to 16 October, and no induced leak or breach test is (6.2).

      7.2 Future Plan

      We plan a sealed cartridge‑style replacement system for the culture and medium, so that a contained module can be replaced during servicing without opening the bioreactor. Planned testing covers longer operation, membrane fouling, cartridge lifetime, protectant transfer rate and field‑relevant conditions.
