ReLEAF Perfusion Bioreactor

Overview

A closed loop for continuous production and physical containment.

The ReLEAF perfusion bioreactor is a closed culture system designed to continuously grow engineered B. subtilis while physically retaining the cells inside the system. Culture circulates through the lumen side of a hollow‑fiber membrane, while smaller soluble products can cross the membrane into a separate collection stream.

Part2

Purpose

2.1 From TgPGPR to a Bioreactor

Climate change makes crop stress more frequent and harder to predict, and we want to give crops a genetically modified bioprotectant to help them cope. Our first design was a transgenic plant‑growth‑promoting rhizobacterium (TgPGPR) that would live on the roots and make the protectant there.

We chose a bioreactor instead, for two reasons. The main one is biosafety. Once a transgenic organism is released, its expression, dose and spread can no longer be controlled. The other is survival. Lab strains of B. subtilis lack motility, can’t form biofilm and have metabolic deficiencies, so they would struggle in the field anyway.

We only need the protectant in the field, so we keep the organism in a machine and send out only its product. That turns a release problem into a containment problem.

2.2 Why B. subtilis and Perfusion Hollow Fiber

We chose Bacillus subtilis because the FDA classifies nonpathogenic, nontoxigenic strains as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) under good manufacturing practices. It is easy to engineer, and it secretes recombinant protein into the medium, so the cells never have to be broken open to collect the protectant.

We used two strains. B. subtilis 168 ran the growth tests and the first integrated run, where it barely made detectable enzyme, probably because its own secreted proteases broke the product down. Every later run used B. subtilis WB800, which lacks those proteases (4.2, prototype 1). Both are nonpathogenic lab strains, and the hardware is the same for either.

Secretion suits a hollow fiber. The culture runs through the fiber lumens and medium flows past the outside; the protectant crosses the membrane and the bacteria cannot. The cells stay intact, so the culture can keep producing for days. The longest logged run is 116 hours.

2.3 Against Shelf Production

Peptides degrade in storage and often need a cold chain, so a bottle can lose potency before it is used. Made on site, the protectant is fresh and its dose is known, and nobody pays to chill and ship a product that is mostly water. That matters most to small growers.

Part3

Hardware Highlights

3.1 Hollow‑Fiber Perfusion

Fresh medium flows in and waste flows out while the culture keeps running, so the cell density stays high and the protectant comes off as a steady stream.

3.2 Containment and Product Release

The membrane’s pores let the protectant through and hold the cells back, so the part that contains the organism is also the part that delivers the product. Everything that reaches the plant has crossed it.

3.3 Integrated Monitoring

An in‑line photometer reads OD600 through a flow cell without drawing a sample, a probe reads pH in the vessel, and the transmembrane pressure is tracked so fouling shows up as a trend before it becomes a failure. Everything reports to a web dashboard on the local network, so we can watch a run without touching the rig.

4.1 Hollow Fiber Membrane

This track mapped each pump output (%) to the flow rate Q it gives and the pressure that flow builds across the membrane. With that ΔP–Q relationship the bioreactor can be monitored and controlled from the pump output alone. Two manual rounds showed the result depended on who measured it. The instrumented third round separated the membrane from the plumbing and set an operating point of 263 mL/min at a TMP of 0.30 bar. A magnetic pump, tried for long runs, could not move the medium, so the peristaltic pump stayed.

Prototype 1 – Manual PQ

Round 1: three pressure sensors, flow measured by hand

Design

The goal was a ΔP–Q relationship, so that the pressure across the membrane is known from the pump output without measuring flow and pressure by hand each time. Round 1 put a pressure sensor on each of the module’s three ports: feed (medium entering the lumen side), retentate (medium leaving it) and permeate (the shell side, reached through the membrane).

The transmembrane pressure (TMP) is the mean lumen pressure minus the shell pressure: TMP = (Pfeed + Pretentate) / 2 − Ppermeate. To tie pressure to flow, the pump stepped from 10% to 100% output in 10% steps, one minute at each.

Build

The three sensors were BP8G‑AGB ceramic pressure transmitters (Jingfeng), 0–1 MPa gauge, with a three‑wire 0–5 V output (10.1). The loop ran on water, as every calibration run did until the magnetic‑pump test (prototype 4). Q was measured by hand, here and in round 2: at each output the pump ran for one minute, drawing from a container of water into an empty measuring cylinder, and Q is the volume collected.

Test

Outputs from 10% to 60% were measured three times, 70% to 100% once. The average Q rose from 100.3 mL/min at 10% to 1535 mL/min at 100%. Up to 50%, each 10% step added about 210–225 mL/min, close to a straight line. Above that the steps shrank, to about 55 mL/min from 80% to 90%, so the pump may be near its maximum delivery. Repeated trials agree within 12 mL/min.

Prototype 3 – Automatic PQ

Every reading by instrument; the plumbing subtracted

  1. DDesignInstruments only; membrane vs. plumbing
  2. BBuildTransducers to 3.3 V; Hall‑effect flowmeter
  3. TTestΔP = aQ + bQ², R² 0.995
  4. LLearn263 mL/min at a TMP of 0.30 bar
Design

Round 3 dropped manual measurement. Pressure came from the transducers and Q from an in‑line flowmeter. That removes reading error, the error of guessing Q from the pump setting, and the slips of manual testing.

Part of the pressure drop comes from the plumbing (valves, fittings and the flow cuvette), so we measured the loop twice at the same pump outputs, without the module and with it, changing nothing else. The difference is the module’s own hydraulic contribution.

We fitted the pressure drop with a through‑origin quadratic model:

ΔP=aQ + bQ2

The linear term is laminar friction along the fibers and tubing; the quadratic term is minor losses at the valves, fittings and flow cuvette. The fit passes through the origin because there is no pressure drop at zero flow.

Build

Pressure transducers. Their output, up to 5 V, is too high for the microcontroller’s analog input, so a two‑resistor divider brings it to 0–3.3 V (the BP8G‑AGB transmitter, round 1).

Flowmeter. In a Hall‑effect flowmeter, a magnet on a rotor spins as water flows through, a sensor beside it picks up each pass, and the pulse frequency gives Q.

Two configurations. In the Background configuration a plain silicone tubing jumper replaced the membrane module, with every valve, fitting and the flow cuvette in place. In the HFM configuration the module was fitted and nothing else changed.

Test

Both configurations ran on 16 August 2026 at pump outputs from 10% to 24% in 2% steps, one minute each. Both data sets fit the model with R2 0.995, and the residuals show no systematic trend.

The two configurations did not land on exactly the same flow rates, so they could not be subtracted point by point. Instead, the Background fit predicted the plumbing’s pressure drop at each HFM flow rate, and that was subtracted. The plumbing is 46% of the total at 120 mL/min and 55% at 450 mL/min. Without the subtraction, the membrane’s own resistance would have been overstated by about a factor of two.

We then compared the net pressure drop with the Hagen–Poiseuille prediction for laminar flow through the fibers. From 113 to 263 mL/min it is 0.90–0.96 times the prediction; above 300 mL/min, 1.07–1.26 times. We put the excess down to minor losses where the flow squeezes from one wide channel into eleven narrow fibers, and to entrance effects before the velocity profile is fully developed.

Two checks on the geometry agree. The net pressure drop gives an effective count of about 11 open fibers, the module’s nominal count, so no fiber is blocked. And the shear rate calculated from Q agrees within 2.8% with the one back‑calculated from the wall shear stress.

Learn

Round 3 gave the ΔP–Q relationship, ΔP = aQ + bQ2: laminar friction plus minor losses. Two lessons carry forward. The plumbing must be subtracted before the membrane is judged, or its resistance reads about double. And in the operating range the membrane behaves as theory predicts, with all eleven fibers open.

These set the operating window. We recommend 263 mL/min, a shear rate of about 4060 s−1, at a TMP of 0.30 bar. That is mid‑range in the manufacturer’s shear‑rate window of 2000–6000 s−1, keeps the TMP low enough to avoid irreversible compaction of the membrane, and gives an axial TMP non‑uniformity of 28%, still acceptable before filtration turns uneven along the fiber. At 313 mL/min the non‑uniformity reaches 46% (not recommended); at 450 mL/min, 67% (prohibited).

Prototype 4 – Magnetic Pump Test

A wear‑free pump for long runs

  1. DDesignNo wear, for long continuous runs
  2. BBuildIn the loop; a gravity‑fed tank planned
  3. TTestCulture medium: not enough pressure
  4. LLearnKeep the peristaltic pump
Design

Our runs last days, and the peristaltic pump wears mechanically, so its limited life was a liability. A magnetic pump has no such wear and should last much longer in continuous use, so we tested one as a replacement.

Build

The magnetic pump replaced the peristaltic pump in the loop. It is weaker and cannot lift liquid out of the serum bottle, so we planned a clear PC‑water‑cooling reservoir, with ports at the top and bottom to gravity‑feed it. We dropped that plan on 16 August, when the pump proved too weak for the bioreactor (notebook, week 24).

Test

The calibration runs used water; this test used the culture medium, LB. The magnetic pump could not build enough pressure to push the medium through the system and never held a working flow, so we have no data set from it.

Learn

The peristaltic pump stays. It wears out sooner, but it reliably moves the culture medium. It is the pump in the integrated bioreactor (4.2).

4.2 Integrated Bioreactor

Here the membrane track and the long‑term run meet. Prototype 1 is the first integrated run, 116 hours with B. subtilis 168. Prototype 2 (WB800) adds the pinch valve, the pH probe and one mainboard, and finds ACC deaminase on the shell side at the lumen’s level. Prototype 3 holds the pH with KOH and sterilizes the loop in place. In all three ACC deaminase is under a constitutive promoter, so light control is out of the test and the loop itself is what is examined. What the reviewers changed between runs is in part 6.

Prototype 1 of 3

Prototype 1 – Pump Control

First integrated run, with constitutive ACC deaminase production

  1. DDesignWhen it plateaus; sensors under load
  2. BBuildEvery part in one loop, strain 168
  3. TTest116 h: plateau at 40 h, then regrowth

Design

We had tested each part alone first. The in‑line photometer read optical density reliably for about 240 hours of a 434 h logged run (photometer page, 5.1), the bacteria made ACC deaminase, and the pressure transducers and flowmeter worked on water (4.1, prototype 3). This cycle put every component into one system for the first time. It asked when B. subtilis reaches its plateau in the loop, whether the system supports ACC deaminase production, and how the sensors work and hold up together under operating conditions.

ACC deaminase is under a constitutive promoter, so the strain expresses continuously and light control is not a variable. To speed growth and shorten the run, it ran at 37 °C with 500 mL of medium.

Build

Structure. The loop has a lumen side and a shell side. On the lumen side the peristaltic pump is the main pump, a magnetic stirrer sits under the reservoir, and the culture passes three pressure transducers, one flowmeter and the in‑line photometer, with a Luer valve for samples. In this run the shell side was a closed loop of its own, also with a Luer sampling valve.

Electrical circuit. Transistors and capacitors were added so that the sensors read properly. The photometer’s circuit is on its own page (photometer page, 9.2).

Gene circuit. B. subtilis 168 carrying pSTK‑EXP1‑Pveg‑Csn:AcdS, an expression vector that makes ACC deaminase constitutively. Erythromycin at 5 µg/mL kept the plasmid in the cells and guarded against contamination.

Test

The run started on 1 September 2026 and lasted 116 hours. Partway through, the computer running the microcontroller installed an update and shut down, and since the data lived in the web interface, about 10 hours were lost (grey in the plots). The culture reached its plateau at about 40 hours; at around 47 hours an unexpected second growth began and lasted to the end of the run.

Not all the hardware held up. The pressure reading was unstable throughout, did not respond when the loop was handled, and never returned to its baseline, even with the main pump stopped.

A faint band consistent with ACC deaminase (~42 kDa) appeared in shell‑side medium (the western blot below).

Learn

In the loop the bacteria reach plateau after roughly 40 hours, and that is when they start to make detectable ACC deaminase. A faint ~42 kDa band in shell‑side medium suggests that ACC deaminase reaches the shell side, but expression is weak.

A second growth after the plateau is not what a batch curve does. Our working explanation is that the erythromycin degraded, the cells began to lose the plasmid, and with less metabolic burden they grew again. Plasmid stability was what Prof. Chang asked about in September (6.1). Strain 168 also barely made detectable ACC deaminase, probably because its secreted proteases degrade the product, so the next prototype uses B. subtilis WB800, which lacks them. The unstable pressure readings still need explaining; the electronics review that followed is in 6.2.

Prototype 2 – Pinch Valve & pH

WB800, a pinch valve, a pH probe and one mainboard

  1. DDesignNew strain, steady transducers, valve and pH
  2. BBuildMainboard; 1 L; a product reservoir

Design

Prototype 2 answers what prototype 1 and the Owl Lab review found. First, the host strain changed from B. subtilis 168 to WB800, which lacks secreted proteases (prototype 1, Learn).

Second, the hardware. The pressure transducers had read unstably, so they now run on 12 V DC instead of 5 V, with their ground on the DC–DC module rather than on the Arduino UNO Q. The old power supply could not deliver the current the motors and pumps need (6.2); the mainboard Owl Lab proposed (6.2) supplies both.

Third, we added a pH probe and a servo‑driven pinch valve. The valve raises the transmembrane pressure to push product from the lumen side to the shell side, the gap Prof. Chang found in June (6.1). Both plug into the mainboard as modules. The medium went from 500 mL to 1 L, still at 37 °C.

Build

Structure. The shell side now drains to a reservoir that collects the product. The lumen side carries three pressure transducers, one flowmeter, the in‑line photometer, the pinch valve and the pH probe.

Fixed parts. The pinch valve, a printed body holding an STS3215 12 V servo motor (10.2), clamps the return line about 50 mm (2 in) from the Out‑Lumen connection. Electrical circuit. The electronics are built around the mainboard, in effect an extension of the UNO Q with room for the design to change. It replaced the two‑board design after Owl Lab’s two reviews (6.2). Gene circuit. B. subtilis WB800 carrying pSTK‑EXP1‑SamyQ‑AcdS‑v2t1, with erythromycin kept at 5 µg/mL. The run started on 8 October 2026, with permeate drawn at 24 mL/min (notebook, week 32).

Design

Prototype 2 left the medium too acidic. A pH of 6.5 or lower badly harms B. subtilis growth and cell division and the stability of the protectant enzyme, so we kept the pH probe on the lumen side and added KOH whenever the pH dropped.

We also worked out how to sterilize the assembled loop without an autoclave, by chemical clean‑in‑place (CIP). We pump in 900 mL of distilled water, then 100 mL of 35% hydrogen peroxide; they mix in the lumen reservoir and circulate for 15 minutes, and a starch–iodide test paper checks for peroxide. The solution drains through a tube, which is reattached to close the loop. Then 1 L of distilled water circulates for 30 minutes, with a fresh test paper after each wash, until no peroxide is left. It usually takes five 1 L washes.

Build

As prototype 2, on the same strain (B. subtilis WB800), with one change. We blamed the acidity on too little oxygen, so the culture volume went from 1 L to 500 mL. The same reservoir has the same surface for oxygen to diffuse through but fewer bacteria to supply, so diffusion alone can give them enough. The notebook adds that permeate was halved to 12 mL/min and the photometer’s acceptance gate was running (notebook, week 33).

Test

The run started on 14 October 2026. Since prototype 2 had shown both sides read the same (its Learn), protectant was drawn from the shell side only, twice, at hours 1.10 and 18.38. At the 38‑hour mark, unnoticed at the time, a Luer valve at the start of the lumen loop broke in half and medium leaked into the incubator. Up to then the pH and OD curves were reliable: clear readings of growth, and of how the pH responded to growth and to KOH. The OD rose from 0.14 to 2.71 by 37 h, a fitted growth rate of 0.286 h−1 (notebook, week 33). That is inside the range forecast before the run. On 1 October a growth rate was registered of 0.2986 h−1, passing if the fitted rate fell between 0.2705 and 0.3300 h−1 (notebook, week 31). After 38 hours the pump’s shaking had knocked the photometer’s light path out of line, so later OD data cannot be used.

Learn

We traced the broken valve to the clean‑in‑place step. We had assumed the 35% hydrogen peroxide would be diluted once it mixed in the reservoir, but it passes through the rest of the loop before it gets there.

The pH probe showed how long the medium takes to rebalance after KOH is added by hand while the bacteria keep growing, and how far the pH shifts in the real bioreactor. Both will help us build automatic pH correction in the next cycle.

6.1 Prof. Chang Jo‑Shu

Prof. Chang (張嘉修), Chemical Engineering, National Cheng Kung University, is an expert in biomanufacturing. Both meetings were in person.

Prof. Chang 18 June first meetingNo force drove the protectant across the membrane; also its size and how to see fouling

Is a hollow‑fiber bioreactor feasible, and what breaks when it is scaled up? Held before the bioreactor was assembled, it set the agenda that integrated prototypes 1 and 2 were built against. Source: Notebook, week 16; team Human Practices page.

Transmembrane pressureThe largest gap he found was that nothing in the design pushed the protectant from the lumen side, where it is made, to the shell side, where it leaves. A higher flow rate would only recirculate the fluid, not build outward pressure. He recommended a valve after the membrane to build pressure, and narrower tubing to concentrate it.

MembraneOur membrane might be too big, which raises the risk of cells sticking to or clogging the fibers, so he recommended a smaller one. Flow rate and transmembrane pressure must be monitored, since they show fouling (caking). And we should be ready to tell judges why we chose hollow fiber, which we could not yet answer.

6.2 Teacher He, Owl Lab

Owl Lab is a robotics and electronics teaching lab. With the bioreactor almost built, we asked Teacher He to find its weak points before they caused problems in an experiment.

Teacher He, Owl Lab 20 September first meetingThree faults in servo driver and torque, power supply and board layout, all fixed before prototype 2

A review of the servo control, power supply and control‑board layout of the bioreactor built for integrated prototype 1. Every fault it found was corrected before prototype 2 ran. Source: Team Human Practices page.

Servo controlWe had planned a PCA9685 driver board for the servos that turn the Luer valve and the pinch valve. In his experience it is unreliable. Told to turn 90°, it may stop at 85°, and a valve that misses its position can cause a sudden pressure spike that breaks the tubing and leaks. The servos also lacked the torque to turn the valves. He suggested the STS3215 servo, driven over UART, which is accurate and strong enough.

Power supplyThe plan powered everything through an AMS1117 module, 12 V down to 5 V for most sensors and 3.3 V for the UNO Q. With valve and pump control added, they need more current than it can give.

Control boardThe Arduino UNO Q uses 3.3 V logic, not the usual 5 V, so the pumps, motors and sensors need their voltage stepped. After reading the code, he confirmed the UNO Q has to stay the main board and suggested two boards in series: the UNO Q reads the sensors and serves the interface, and an Arduino Mega 2560 drives the pumps and valves. That keeps high‑current parts off the main controller and voltage‑sensitive sensors, such as the pressure transmitters, away from the pump and valve current.

ChangedWe dropped the PCA9685 for STS3215 servos over UART, which drive the pinch valve added in prototype 2. The mainboard’s own 12 V to 5 V and 3.3 V converter replaced the AMS1117 and gives the pumps and valves the current they need. Two days later the mainboard, built for the same isolation, replaced the two‑board plan (next entry).

Teacher He, Owl Lab 22 September a better planOne mainboard in place of two boards and a tangle of wires

Owl Lab got back in touch two days later, which led to the largest single hardware change in the project, the mainboard that prototype 2 is built around. Source: Team Human Practices page.

MainboardTwo boards would have meant a bulky package with wires everywhere. Instead, one mainboard carries everything: a converter from 12 V to 5 V and 3.3 V, and every UNO Q pin brought onto it, so the board in effect becomes the UNO Q. A jumper wire sets each pin to any of the three voltages, so any pin can be used for anything and the old tangle becomes swappable modules.

ChangedWe dropped the two‑board design and built the mainboard (4.2, prototype 2). It supplies the current the AMS1117 could not, and it keeps the 3.3 V UNO Q apart from the pumps and valves, the aim of the two‑board plan. With the transducers moved to 12 V and their ground on the DC–DC module, it also ended prototype 1’s unstable pressure readings, a problem we had not asked about. By prototype 2’s Test the transducers read stably. And it made the sensors modular, so the pH probe and the pinch‑valve servo went in without rewiring, with power delivery and electrical isolation reworked (notebook, week 30; 7.1.3).

Two points changed the photometer instead: Prof. Chang’s advice to dilute samples above an OD600 of 1 (photometer page, 5.2), and Owl Lab’s mainboard, which made the photometer a plug‑in module (photometer page, 9.2). Both are in the photometer page’s Adjusting to Feedback.

Part8

Containment & Safety

Keeping genetically modified organisms out of the environment is the first priority of the ReLEAF bioreactor.

To check that the membrane holds the bacteria in, we took medium from the lumen and shell sides of the running bioreactor, plated both, and compared them after 24 h at 37 °C.

Colonies grew from the lumen side and none from the shell side, so in this test the membrane worked as a physical barrier and kept B. subtilis out of the medium that leaves the machine. A second plating, of shell‑side medium from an earlier growth run, also grew no colonies (photometer page, 2.1).

A leak outside the membrane. In integrated prototype 3 a Luer valve at the start of the lumen loop broke in half at the 38‑hour mark, and culture medium leaked into the incubator (4.2, prototype 3). The membrane did not fail. We traced the break to the clean‑in‑place step, whose 35% hydrogen peroxide reaches the valves before it is diluted in the reservoir (its Learn). The planned droplet sensor under the loop is meant to catch leaks like this (9.2.3).

Handling the peroxide. 35% hydrogen peroxide is a strong oxidizer that harms skin and eyes. As standard lab practice, it is handled with gloves and eye protection, in a well‑ventilated space.

9.2 Future Plan

9.2.1 Containment in Layers

Under normal operation the bacteria are already contained. The membrane’s 0.2 µm pores are smaller than any B. subtilis cell, and shell‑side medium grew no colonies in two platings (8). Containment is the core promise of our project, so for the commercial bioreactor we plan two more layers of protection around the membrane, each catching a failure the other cannot see. The first uses hardware the bioreactor already has; the second adds one sensor.

Pressure comes first because it needs no new parts and acts before anything escapes. The droplet sensor catches fluid where it should never be, which pressure cannot.

9.2.2 Layer 1: Pressure Alarm and Pump Stop

The lab bioreactor already reads pressure at three membrane ports (feed, retentate and permeate) and shows the transmembrane pressure (TMP) on the dashboard (4.2); the commercial design reads all four (In‑Lumen, Out‑Lumen, In‑Shell, Out‑Shell). Today these numbers are only displayed. We will program the controller to act on them: when the lumen pressure or the TMP passes a set threshold, it sounds an alarm, stops the culture pump and opens the pinch valve.

Too much pressure is the condition most likely to force cells through a damaged fiber or burst a fitting, so this layer acts before a breach happens. A sudden drop can mean the membrane has already given way, often before any liquid shows, so the run stops and a shell‑side sample is plated to check (8).

The plan

Sensors
Four pressure transducers, one at each membrane port (the lab rig has three today)
Trigger
Lumen pressure or TMP above a threshold for several readings in a row, or a sudden drop
Action
An alarm on the screen and the phone page; the culture pump stops; the pinch valve opens to relieve the pressure
Reset
By hand, once the cause is found; no automatic restart
New hardware
None, a firmware change only

9.2.3 Layer 2: Droplet Sensor under the Loop

The first layer watches fluid where it should be; the second watches where it should not. A droplet sensor under the setup will detect liquid escaping from the tubing or connections, then sound an alarm and stop the pumps, so a slow leak from a loose fitting cannot run on unnoticed.

The plan

Sensor
A droplet (moisture) sensor at the low point of a drip tray under the loop
Trigger
Any moisture on the sensor
Action
An alarm; the pumps stop
New hardware
One sensor and a drip tray

9.2.4 How the Layers Work Together

Each layer answers a different failure, and both end the same way. The alarm sounds, the pumps stop, and nothing restarts until a person has found the cause.

9.2.5 Sealed Refill Cartridges

Over a season a farmer has to top up both the medium and the culture, and neither is safe to do by hand. So the commercial bioreactor takes two sealed, single‑use cartridges: ready‑to‑use sterile medium, and a B. subtilis spore stock that ships at room temperature. Both drop into one keyed dock: the medium cartridge has a D‑shaped collar and the spore cartridge a round one, so neither fits the other’s port.

A valve in each cartridge’s base stays shut until the cartridge is seated and closes as it lifts out, and the medium bag collapses as it drains, so no vent is needed. Medium goes in first, then the spores. The cartridges are the only part a farmer handles; spent ones come back to us sealed.

The next steps toward a prototype are a reliable seal (the printed parts were not watertight, so it needs moulded parts or O‑ring glands), sterility over repeated changeovers, and confirmation that the strain sporulates and germinates reliably from stored spores.

9.2.6 Water Supply and Irrigation Line

The commercial bioreactor will connect to the farm’s water supply. The protectant stream from the shell side is mixed with that water and pumped into the irrigation line, so it reaches the roots with the irrigation water and the farmer adds nothing by hand.

9.2.7 Scaling

We would grow capacity by scaling out, adding copies of the module already characterised, the way a vertical farm adds identical units. From one module to eight the membrane never limits; the pumps, power and medium supply around it do, and a duty‑cycled pump would ease that.
