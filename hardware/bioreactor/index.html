ReLeaf Perfusion
Bioreactor

The production stage of RELEAF. Engineered B. subtilis grows in a stirred reservoir and is perfused continuously through a PES hollow‑fiber membrane. Protectant diffuses across the fiber wall into the shell side and is drawn off to the field; the bacteria remain on the lumen side for the life of the run.

This document reports the device to the standard of strict reproducibility: a team in another country, with no contact with us, should be able to rebuild it from what follows. Where a specific number, part, or statement must be supplied before publication, it is marked explicitly rather than invented.

Part1

Overview

1.1 What the Device Has to Do

RELEAF asks a bacterium to manufacture a plant protectant on demand, in the field, next to the crop that needs it. That creates a problem the biology cannot solve on its own: the protectant has to reach the soil, and the engineered organism must not. Every part of this device exists to hold those two requirements apart.

0cells

Colony formation on LB agar plated from the shell‑side medium, cycle 1. The separation the design depends on was tested directly rather than assumed — see .

The answer is a perfusion loop built around a hollow‑fiber membrane. Culture is pumped from a stirred medium reservoir through the fiber lumens and back to the reservoir, continuously. The fibers are permeable to the secreted protectant but not to B. subtilis, so the shell space surrounding the fibers fills with cell‑free product. That shell‑side fluid is what leaves the machine.

Because the culture never has to be opened to be measured, optical density is read in‑line by the V4 photometer sitting in the return leg. The result is a closed system that reports its own state: no sampling, no lid, no interruption.

1.2 The Two Circuits

Everything in the record below refers to one of two fluid paths. They meet only at the membrane wall, and they never mix.

Lumen side

Culture loop

Medium reservoir → peristaltic pump → fiber lumens → in‑line photometer → back to the reservoir. Contains the organism. Closed for the duration of the run.

  • Reservoir 500 mL vessel, 300 mL working volume
  • Inoculum 20 mL overnight B. subtilis culture
  • Flow rate 94 mL/min
  • Stirring 200 rpm magnetic (added cycle 2)
Shell side

Protectant harvest

The space around the fibers. Fills by diffusion across the fiber wall, is drawn off through the shell ports, and carries protectant to the delivery stage. Cell‑free by construction, verified by plating.

  • Lumen volume ~200 mL
  • Membrane PES hollow fiber
  • Pore rating
  • Delivery to soil or hydroponic line
Needs

The membrane's molecular weight cut‑off, and the protectant's size, to state the separation margin quantitatively rather than by observation.

Part2

Architecture

2.1 System Diagram

The full setup as designed, including the sensing and delivery stages that sit either side of the reactor itself.

Reading the schematic clockwise from the culture vessel: the stirred reservoir is induced by a green light signal driven by the AIoT stress predictor. The peristaltic pump drives culture through the sampler and the in‑line spectrophotometer, past a pressure sensor, and into the top of the membrane column. Culture returns through a pinch valve. On the harvest side, a second pump moves shell‑side fluid past OD and pH sensors, through a pH regulator, and out to the delivery stages.

2.2 Flow Path and Control

Perfusion

Peristaltic pump, 94 mL/min, single continuous loop. Non‑contact pumping keeps the fluid path sterile.

Mixing

Magnetic stir bar at 200 rpm in the reservoir. Added in cycle 2 after sedimentation was diagnosed.

Induction

Green light signal to the culture vessel, triggered by the AIoT stress prediction upstream.

Optical density

In‑line, dual‑sensor ratiometric, no sampling. See the photometer record.

Pressure

Sensor on the feed leg to catch fiber fouling and over‑pressure before the membrane is damaged. confirm

pH

Monitored and regulated on the harvest side before delivery. set point.

Why perfusion rather than batch

A batch reactor must be opened to be harvested, and every opening is a containment event and a contamination risk. Perfusion moves product out continuously through a barrier that the organism cannot cross, so the culture vessel stays closed from inoculation to shutdown.

Part4

Iteration History

Two complete design–build–test–learn cycles, each reported with what failed and what that failure changed. A third is in progress.

  1. 01

    Continuous flow, proof of containment

    Cycle 1 · 06‑06

    The first build established that a continuous‑flow loop around a PES hollow‑fiber membrane works at all: membrane, medium reservoir, peristaltic pump and luer valve, nothing else. A 500 mL reservoir held 300 mL of LB inoculated with 20 mL of overnight culture, perfused through a fiber cartridge of roughly 200 mL lumen volume.

    The pump hit its 94 mL/min target, but the run ended early on medium leakage at the pump head. Final readings were OD600 0.304 in‑tube against 0.48 in the reservoir.

    Learned

    The gap between tube and reservoir OD pointed at sedimentation: with no active mixing, cells settled and nutrients stratified, so the reservoir was not representative of the loop. Two hardware changes followed — biocompatible BPT tubing for a reliable seal at the pump, and an active magnetic stirrer to keep the reservoir homogeneous. The sampling port also moved to the reservoir outlet to make the reading representative.

  2. 02

    Active mixing, and a full growth curve

    Cycle 2 · 06‑14

    A 200 rpm magnetic stirrer was added to the reservoir, and the sampling point moved to sit close to it — the reservoir holds far more volume than the fiber cartridge, so a reading there better represents the whole module. The run was held at 37 °C rather than room temperature, with a shake flask as control.

    The pump held 94 mL/min for the entire run and nothing leaked. The bioreactor reached plateau at OD600 0.8–0.9 after 2 hours; the flask control reached 0.6–0.8 after 4.5 hours.

    Learned

    Continuous stirring was the missing variable: it prevented sedimentation and kept the system efficient, and the complete growth curve confirmed it. The reactor also outpaced the flask — higher plateau, less than half the time. Next: run at 22 °C to characterise field conditions, and upgrade to higher‑durability BPT tubing to stop the tube degradation seen in earlier trials.

  3. 03

    Field temperature and durability

    Cycle 3 · in progress

    Planned: characterise growth kinetics at 22 °C, the temperature the device will actually see in the field, and qualify BPT tubing over a long run to establish service life.

    Needs

    Results of the 22 °C run, and a tubing service‑life figure.

The cycle 2 comparison is the central result so far. Under identical medium and temperature, the perfusion reactor reached a higher plateau density in less than half the time of a standard shake flask. The likely mechanism is the combination of continuous mixing and constant perfusion through the fiber bundle: neither nutrients nor cells stratify, and the effective surface for gas exchange is far larger than a flask's.

Needs

5.2 Containment

Medium drawn from the shell side and plated on LB agar produced no colonies (). This is the single most important result on the page: it is direct evidence that the fluid the device delivers to a field carries no engineered organism, and that the barrier is the hardware itself rather than an operating procedure.

Confirm

Number of biological replicates, plated volume per replicate, and incubation time and temperature — a containment claim needs its n.

5.3 Open Parameters

Membrane fouling

Highest priority

Protein and cell debris accumulating on the fiber wall will reduce flux and eventually protectant transfer. No fouling measurement has been made over a long run, and it is the most likely limit on service life.

Needs

Part6

Role & Outlook

6.1 Role in the Project

RELEAF runs predict → produce → protect. The AIoT stage predicts plant stress and issues a light signal; this bioreactor is the produce stage that receives it. The DiOPAL array established the light conditions that drive protectant expression, and the V4 photometer is embedded in this loop as its eye. What leaves here is cell‑free protectant, bound for soil or a hydroponic line.

UpstreamDiOPAL defines the light that induces expression This deviceBioreactor grows the culture, separates the product EmbeddedV4 Photometer reads OD600 in‑line, without sampling

6.2 What Would Make This Deployable

  • A multi-day run with the photometer logging continuously, to establish that the loop is stable over the timescale a field deployment needs.
  • A fouling curve from transmembrane pressure, converting "it works for hours" into a stated service interval.
  • 22 °C kinetics, so the throughput claim reflects field conditions rather than an incubator.
  • Replicated containment plating with a stated n, so the safety claim carries a number.
  • A costed BOM against a named commercial perfusion system.
