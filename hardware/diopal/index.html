LPA: Light Plate Apparatus

The LPA is a 24‑culture LED array designed to test how wavelength and light intensity affect our CcaS/CcaR optogenetic system. Red and green LEDs are arranged across six light conditions, with four replicate cultures per condition.

Through three design–build–test–learn cycles, we improved LED consistency, reduced light leakage between wells, and moved intensity control into software using PWM. The final enclosed design provides controlled and repeatable illumination for optogenetic testing.

4 × 6 array · 24 cultures · 6 conditions · 3 intensity levels

Orange marks show what is not on record yet.

Purpose

Our optogenetic system depends on light intensity and wavelength. To test the CcaS/CcaR circuit reliably, every culture needed to receive controlled and repeatable illumination. We therefore built the Light Plate Apparatus to expose multiple samples to defined red and green light conditions while minimizing variation between wells.

Dual‑Wavelength Array

Intensity is controlled through pulse‑width modulation from an Arduino, with one MOSFET assigned to each intensity tier. Once a condition is set, the output remains fixed throughout the run so the LPA provides repeatable illumination rather than adjusting light dynamically.

The LPA reached this form in three design‑build‑test‑learn cycles, and what each cycle found set the next one’s design.

Prototype 1 — Dual‑Wavelength Array

Does the idea work at all? A green and a red LED in every column, switched on and off, with nothing calibrated yet.

  1. DDesignA green and a red LED per column
  2. BBuildThe first array, wired to a breadboard
  3. TTestThe two channels switch, but the output is uneven
  4. LLearnLED output varies, and outside light gets in

Design

The first prototype tested the simplest version of the idea by placing one green and one red LED in each column, allowing us to switch between wavelengths and directly control the light signal used for protectant production. At this stage, the LEDs were not yet calibrated because our goal was first to confirm that the dual‑wavelength concept worked before refining its precision. We also included basic wire‑management features from the beginning to keep the growing wiring harness organized.

Build

The first prototype used green and red LEDs mounted in a 3D‑printed holder and wired to a breadboard for direct control. This setup allowed us to test whether the dual‑wavelength concept could work before refining the optical output or enclosure.

Not on recordThe board layout, and how the LEDs were wired and driven at this stage. More construction photos from this prototype are wanted too.

Test

We powered the first dual‑wavelength array and confirmed that the red and green LED channels could be switched independently. However, the test also showed that LEDs driven at the same setting did not produce consistent light output, and the open setup allowed ambient light to reach the cultures.

Not on recordMarked missing in review: no measurements, settings or photographs from this first test run are recorded, only the outcome described above.

Learn

The first prototype confirmed that the dual‑wavelength concept was feasible, but it also revealed two major sources of variation that had to be addressed before the light conditions could be compared reliably.

LED output was inconsistent

Individual LEDs produced different light intensities even when driven at the same setting. This meant that our low, mid, and high intensity levels were not yet consistent across the array, making comparisons between conditions unreliable.

External light interfered with the experiment

Because the array was still open, ambient light could reach the culture tubes and add uncontrolled illumination. This was especially important for the lowest‑intensity conditions, where outside light could make up a larger portion of the total exposure.

Prototype 2 — Matched Output & Light‑Shielding

Make intensity a real quantity: measure every LED, build columns of matched LEDs, and shield the array from outside light.

  1. DDesignThree intensity tiers from matched LEDs
  2. BBuildEvery LED measured on its own, then rewired
  3. TTest40 readings, sorted into matched groups
  4. LLearnMatching works; the tiers must come from PWM

Design

Prototype 2 focused on making the light conditions reproducible. We measured the output of every LED individually, then grouped LEDs with similar intensities into matched sets for each wavelength. These matched LEDs formed the low, mid, and high intensity columns. We also added shielding around the array to reduce interference from ambient light.

Build

Every LED was driven at 5 V and measured individually on a lux meter model and resolution not on record using a printed sensor fixture, so each reading is taken at the same distance and orientation. Each LED carries a tag made of its row and its column, with rows A to D running downward and columns 1 to 5 running across, so any physical LED can be traced back to its own measurement. The array was then rewired with the matched units grouped into columns.

Test

We measured all 20 green LEDs and all 20 red LEDs individually at 5 V, then sorted them into matched groups of four based on output. After rewiring, the six channels formed the low, mid, and high conditions for each wavelength.

Learn

Matching the LEDs substantially reduced variation within each group. Before sorting, the green LEDs varied by 26% and the red LEDs by 22%. After matching, the four LEDs within each group differed by only about 0.4–1.7% for green and 1.5–1.8% for red, showing that the matching process successfully improved consistency.

However, matching alone did not create three distinct intensity levels. The low, mid, and high groups were still too close together in output, especially for the green LEDs. The green high group also remained slightly less consistent than the other green groups because it was formed from the remaining LEDs that did not fit as closely into the first two groups.

This showed that LED matching should be used to make each column internally consistent, while the actual low, mid, and high intensity levels should be set through PWM duty cycle rather than LED selection.

Prototype 2 also left several practical limitations unresolved: the electronics were still exposed, intensity settings still had to be adjusted manually, and the culture tubes did not yet have a fixed, repeatable position above the LEDs. These issues shaped the design of the next prototype.

DBTLPrototype3

Prototype 3 — Enclosed Software‑Controlled Instrument

  1. DDesignEnclosure, intensity in code, sliders, fixed tubes
  2. BBuildArduino PWM through three MOSFETs, one per tier
  3. TTestOvernight on a shaker: a post snapped, light leaked
  4. LLearnFour screws, reinforced solder joints, black tube holder

Design

Prototype 3 kept the matched LED groups and light shielding from Prototype 2, but focused on making the LPA easier to operate and reproduce through four key changes.

Enclosed electronicsA printed outer frame houses the LED wiring and perfboard so the electronics are protected and contained.

Intensity in softwareLow, mid, and high light levels are set through PWM values in code rather than by manual adjustment.

Tool‑free assemblyPrinted slider components allow the LPA to be assembled and disassembled without tools.

Fixed tube positionThe tube holder keeps each culture at the same distance from its LED, improving repeatability between runs.

Build

The MOSFET switching stage was moved from a breadboard beside the array to a perfboard mounted beneath the LED holder, allowing the electronics to be integrated into the enclosed device.

Control runs from an Arduino Uno R4 WiFi. PWM signals from the Arduino drive three MOSFETs, one per intensity tier, and software sets the duty cycle for each tier. The selected intensities are held constant for the duration of a test, so each culture receives a fixed, well‑defined dose. Three calibrated tiers across two wavelengths give six defined light conditions per run.

Test

During the overnight shaker test, one of the printed connection posts on the bottom plate broke off and several wires came loose and had to be resoldered. The run also revealed light leakage through the white tube holder, allowing illumination from neighboring LEDs to reach adjacent wells and compromising the intended light conditions.

Learn

Reinforce the base at four points

The overnight run showed that concentrating the load on only two mounting points was not mechanically robust enough. We therefore used all four screw positions on the bottom plate to distribute the load more evenly and reinforced the soldered connection. These changes have not yet been tested in a second overnight shaker run.

Block light between neighboring wells

The white tube holder allowed light to scatter into adjacent wells, so we replaced it with a black holder and increased the wall thickness from 2 loops to 4. The darker, thicker walls were designed to improve optical isolation between light conditions.

Final Design

After three prototypes, the final LPA combines the features that proved necessary across the engineering cycles: matched LED groups, PWM‑based intensity control, enclosed electronics, fixed tube positioning, and improved light shielding.

The final assembly consists of a reinforced base plate, enclosed housing, internal perfboard, slider components, and a black tube holder designed to reduce cross‑illumination between neighboring wells. Together, these changes make the system more repeatable to assemble, easier to operate, and better suited for controlled optogenetic experiments.

The 3D walkthrough at the top of this page still shows the white tube holder; the final one is black.

Limitations

Fixed dose, no feedback

The LPA delivers a preset light dose and does not respond to culture conditions during a run. This makes it suitable for characterization, but not for closed‑loop control without an added feedback system.

Wavelengths assumed, not measured

The green and red wavelengths are based on published CcaS/CcaR values rather than measurements from our own LEDs. The stated wavelengths therefore remain assumptions until the LEDs are directly characterized.

No correction for drift

LED matching reduces variation between components at assembly, but it does not correct for changes in output as LEDs warm up or age during longer runs.

Measurement note

Lux is weighted for human vision, so red and green readings should not be treated as equivalent biological light doses. Future characterization should use photon flux.

References

The LPA is not a new idea. Illuminating a plate of cultures from below with per‑well control is the Light Plate Apparatus pattern from the optogenetics literature citation; our LPA follows that pattern and is named after it. What we built on top of it is the dual‑wavelength column layout, the resistance‑matched LED selection, and the enclosure and tube holder that fit our tubes.

The biology is the CcaS/CcaR green/red switch, also from the literature, not designed here. Our dose target comes from the Castillo‑Hair characterisation of that system citation, whose half‑maximal point is 4.66 µmol m−2 s−1 value and source to confirm.
