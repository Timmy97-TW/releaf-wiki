Technical record

LPA

A dual‑wavelength LED array. A testing apparatus that holds engineered Bacillus subtilis 168 under six defined light conditions in a single run, so the effect of wavelength and intensity on protectant production can be measured directly.

Section 1, the hardware highlights, is the 3D walkthrough above. Where a value or a passage is not on record yet, it is marked in orange with what is missing rather than estimated.

Section02

Purpose

The LPA is a testing apparatus, not part of the bioreactor control loop. It exists to answer one biological question:

How does the wavelength and intensity of applied light affect protectant development in engineered Bacillus subtilis 168?

The strain carries a CcaS/CcaR light‑responsive expression system. Green light induces protectant production and red light halts it. To characterise that switch we need to expose cultures to defined wavelengths at defined intensities, repeatably, with nothing else varying. The LPA is the fixture that makes that possible.

The array is a 4 × 6 grid built around two wavelengths and three intensity levels. Each of the three tiers has a green channel and a red channel, and each channel is four internal‑resistance‑matched LEDs. One run therefore compares low, medium and high intensity at both wavelengths at the same time, under identical external conditions, which is the only way the comparison is fair.

Intensity is set by pulse‑width modulation from an Arduino through three MOSFETs, one per tier, and each tier is held constant for the whole of a test. The LPA applies a fixed dose rather than adjusting output as it goes. It characterises, it does not control.

The LPA reached this form in three design‑build‑test‑learn cycles, and what each cycle found set the next one’s design ().

3.1 Design

The first build tests the simplest version of the idea: put a green and a red LED in each column, so we can switch between them quickly and exercise the on and off control of protectant production directly. Nothing is calibrated at this stage, because the question is whether the concept works at all before we spend effort making it precise.

Wire‑management prints were included from the start, since even at this size the harness gets unmanageable fast.

3.4 Learn

The dual‑wavelength concept worked, but two things were not controlled, and both matter more than they first appear.

LED‑to‑LED output was not consistent

Internal‑resistance variance is a common problem in cheap single‑bulb LEDs. Nominally identical parts draw different currents and emit different intensities at the same drive level. As long as that is true, an intensity tier is not a real quantity, and the wavelength comparison is not fair either, since the green and red channels would be running at unknown relative brightness.

External light was not blocked

Ambient light adds an unknown amount of illumination to every tube, and it lands hardest on the low‑intensity condition, which is exactly where we need the cleanest separation.

Both have to be solved before intensity means anything, which is what cycle 2 is for.

4.1 Design

Cycle 2 introduces the three‑intensity structure that defines the array, and the LED matching needed to make it real.

The approach is to measure the light output of every LED individually and use that to identify units with similar internal resistance. From the matched units we form three groups of four per wavelength, one group per intensity tier, arranged as columns.

Because the LEDs within a column are matched, the column can be recalibrated as a unit through straightforward mathematical adjustment, so that every column at a given tier matches every other. That is what makes low, medium and high reproducible quantities rather than labels.

Coverage was added to block external light, so that the intensity a culture receives is the intensity we set.

4.2 Build

Every LED was driven at 5 V and measured individually on a lux meter model and resolution not on record using a printed sensor fixture, so each reading is taken at the same distance and orientation. Each LED carries a tag made of its row and its column, with rows A to D running downward and columns 1 to 5 running across, so any physical LED can be traced back to its own measurement. The array was then rewired with the matched units grouped into columns.

4.4 Learn

The variance is real and it is large. The green LEDs ran from 2.01 to 2.54 kLux, a spread of 26% across parts sold as identical. The red LEDs ran from 360 to 438 lux, a spread of 22%. Building intensity tiers out of unsorted LEDs would have meant every column sitting at an unknown brightness, so the matching step is not optional.

Sorting by measured output tightens each group considerably. The green low group holds 2.33 to 2.34 and the green mid group holds 2.35 to 2.37, which is well inside 1%. The red groups hold to about 2% each.

Two things did not come out clean, and both need addressing before this data supports an intensity claim.

  • The loosest of the three green groups. Green high spans 2.38 to 2.42 kLux, about 1.7%, against 0.4% and 0.9% for the other two green groups. It is the leftover group, made of what did not match anything else.
    To confirmOn the raw readings this group (1.7%) is as tight as the red groups (about 2%), so this point may not stand. The doc’s “roughly 5%, five times looser” rests on two 2.50 readings that are not in . Team to decide.
  • The tiers are too close together. Green low to green high is about 4%, and red low to red high about 10%. That is the natural spread of the parts, sorted, rather than three distinct light levels.
    To confirmThe doc gives about 7% for green, from the same 2.50 readings.

The tiers therefore have to come from the PWM duty cycle rather than from LED selection. Matching makes the four LEDs in a column agree with each other, and software sets how bright that column runs. That is what cycle 3 builds.

What remained after this cycle was also practical. The electronics were still exposed, intensity was still set by hand, and there was no dedicated way to hold the test tubes in a repeatable position over the array.

5.1 Design

Cycle 3 keeps the calibrated tiers and the light shielding from cycle 2 and turns the array into something that can be handed to someone else and used. Four changes do that. Each is tagged with the CAD part it lives in.

01 · LPA_Box

Enclosed electronicsAn outer frame print houses the LED wiring and the perf board, so the electronics are enclosed rather than sitting loose on the bench.

02 · Arduino code

Intensity in codeLight intensity is varied in code, so a run is defined by a set of values rather than by a physical adjustment someone has to remember.

03 · Left_C, Right_C

Tool‑free assemblySlider prints allow assembly and disassembly without tools.

04 · Tube_Holder

Fixed tube positionA tall print holds the test tubes at a fixed position over the array, so the distance between LED and culture is the same every run.

5.2 Build

Control runs from an Arduino Uno R4 WiFi. PWM signals from the Arduino drive three MOSFETs, one per intensity tier, and software sets the duty cycle for each tier. The selected intensities are held constant for the duration of a test, so each culture receives a fixed, well‑defined dose. Three calibrated tiers across two wavelengths give six defined light conditions per run.

5.3 Test

During our overnight test run, the connection point on the bottom plate broke off. This happened because the LPA was put on a shaker, and the frequency was likely too high for the print to handle. Several wires came undone and had to be resoldered.

The white tube holder print () allowed light to leak into neighbouring holes. This compromised our data, as the test tubes were receiving light from more than one source.

5.4 Learn

Hold the plate at four points

In future runs we will screw in all four screws on the bottom plate instead of just two, which should distribute the force more evenly so it is less likely to break. Every soldered connection was reinforced so that it would not break under the same conditions.

Black, thicker tube holder

We replaced the white tube holder print with a black one, and increased its wall thickness from 2 loops to 4 (; the last two panels of set the white and the black side by side).

6.3 Limitations

A fixed dose, no feedback

The LPA applies a fixed dose and does not respond to the culture, so it cannot be used to close a control loop. That is deliberate, but it means nothing here transfers directly to the bioreactor without a feedback path being added.

Wavelengths assumed, not measured

The wavelengths are currently taken as the classic CcaS/CcaR values rather than measured on our own parts, so the stated peaks are assumptions until the LEDs are characterised.

No correction for drift

Matching corrects for spread between LEDs at build time. It does not correct for drift as the LEDs age or warm up during a long run.

A note on the units

Every reading on this page is in lux, which is weighted for human vision (peak near 555 nm), so the weighting error is not the same size on the green and the red channel. The dose that matters to the cells is photon flux, in µmol m−2 s−1. The team found this in week 16; until the readings are converted, they compare LEDs with each other and cannot be compared with a published dose.

Section10

References

The LPA is not a new idea. Illuminating a plate of cultures from below with per‑well control is the Light Plate Apparatus pattern from the optogenetics literature citation; our LPA follows that pattern and is named after it. What we built on top of it is the dual‑wavelength column layout, the resistance‑matched LED selection, and the enclosure and tube holder that fit our tubes.

The biology is the CcaS/CcaR green/red switch, also from the literature, not designed here. Our dose target comes from the Castillo‑Hair characterisation of that system citation, whose half‑maximal point is 4.66 µmol m−2 s−1 value and source to confirm.
