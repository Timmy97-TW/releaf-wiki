Summary

The photometer reads OD600 from the flowing culture without ever drawing a sample.

A 600 nm LED shines through a 2 mm flow cuvette in the bioreactor’s culture loop. A nominal 90:10 splitter sends a tenth of the beam to a reference sensor before the sample, and OD600 comes from the ratio of the two sensors, so each reading is normalized against the LED’s own output. It ran in line for more than 400 hours, 2,132 points logged over the first 336, with nothing withdrawn from the culture.

Against the BioDrop, its raw output agreed to a median 3.8% with no correction applied. Its parts come to US$360.60 in known costs, before the printing filament and screws, against about US$15,000 for the BioDrop; the materials list (10.1) itemizes each part.

Part1

Overview

In‑Line Flow‑Cell Photometer

A subsystem of the ReLEAF bioreactor.

The In‑Line Flow‑Cell Photometer is the optical‑density subsystem of the bioreactor. Medium from the lumen loop of the hollow‑fiber bioreactor passes through a flow cuvette with a 2 mm light path, where the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) is recorded continuously, without drawing a sample. We built two designs: the “Pro” photometer, used in lab testing, and the “Slim” photometer, tested in early October, which reduces cost while keeping its reliability, as its R² value shows. Reproduction (part 10) gives the CAD STL files for both, and the materials list and a shortened assembly guide for the Pro photometer, for readers who want to replicate this subsystem without the full bioreactor.

Before reading the culture with our own photometer, we established a growth curve of Bacillus subtilis 168 in the hollow‑fiber membrane (part 2).

Prototypes I‑0606 and I‑0610 · 6–10 June 2026

Design

The objective was a functional continuous‑flow loop through a PES hollow‑fiber protein membrane (lumen volume ~200 mL), with a peristaltic pump, a Luer valve and a 500 mL reservoir containing 300 mL of LB medium, inoculated with 20 mL of a multi‑overnight B. subtilis liquid culture (the illustration below).

Build

After the pump of prototype I‑0606 leaked (Test, below), it was replaced by the LP01‑1S3*5, a pump with more durable tubing (prototype I‑0610), chosen over BPT tubing because it was available in our workspace and expected to be strong enough.

Test

The peristaltic pump reached the target flow rate of 94 mL/min, but the first trial was terminated early by localized medium leakage within the pump. Final OD600 readings were 0.304 (in‑tube) and 0.48 (medium reservoir) (the growth chart below). To confirm microbial presence and viability, 2 mL of medium was drawn from the shell side of the hollow‑fiber membrane and plated on LB agar. We observed no colony formation (the plate below), providing functional proof that our bioreactor is biosafe for public agricultural use.

With the new pump, the test ran for 27 hours before its tubing also failed under the load of driving flow through the hollow‑fiber membrane. Over that run, the bioreactor culture changed little relative to the stationary flask, which shows that the low bioreactor readings came from the setup, not from a faulty pump.

Learn

The divergent tube and reservoir OD600 values indicated poor microbial viability; we hypothesize that without active mixing, nutrient stratification and cell sedimentation in the reservoir limited growth. Three changes followed: biocompatible tubing (BPT), for durability and stable sealing at the pump’s transition points; a magnetic stirrer, based on the laboratory hot‑plate stirrer, against sedimentation; and sampling ports moved to the reservoir outlet, for accuracy and lower contamination risk.

Design

V1 places the bench layout in the loop. The light path stays horizontal, as on the bench, and each component stays adjustable, since the cycle 1 spacings were not yet trusted as final. The in‑line flow cuvette reads the culture with nothing drawn out.

Build

The optical train slides on a single printed rail, set by hand to the cycle 1 distances. The flow cuvette is plumbed into the loop at both ends.

Test

The readings fluctuated drastically, because of bubbling in the flow cuvette and the loose tolerances of the lens and beam‑splitter mounts.

Learn

Both causes were structural, not electronic. In a horizontal path a bubble stays in the beam until flow pushes it out, corrupting a long stretch of readings, and the loose mounts let the lens and beam splitter drift from the cycle 1 geometry between runs. V2 therefore stands the light path up and tightens the tolerances.

Design

Bubbles still paused in the vertical cell. A tilt leaves no horizontal surface inside the cuvette, so bubbles have a continuous upward path along the wall. The tilt sits in a wedge under the body, which keeps the cycle 1 beam geometry and can be reprinted at another angle without disturbing the alignment. Gas is also removed from the loop before a run.

Build

The printed wedge has structural support underneath, so the body does not flex under its own weight. A dual Luer valve with syringes bleeds gas from the loop before the instrument starts.

The instrument separates the two concentrations cleanly and in the right direction, so the tilted geometry holds; doubling the TiO2 moved the reading by a factor of 1.56, which this data alone cannot explain. TiO2 shows that the optics work, but as a static sample it does not move, grow or produce bubbles. V4 therefore requires an enclosed light path from the LED to both sensors, direct LED control in case the source drifts, live display of both channels, and a long run on flowing culture.

Design

V4, the prototype of the long run, makes three changes:

  • Component positions are fixed at their default alignment, which removes the drift seen in V1.
  • TPU lines every mount surface touching glass, gripping the cuvette and lens without scratching them; scattering from a scratched 2 mm cuvette window reaches the same detector as scattering from cells.

Build

The instrument runs on an Arduino Uno Q. A live web dashboard on the local network shows OD600, elapsed time, both lux readings, their ratio, the bubble and outlier rejection rate and the data‑point count, so the run can be watched from any machine without touching the rig.

Part4

Simplified Photometer (Slim)

4.1 Rationale

The beam splitter, the paralleling lens and the flow cuvette account for US$275 of the Pro photometer’s US$360.60 in parts (10.1). The flow cuvette is required for in‑line measurement, so the Slim removes only the paralleling lens and the beam splitter.

4.2 Design Changes

Optical fibers carry the light in parallel paths of controllable, proportional intensity, which replaces both the paralleling lens and the beam splitter. For ease of design, the fibers are split 1:1 between the sample and reference sensors, instead of the Pro’s 9:1. These changes reduced the size of the photometer by more than two‑thirds and its cost by more than US$130.

4.3 Comparison with Pro

The Pro reads OD600 nearly 1:1 with the BioDrop, so the bounds of its range also roughly match the BioDrop’s. The Slim matches in linearity but reads values about 5 times smaller, a sensitivity ⅕ that of the other two instruments; its estimated gain in range is untested (5.3). The Slim is cheaper to build and has fewer components, so it needs less maintenance to stay accurate over long periods. Part 5 addresses the ⅕ difference.

4.4 CAD Files & Material List

The Slim photometer has no full assembly guide yet. Its materials list (10.1) and CAD STL files (10.2) are in Reproduction.

Part5

Calibration

5.1 Rationale

Calibrating with culture brings problems of growth time, settling and cell lysis, so we used stable scattering targets. The iGEM standard for OD600 calibration uses SiO2; TiO2 was available in our lab, performs nearly identically when suspended in 1% SDS solution, and is cheaper, so we ran a TiO2 calibration curve first and an SiO2 curve second. The Slim photometer was finished after the TiO2 test and was tested only in the SiO2 run. The mass of particles added is the ground‑truth linear axis against which the BioDrop, the Pro and the Slim are each judged independently.

5.2 TiO₂ Calibration of the Pro Photometer

Known amounts of TiO2 were added to a 500 mL solution, with a BioDrop cuvette spectrophotometer as the reference instrument: 1 mg at a time up to 10 mg, then in 5 mg increments up to 60 mg. The small first steps give a denser low‑density region, in case the in‑line photometer proved inaccurate at low values; the uniform wider spacing gives a direct agreement comparison while still testing linearity.

One single‑point exclusion was applied on objective grounds (open marker): the 5 mg BioDrop reading exceeds the readings both above and below it, which is impossible for an increasing standard. Against the TiO2 mass added, each instrument regresses at R² > 0.999 (Pro photometer 0.9999, BioDrop 0.9998), so the raw, uncalibrated output is as linear in true concentration as the reference instrument.

A line fit through the raw Test 2 points has a residual scatter of RMSE = 0.011 OD600 (R² = 0.9997), which quantifies the tightness of the raw agreement.

We had expected to need a calibration transform fitted against the BioDrop, but the raw output already sits near one‑to‑one with the reference (median paired difference under 4%). On the evidence of these two runs we treat calibration as unnecessary, not pending, and every reading in this wiki, including the continuous growth curve, is the raw, uncalibrated output; the SiO2 run was still to confirm this.

The two TiO2 runs in fullthe loop, Test 1, its exclusion

Test 1 added TiO2 1 mg at a time for 40 data points, a dense, uniform series that resolves how linear each instrument is; Test 2 is the series above. The loop consisted of a magnetic stirrer, a peristaltic pump, the photometer and a 500 mL reservoir, whose cap had holes for the loop inlet and outlet, a sampling port, and an air hole that prevented a vacuum during sampling or pressurization when adding TiO2.

In Test 1 the 37 mg BioDrop reading is a handling error, off the trend of its neighbors, shown as an open marker. Its paired photometer reading is on trend, which puts the error in the reference measurement.

Against TiO2 mass, the raw output has a residual scatter comparable to the BioDrop’s (Test 1, 2–40 mg: 0.030 against 0.031 OD600).

5.3 SiO₂ Calibration of the Pro and Slim Photometers

The Pro and Slim photometers ran in sequence on one SiO2 series. Before each addition, a set amount of the solution was sampled for the BioDrop, keeping the total volume constant.

Both photometers responded consistently as SiO2 increased. The BioDrop readings are irregular past OD600 2, because from there each sample was diluted 4‑fold and the reading multiplied by 4, which amplifies any small discrepancy; past that point the BioDrop data are almost entirely unreliable.

The Slim was more linear (R² = 0.992) than the Pro (R² = 0.974), which demonstrates the Slim’s reliability and supports the Pro’s TiO2 result. The likely cause is the Slim’s structure, which places the light closer to the sensors and cuvette. At that distance, light scattered through small angles by the 1 µm SiO2 particles still reaches the 3.5 mm × 2 mm sensor receptor. More light therefore reaches the Slim’s sample sensor and its measured OD600 is lower: it no longer matches the BioDrop, but the measurable range increases.

To compare sensitivity within the BioDrop’s specifications, the range was narrowed to OD600 1.

In this range the R² values were 0.9956 (BioDrop), 0.9874 (Pro) and 0.9947 (Slim): within the range where the BioDrop is reliable, all three instruments follow SiO2 concentration in the same proportional way. The Pro’s line runs parallel to the BioDrop’s. Plotted against each other, the two give a slope of 1.001 and a constant offset of about 0.12 OD (intercept −0.123), the Pro reading lower at every point.

The Slim’s readings fall on a straight line against the Pro’s (R² = 0.993) at about ⅕ of the scale: each increase in SiO2 moves its reading about ⅕ as much. Because its reading climbs more slowly, the Slim should read denser solutions before its sensor runs out of range. We estimate about five times the range of the BioDrop, but have not tested past the top of this series.

5.4 Blank Protocol

The BioDrop is normally calibrated against distilled water, and this difference in blanks caused much of the inaccuracy of our early prototypes (3.3). Both instruments are now blanked on the growth medium, which accounts for the medium’s own absorption of 600 nm light. This change gave almost entirely accurate data that aligned strongly with the BioDrop readings.

5.5 Calibration Procedure

Neither photometer needs a calibration equation fitted to the BioDrop, since both gave nearly equally linear data in the TiO2 and SiO2 tests. The Pro reads OD600 directly; the Slim’s readings need one scale factor (×5.57, 5.3).

6.2.2 Optical Layout

Light from the 600 nm LED is made parallel by the lens, narrowed by a 2 mm slit and split 90:10. The sample sensor receives the light after the culture, the reference sensor the LED alone. As the LED ages and dims, both sensors register the change, so OD600 is taken from their ratio and the drift cancels.

Choice of 600 nmthree reasons
  1. Standard. Bacterial density is reported as OD600, so the readings compare directly with the BioDrop and with published growth curves.
  2. Scattering by cells. At 600 nm a culture dims the beam mainly by scattering off the cells; proteins and DNA absorb in the UV, and most of the medium’s color in the blue.
  3. Optogenetic switch. CcaS/CcaR responds to green (~535 nm) and red (~672 nm). Amber lies between the two peaks, so the photometer’s light does not switch production on or off.

The reference side copies the sample side’s geometry, so the two sensors receive the same LED and differ only by the culture. As in the Pro, the sample‑to‑reference ratio cancels LED drift, with the fiber split in place of the beam splitter.

6.4 One Limit for Both

The ×5.57 factor and the OD600 2.5 working limit come from a single run on one scattering standard (silica). Cells are larger and softer than silica and change shape as they grow, so they scatter differently. Both photometers need the same series run on culture before they are used for exact numbers.

Part7

Software

7.1 Data Acquisition and Filtering

  1. Take a burst of 14 readings

    For each measurement the LED switches on and the sensor takes 16 lux readings in a short burst; the first two are discarded while the LED settles. A bubble drifting through the beam during the burst may make one or two readings jump.

  2. Find the median

    The firmware takes the median of the burst, not the mean: one or two extreme readings drag a mean but barely move the median, a bubble‑resistant estimate of the signal.

  3. Measure the spread

    The firmware calculates the median absolute deviation (MAD), the typical distance of the readings from the median.

  4. Reject outliers

    Readings beyond median ± 3·MAD are rejected before OD600 is calculated. The rejection percentage is reported live; above 50% the dashboard shows it in red as an alarm for excessive bubbles in the bioreactor, so a long‑lasting air slug cannot leave the record wrong indefinitely.

Testing the filter on silica (10 October)

On 10 October both photometers ran in series on the same loop for 4.5 hours on 1 µm silica microspheres, across 40 concentrations in 2.5 mg steps (5.3). Each reading was computed with eight estimators from the same 14 readings and compared with the BioDrop. In clean flow the eight never disagreed by more than 0.004 OD, less than the scatter during calibration.

Under bubbles, one‑sided estimators failed. Five of the eight (MAD, Qn, biweight, mean and median) treat unusually bright and dark readings alike; the other three (upper, p75 and p90) keep only the bright end of the burst. The instrument had run “upper”, assuming a bubble can only block light, but in a turbid culture air replaces particle‑laden liquid and more light reaches the detector. The five symmetric estimators stayed within 0.02 OD of each other on the Pro and 0.006 OD on the Slim; the three one‑sided ones moved together away from them, better on the Pro and worse on the Slim, so a bubble pushes differently in the two. We kept the symmetric MAD filter.

No estimator rescues a reading taken through air. A bubble can spoil a few of the 14 readings, or fill the cell for several seconds so that every reading agrees perfectly and is wrong. The MAD filter caught 94% of the first kind and none of the second. In one instance the Pro reported OD600 0.79 for 6 minutes while the actual value was 3.18, and the software flagged every reading as good. We therefore added two gates after the estimator:

Gate 1: the burst against itself

The 14 readings are computed with the MAD filter and with an upper‑percentile estimator; if the two differ by more than 0.03 OD, the reading is rejected. The upper estimator is not used for the OD itself: the two agree within a few thousandths of an OD in clean flow and diverge when a bubble passes. On the 10 October run this flagged 97% of disturbed readings and rejected none of the clean ones.

Gate 2: the reading against the recent past

A reading more than 0.05 OD or 5% from the last accepted value is held back; the new level is accepted once 3 consecutive readings agree with it. This catches air slugs, because a growing culture does not move 0.05 OD in 30 minutes. It costs a few minutes’ delay and some lost points, and keeps the growth curve reliable over days and weeks.

7.2 OD600 Calculation

Each sensor reports illuminance in lux: the sample sensor measures light transmitted through the culture, the reference sensor light diverted before the sample. At the start of a run, a blank on the growth medium gives K, the sample‑to‑reference ratio with no bacteria present:

K= SblankRblank

During the run, each filtered pair of readings is converted to OD600:

OD600= −5 · log10( SliveK · Rlive )

S and R are the sample and reference readings in lux. The factor 5 (10 mm ÷ 2 mm) scales the absorbance across the 2 mm cuvette to the standard 1 cm path. The dashboard snapshots of the 400‑hour run (3.5) reproduce this formula to within 0.0003 OD.

Part8

Electronics and Firmware

8.1 Control Architecture

Both final photometers use a soldered PCB connected to an Arduino Uno Q, not a breadboard. The Uno Q runs the code from a power source alone, with no computer attached, which brings the bioreactor closer to being cheap and accessible and removes a failure point (a computer–Arduino cable coming unplugged).

The Arduino has a limited number of ground ports, so the ground wires of the sensors, LED and transistor are linked together.

A transistor lets the LED run at a different voltage from the sensors and switch on and off without affecting them. As the bioreactor scales up, the LED voltage may need to increase, and keeping the LED off between readings is safer and more sustainable than leaving it on 24/7.

The LED’s ground wire connects to the transistor’s collector. Arduino port D3 connects to the base through a 1.2 kΩ resistor; the base also passes through a 10 kΩ resistor to the emitter output, and these join the sensor ground wires at one Arduino ground port.

The SDA and SCL lines of the sample and reference sensors are joined and connected to the same Arduino ports, so one microcontroller reads both sensors at the same time.

The LED takes a 5 V supply, which may need to increase when the bioreactor scales up; the sensors need 3.3 V. The two sensor supplies are therefore combined and the LED’s is kept separate.

The PCB leaves six wire outputs (SCL, SDA, D3, 3.3 V, 5 V and ground), which simplifies the last setup step.

Part9

Specifications and Future Implications

9.1 Future Use

The photometer lets students and researchers measure OD600 during growth runs without sampling, which removes the chance of human error in that process. Its parts cost about US$360 for the Pro (10.1) and less for the Slim (6.1); the BioDrop it was compared against during calibration costs about US$15,000.

9.2 Limitations

Single‑Wavelength Measurement

The system is designed for 600 nm only, so it cannot replace a spectrophotometer for applications requiring broader wavelength analysis.

Manual LED Alignment

To keep the photometer compact, we did not test multiple LED clip designs, and the LED does not always point straight when first seated. During assembly it is rotated in the clip until the light path aligns with the sensors, a short manual step.

Further Validation of the Slim Photometer

The Slim photometer needs further comparison with the Pro and the BioDrop to determine where its performance matches theirs and where it needs refinement.
