Summary

The photometer reads OD600 from the flowing culture without ever drawing a sample.

A 600 nm LED shines through a 2 mm flow cuvette in the culture loop; a nominal 90 : 10 splitter sends a tenth of the beam to a reference sensor, and OD600 comes from the ratio of the two. It ran in line for more than 400 hours with nothing withdrawn.

Its raw output agreed with the BioDrop to a median 3.8%. The parts cost about $150, against about $15,000 for the BioDrop.

Part2

Overview (Final Design)

To monitor growth without opening the bioreactor, we built an in‑line photometer that measures OD600 directly from the flowing culture.

There are two versions. The complex photometer (“Pro”) is used in lab testing and is the subject of most of this page. The simplified photometer (“Slim”, part 5) was tested in early October, too late to integrate, so the complex one remains the bioreactor’s final assembly.

2.1 Wavelength Selection

  1. Standard for bacterial density. Readings compare directly with the BioDrop and published growth curves.
  2. Measures turbidity. Cells scatter strongly; the medium absorbs less than at shorter wavelengths.
  3. Avoids optogenetic cross‑talk. 600 nm sits between the green (~535 nm) and red (~672 nm) response regions of our CcaS/CcaR system.

2.2 Beam Splitting and Reference

The beam splitter sends 90% of the light on through the flow cuvette to the sample sensor and 10% off at 90° to the reference sensor. An LED degrades over long runs; against the reference, the sample sensor only has to measure the bacteria’s scattering. The software does the calculation, keeping the interface simple for a small‑scale farmer.

Part5

Simplified Photometer

5.1 Rationale

The beam splitter, the paralleling lens and the flow cuvette account for nearly all of the complex photometer’s cost. Optical fibers replace the lens and the splitter: they carry the light in parallel paths and split it 1:1 between the sample and reference sensors (9:1 in the complex build). The result is more than two‑thirds smaller.

Part6

Calibration

6.1 TiO₂ Calibration — Complex Photometer

A growing culture changes while it is measured, so no two instruments read quite the same sample; an inert mineral suspension added in weighed steps gives a ground truth independent of both. The iGEM protocol specifies silica, but TiO2 was on the shelf and behaved almost identically in 1% SDS.

Across two runs the raw, uncalibrated output tracked the BioDrop to a median paired difference of 3.8%, both instruments regressing against TiO2 mass at R² > 0.999. No correction transform has been applied since. The run said nothing above OD 1.9, or about the simplified build; the silica run covers both.

The two TiO2 runs in fullmethod, exclusions, Test 1

TiO2 in 500 mL of 1% SDS, against a BioDrop cuvette spectrophotometer, run twice. Test 1 added 1 mg at a time for 40 points; Test 2 added 1 mg at a time to 10 mg, then 5 mg at a time to 60 mg. The loop: a magnetic stirrer, a peristaltic pump, our photometer and a 500 mL reservoir whose cap had an inlet, an outlet, a sampling port and an air hole.

Two single‑point exclusions, shown as open markers: the 37 mg BioDrop reading in Test 1 (off its neighbors’ trend) and the 5 mg one in Test 2 (above the readings on both sides). In both, the paired ReLEAF reading is on trend, which puts the error in the reference.

The raw Test 2 points scatter RMSE = 0.011 OD600 (R² = 0.9997) about the line of identity. Against TiO2 mass the raw output scatters about as much as the BioDrop (Test 1, 2–40 mg: 0.030 against 0.031 OD600), so fitting our readings to the BioDrop would only tie them to its error.

6.2 SiO₂ Calibration — Complex Photometer

On 10 October, silica (the iGEM calibration standard) was added to 500 mL of 1% SDS in 2.5 mg steps from 2.5 to 100 mg, forty points, stirred and circulated through the same loop. Every step was read on the BioDrop, the complex photometer and the simplified photometer (Prototype 2, part 5), the first run to put all three on one series.

Where the reference runs out

The BioDrop’s direct channel climbs to about OD 2.3, then from 70 mg returns exactly 2.50 at every point: a ceiling. Past it we read dilutions (two-fold from 22.5 mg, four-fold from 50 mg) and multiplied back, so every BioDrop value above OD 2.5 is reconstructed. From 22.5 to 50 mg the direct and two-fold readings disagree by up to 14% (35 mg: 1.536 against 1.326); above 50 mg the corrected BioDrop falls as silica is added at 7 of 20 steps, worst by 0.34 OD at 80 mg, which a weighed inert standard cannot do. Above OD 2.5 the BioDrop is a noisy estimate.

6.3 SiO₂ Comparison — Simplified vs. Complex vs. BioDrop

The same series, on the simplified photometer. It is linear across the entire range: R² = 0.992 against silica mass, against the complex build’s 0.974, reversing direction only once in 39 steps, by 0.013 OD.

With R² = 0.993 between them, one constant converts it: OD = 5.573 × r − 0.073. Rescaled, its mean difference from the BioDrop below OD 2.5 is −0.10, near enough the complex build’s. But the factor is a 5.6‑fold compression: the simplified reading moves only from 0.03 to 0.60, the complex one from 0.05 to 3.17. Quieter scatter does not pay for the lost span, so it resolves a change about twice as coarse. The team expects the compressed scale to give a range about five times wider than the BioDrop’s, not yet tested above the top of this series.

Final Designs & Specifications

7.1 Which Version Should Someone Use?

Use the complex photometer for a bioreactor run.
It reads OD600 directly, resolves twice as finely, and its reference beam keeps a slow drift in the LED from being recorded as growth over hundreds of hours.
Use the simplified photometer where cost and size decide.
A teaching lab, a replicate vessel, or anywhere a second instrument is better than none: it is smaller, cheaper and has fewer parts, but needs its scale factor (6.3).

Part8

Software

8.1 Data Acquisition and Filtering

  1. Take a burst of 14 readings

    The LED switches on and each sensor takes 16 lux readings; the first two are dropped while the LED settles.

  2. Find the median

    One or two readings jumped by a bubble barely move the median.

  3. Measure the spread

    The median absolute deviation (MAD).

  4. Reject outliers

    Readings beyond median ± 3·MAD are rejected. The rejection percentage is reported live; above 50% the dashboard shows it in red and asks the user to clear bubbles in the bioreactor.

Testing the filter on silica (10 October)

During the 4.5‑hour silica run (6.2, 1 µm microspheres) we logged eight estimators from the same 14 readings. In clean flow they differed by at most 0.004 OD. Under bubbles the five symmetric ones (MAD, Qn, biweight, mean, median) stayed within 0.02 OD of each other on the complex build and 0.006 OD on the simplified one; the three bright‑end ones (upper, p75, p90) moved away, since in a turbid culture air lets more light through. We kept MAD.

A bubble that fills the cell for seconds makes every reading wrong and in agreement. The MAD filter caught 94% of partial disturbances and none of these: once the complex photometer reported 0.79 OD for 6 minutes while the actual value was 3.18. So two gates follow the filter:

Gate 1: the burst against itself

If the MAD result and an upper‑percentile estimate of the same 14 readings differ by more than 0.03 OD, the reading is rejected. On the 10 October run this flagged 97% of disturbed readings and rejected no clean ones.

Gate 2: the reading against the recent past

A reading more than 0.05 OD or 5% from the last accepted value is held back until 3 consecutive readings agree with it; a growing culture does not move 0.05 OD in 30 minutes. The cost is a few minutes’ delay.

8.2 OD600 Calculation

A blank on the growth medium gives K, the sample‑to‑reference ratio with no bacteria:

K= SblankRblank

During the run, each filtered pair converts to OD600 as:

OD600= −5 · log10( SliveK · Rlive )

S and R are the sample and reference readings in lux. The 5 is 10 mm ÷ 2 mm, scaling the absorbance across the 2 mm cuvette to the standard 1 cm path. The dashboard snapshots in 4.5 reproduce this formula to within 0.0003 OD.

Part9

Electronics

9.1 Control Architecture

Both versions use a soldered PCB on an Arduino Uno Q, which runs from a power supply alone, with no computer attached.

One shared ground. Sensor, LED and transistor grounds are joined.

A transistor for the LED. The LED on the collector; pin D3 drives the base through 1.2 kΩ, with 10 kΩ from base to emitter.

Two sensors on one bus. Both GY‑302 sensors share SDA and SCL at separate addresses.

Two supply voltages. 5 V for the LED, 3.3 V for the sensors.

Six connections to the Arduino: SCL, SDA, D3, 3.3 V, 5 V and ground.

Part10

Future Implications & Limitations

10.1 Limitations

Single‑Wavelength Measurement

It measures only at 600 nm, so it cannot replace a spectrophotometer for broader wavelength analysis.

Manual LED Alignment

We did not test several LED clip designs, so the LED does not always point straight when seated; during assembly it is rotated in the clip until the light reaches the sensors.

10.2 Future Implications

At an estimated $150, against about $15,000 for the BioDrop, it could bring continuous OD600 monitoring without manual sampling to student labs and smaller facilities. Next: easier alignment, simpler assembly, validation in long‑term operation, and wavelengths beyond OD600.
