Summary

A printed plate that floats on the medium, so thirteen Arabidopsis seedlings sit at one depth however much liquid is left. We built it for the plant team, who grow Arabidopsis in it to test protectants against salt stress (cycle 3, Test).

The flotation is sealed air built into the frame itself: two chambers inside the pontoons, 42.27 cm³ between them, with no foam block and nothing strapped underneath. Its displacement, computed from the parts’ own geometry, gives the plate a load line: it sits about 1 mm lower for each 3.5 g it carries. Sixteen pieces print in PLA on a standard FDM machine and go together with no adhesives and no fasteners.

Hydroponics Floating Plate

Part1

Overview

1.1 Standardisation of Hydroponics for Labs

Labs that grow Arabidopsis in liquid medium tend to improvise the setup: a piece of foam cut to size, a mesh taped over a container, or a fixed plate sitting above a reservoir. None of it is repeatable between labs, or even between runs in the same lab.

Our plate floats on the medium, so the seedlings stay at the same depth no matter how much liquid is left. The whole thing is three print files on a standard FDM printer, so another lab can run the same geometry we did. Same depth, same spacing, same conditions, whoever is running it.

1.2 The Problem With Fixed Plates

Our first version was a fixed plate sitting over a liquid chamber. The plate held the seedlings at a set height and the medium sat underneath, so the level had to be set by hand before every run.

The level has to sit just under the growing platform, and as high as possible. Too low and the roots lose contact; too high and the platform floods. The window between those two failures is narrow.

Nor does the level stay where it is set. The plants drink and the medium evaporates, so a level that was right at the start of a run is wrong by the end of it. Someone has to watch the reservoir and top it up, and every top-up is a chance for conditions to drift between replicates. That is what we set out to remove.

Part2

Engineering Cycles

The setup reached its final form in three design–build–test–learn cycles: first the container, then the box we kept, then the printed plate the seedlings sit in. How the plate floats is in 4.3; the plate as it is now, with its specifications and dimensions, is in part 4.

2.1 DBTL Cycle 1 – Finding the Container

Three boxes, three failures, one list of requirements

  1. DDesignA dark tank and a seed board
  2. BBuildLarge tank, small box, tall box
  3. TTestHidden, leaking, too narrow
  4. LLearnClear, deep, sealed, wide

Design

We started from the hydroponic method of Conn et al. (2013)1: seedlings in agar‑filled holders, their roots hanging in ½ MS medium, the tank kept dark against algae, and the medium replaced every 2–3 days. The first container followed it closely: a large opaque tank, with a board on top holding the seed holders, black microcentrifuge‑tube lids filled with agar.

Build

We built three containers in turn, each answering the last one’s failure.

Test

Each box grew seedlings, and each failed in its own way. The black tank was far too big for five seedlings, its foam board was fragile and slow to make, and because it was deep and black we could see neither the roots nor whether the medium had turned cloudy. The small box leaked at its joint and through its bottom whenever it was carried, and black tape over the bottom did not stop it; its lid also fell into the box unless it was set down at one particular angle. The tall clear box held the medium and showed the roots, but its board fitted so tightly that changing the medium was awkward, and it was too narrow for more than a few plants.

Learn

Together they set what the container has to be: clear, to watch the roots and the medium; deep enough for the roots; one watertight piece, to carry to the bench every 2–3 days; wide enough for 5–13 seedlings; and with a board that lifts out.

2.2 DBTL Cycle 2 – The Pipette‑Tip Box

A lab box we already had: clear, deep, autoclavable

  1. DDesignA 1000 µL tip box
  2. BBuildFoam board, 5–13 holes
  3. TTestRoots and medium in view
  4. LLearnKeep the box; print the board

Design

A 1000 µL pipette‑tip box meets every requirement from the first cycle. It is clear, deep and one piece, wide enough for up to 13 seedlings without taking much bench space, and made to be cleaned and autoclaved, so it can be reused run after run.

Build

Test

The roots and the medium could be watched through the clear walls, and the box was contaminated less often than the containers tested before it. It could be reused multiple times after cleaning and autoclaving, and one board took 5 to 13 holes.

Learn

The tip box is the container from here on. The board became the weak part: the foam softened after days in the medium and broke while the medium was being changed, and every hand‑cut board came out a little different. The board had to be printed.

2.3 DBTL Cycle 3 – The Floating Plate

Printed, and floating on its own sealed air

  1. DDesignA printed plate that falls with the medium
  2. BBuildFirst float on 25 June; the plate now; a dark box
  3. TTestFour boxes through a salinity run
  4. LLearnThe poles came off

Design

A printed board keeps its shape in the medium, and every one comes out the same. Our first printed plate sat at a fixed height over the medium, and that showed the next problem: the level has to stay in a narrow window just under the plate, and it drifts as the plants drink and the water evaporates (1.2). So the plate floats instead. Sealed air chambers in its own frame carry it on the medium, and the roots stay at one depth however much liquid is left (4.3).

Build

The first floating version was printed on 25 June, with a pole at each corner carrying washers, so the freeboard could be trimmed after printing instead of reprinting the whole part. The plate now is smaller, 117.5 × 92.5 mm, so it fits the tip box, with 13 seed bores in rows of four, five and four and a clip‑in handle at each end. It prints in PLA: the plate and seed holders with no supports, the handles with 0.86 g of support (4.1).

The tip box is clear so the roots can be watched, but the method keeps the medium dark against algae. So the tip box stands in a black box, 14.7 × 11.5 × 6.2 cm, that covers its lower half, and the tip box’s front clasp sits in a notch in one side. The tip box still lifts straight out to look at the roots.

Test

The plate carried the plant team’s salinity pre‑test: four boxes side by side, one plate in each, for a control and for pre‑, co‑ and post‑treatment with CH Bio peptide (a commercial peptide from CH Biotech that helps plants resist abiotic stress) at 1:500, all under 100 mM NaCl for 7 days. The boxes were photographed from above on 25 July, 28 July and 4 August. By the last date the co‑treatment box had the largest and greenest rosettes and the post‑treatment box the palest, and the co‑treatment plants also had the highest fresh weight and chlorophyll content. The pre‑test ran before the plate’s CAD was revised on 11 August (User Testing), so it used the earlier version of the plate.

In use, the plant team found that the handles make changing the medium easier.

Learn

The corner poles were the safety margin on the buoyancy. We ran the displacement calculation again every time the design changed (4.3), and the volume was tuned closely enough that the poles came off.

The setup’s users are the plant team, who grow Arabidopsis in it for the protectant tests. Two outside visits: one changed the plate, the other changed the plant protocol.

Part3

Results

The plant team ran its CH Bio peptide pre‑tests in our hydroponic boxes: 13‑day‑old Arabidopsis under 100 mM NaCl for 7 days, and 12‑day‑old plants at 37 °C for 7 days, with the peptide given before, with or after the stress. The salt run set the stress for later experiments: 100 mM NaCl stresses the plants significantly without killing them. After several failed plantings in the boxes, the later protectant runs moved to tube racks, where Arabidopsis grew better.

Final Design & Specifications

Three cycles ended on this setup: a 1000 µL pipette‑tip box, the printed plate floating on the medium inside it, and a black box over its lower half to keep the medium dark. The plate falls with the level, so its thirteen seedlings stay at one depth through a run, and it lifts out by its handles when the medium is changed.

4.2 Design Decisions

  • The frame is the flotation. The air that floats the plate is sealed inside its own pontoons, not in a foam block or a float strapped underneath. The slab, both pontoons and their chambers print as one piece.
  • A teardrop cross‑section. Each pontoon is a box section with its lower sides chamfered to a point, a pentagon, so it prints without support.
  • Clip‑in handles. The plate lifts out of the box by its handles, without anyone touching a seedling.
  • No adhesives, no fasteners. The handles clip in and the seed holders drop in, so there is nothing to come unglued in the medium.
  • Printed, not cut. Every plate comes out the same and keeps its shape in the medium, where the foam boards softened and broke (cycle 2).
  • A clear box in a dark one. The tip box shows the roots and the medium; the black box over its lower half keeps light off the medium against algae, and the tip box still lifts out to look at the roots.

4.3 Buoyancy Design

The plate floats on a sealed air volume built into its own frame. There is no foam block and nothing strapped underneath. The pontoon sections are hollow, sealed chambers with a teardrop cross-section. The plate prints at 15% gyroid infill rather than solid, which keeps it light while leaving enough internal structure to hold its shape.

Each pontoon encloses one sealed chamber, a teardrop‑like pentagon in cross‑section (); the two chambers hold 42.27 cm³ of air between them.

Sizing them is a displacement problem. The plate has to displace its own mass plus the seedlings plus whatever water the printed part takes on, and it has to do that while sitting at the freeboard that puts the roots in the medium and the crowns above it. We ran this calculation again every time the design changed, because any change to wall thickness or footprint moves the mass and the displacement at the same time.

The displacement at each waterline gives the plate’s load line (). In water at 1.00 g/cm³ every cubic centimetre displaced floats a gram, so weigh the assembled plate with its seedlings, find that mass along the bottom, and read the freeboard up the side; lists seven of its points. Down the straight sides of the pontoons the hull gains 3.51 cm³ per millimetre of draft, so every 3.5 g added sinks it about 1 mm.
