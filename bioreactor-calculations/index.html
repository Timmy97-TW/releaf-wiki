Bioreactor Calculations

A 150 cm² hollow-fibre module, eight fibres of 1.0 mm bore, a peristaltic pump that will not run slower than 230 mL/min, and a culture of B. subtilis 168 secreting ACC deaminase into the shell side. Every invariant below is computed against that hardware, at the operating point we actually run.

The reactor as it stands on 3 September 2026

The geometry stopped being a design variable in June. What is left to choose is two pump settings and a cell density, and those three numbers fix every quantity on this page.

The vessel is a Biophsep mPES Minilab module, part HF-E-MI-M020-10-60-P, fed inside-out. Eight fibres of 1.0 mm inner diameter and 595.7 mm effective length give 150 cm² of 0.2 µm membrane and hold 3.74 mL between them. That holdup is the whole reason the reactor behaves the way it does: the culture does not live in the fibres, it lives in a reservoir and visits them, at 230 mL/min, roughly 138 times an hour, for about one second each time.

Earlier drafts of this page quoted a 50 kDa cut-off. That was wrong and it has been corrected everywhere. The installed membrane is a 0.2 µm microfilter, which is a different device with different consequences: it retains cells and passes everything smaller, including the 37.6 kDa protein we want to deliver and the vesicles we would rather it did not.

Twenty-four hours after the pumps start

A board of dimensionless groups says whether a design is legal. It says nothing about when the protein arrives. The reactor is a two-compartment system with a growing source in one compartment, so the quantity a biomanufacturing reviewer actually wants is the transient: how the lumen fills, how far behind the shell runs, and when the shell concentration crosses the threshold an assay can see.

Under Level 1 the promoter is constitutive pVeg, so the optogenetic cascade contributes nothing and the model collapses to four states. Growth is logistic with our own 37 °C fit. Production is the specific secretion rate times biomass. Transport is a single two-compartment exchange with the sieving coefficient set to 1, because a 0.2 µm pore does not discriminate against a 37.6 kDa globule.

dX/dt  =  µmax(1 − X/Xmax) X
dPL/dt =  qPX  −  kdegPL  −  (Qp/VL)(PL − PS)
dPS/dt =  (Qp/VS)(PL − PS)  −  kdegPS
µmax = 1.31 h⁻¹, Xmax = 1.54 OD₆₀₀, both from our 1 June refit. Written for mode A.

Only qP and kdeg are unknown, which is what makes the fit tractable. The three secretion scenarios below bracket published heterologous Sec titres in B. subtilis; they are a bracket for sizing the assay, not a prediction. The moment densitometry exists, qP collapses to a number with an interval and everything here is superseded.

The lumen curve is what a whole-culture sample sees; the shell curve is what the blot sees. Which of the three modes ran on the bench is not on record, and the three give different answers. That is item 1 of section 12.

Three things fall straight out of the transient. The shell lags the lumen by VS/Qp, which is eighty minutes at 5 LMH into a 100 mL shell, so a blot taken an hour after inoculation is measuring the transport delay. The optical density plateaus near four hours while the protein keeps accumulating, and that separation is the only thing that lets kdeg be told apart from qP. And under the low bracket a neat lane never reaches the ECL window at all, which is why the concentrated lane is not optional.

Not yet measured

No curve on this page has been fitted to our own protein data. The first anti-His Western on the Level-1 strain runs the day this page was written. Until a standard curve exists on the same blot, the model returns bounds, not parameters.

The pump leaves the practice shear window above 377 mL/min

Wall shear is the master variable in tangential-flow filtration. It sweeps the membrane, it sets the critical flux, and holding it constant is what makes a scale-up linear. For laminar flow in a round lumen it comes out of the flow per fibre and nothing else:

Q₁ = Qxf/N   ·   γ̇w = 32 Q₁ / (π di³)   ·   τw = µ γ̇w   ·   Re = 4ρQ₁ / (π µ di)

Dividing by N first matters. Feeding the pump total into the single-fibre expression overstates the shear eightfold, and that error has appeared in more than one iGEM reactor write-up. With the division done, the numbers are these.

Shear against the pump dial

The dot follows the cross-flow slider in section 1.

Conventional hollow-fibre microfiltration of bacterial cultures runs at 2,000 to 8,000 s⁻¹, and Maiorella's crossflow work puts the balance point between sweeping and damage nearer 1,500 to 3,000 s⁻¹ [9]. The pump floor of 230 mL/min lands at 4,881 s⁻¹, in the upper half of the band and already past Maiorella's balance point. The band runs out at 377 mL/min. That is 6% of a dial that goes to 2,600, so the usable setting is the stop at the bottom of the range. At mid-dial the shear is 2.7 times the top of the band and wide open it is 6.9 times.

Whether that matters depends on the organism. A walled bacterium is not a CHO cell: the 3 to 5 Pa ceiling quoted in mammalian perfusion is irrelevant here, and gross damage to B. subtilis is reported only above roughly 2,770 Pa, which is 560 times our operating stress. Single-pass exposure is safe by a wide margin. Cumulative exposure is the open question: at 230 mL/min a 100 mL loop goes through the fibres 138 times an hour, so a twelve-hour run is sixteen hundred passes through a peristaltic head, and van Reis attributes protein aggregation to pump passages and air entrainment rather than to shear itself [1].

The leading hypothesis

On 6 June a culture in the fibre lumen at 22 °C did not grow at all: mean OD 0.142, regression slope −0.0024 OD/h, p = 0.759, while a serum-bottle control at the same temperature grew. The difference was the reactor, not the temperature. Three causes remain live: oxygen starvation, cumulative shear, and adhesion to the fibre walls pulling cells out of the planktonic signal. The cheapest discriminating test is to run at 230 mL/min and record the setting, which had not been done.

The loop holds two minutes of dissolved oxygen

Oxygen is the constraint most likely to decide the maximum cell density, and in a sealed recirculating loop it is also the one with the weakest supply. The requirement is two lines:

OUR = qO₂ · XDCW   ·   kLareq = OUR / (C* − CDO)
C* = 0.20 mmol/L at 37 °C. CDO is the target, here 30% of saturation.

At our carrying capacity of OD 1.54, with 0.4 g DCW per litre per OD unit and a conservative 3 mmol g⁻¹h⁻¹ uptake, the culture consumes 1.85 mmol L⁻¹h⁻¹ and needs a kLa of 13 h⁻¹. That is a modest number for a sparged fermenter and a demanding one for a closed loop with no gas port.

The rate understates the problem. The inventory shows it. A 100 mL loop held at 30% of saturation contains 0.006 mmol of dissolved oxygen. The same culture consumes 0.185 mmol per hour. Cut the supply and the loop runs dry in under two minutes; even from full saturation it lasts six and a half. Oxygen in this reactor is not a reservoir, it is a throughput, and the fibre lumen with its 3.74 mL holdup contributes essentially none of it.

Fouling is not the constraint at 5 LMH

Below a critical permeate flux, deposition is negligible and transmembrane pressure does not drift; above it, cells build a cake and the membrane fouls [3]. For a particulate suspension in laminar cross-flow the shear-induced diffusion model gives the threshold [4][5]:

Jcrit = 0.072 (a⁴/L)1/3 γ̇w ln(φw/φb)
a = 0.71 µm, the equivalent-sphere radius of a 0.8 × 3 µm rod. φw = 0.60, random close packing. φb from cell density.

The earlier internal explorer exposed the wall-to-bulk ratio as a slider running from 1.2 to 20, which meant anyone could dial the fouling margin to whatever number they wanted. Pinning the wall value at close packing and computing the bulk fraction from density turns Jcrit back into a prediction. At the bench operating point it comes out at 55 LMH against an operating flux of 5, a margin of 0.09.

That is not a close call and it should not be dressed up as one. The membrane is oversized for the duty by roughly an order of magnitude, which follows from the module being chosen for its fibre geometry. The area came with it. Two caveats belong with that. Local flux is not uniform across the surface, so low-level fouling can occur below the nominal threshold and no operating point should ever be quoted as sitting exactly at Jcrit [2]. And the model treats the feed as cells in water; secreted protein, media components and cell debris all add resistance the expression does not carry.

So the consequence is permissive. Flux can be raised more than threefold before fouling becomes the binding constraint, which is exactly the room needed to bring cell-specific perfusion into its conventional band. What stops that is the medium bill, not the membrane.

Cell retention removes the washout limit and replaces it with a medium bill

The 2026 design dossier carried the chemostat inequality Dr < µmax as a washout check. Applied to this reactor it is the wrong invariant, and it has been dropped. A 0.2 µm membrane against 1 to 2 µm rods retains the cells: permeate leaves, biomass does not, so the dilution rate can exceed the growth rate without washing anything out. That is the defining property of perfusion, and quoting a chemostat criterion for it signals that the distinction has not been made.

The invariants that do bite are cell-specific perfusion rate and medium turnover. CSPR is medium delivered per cell per day and modern perfusion practice sits between 0.02 and 0.5 nL. At 5 LMH and OD 1.54 ours is 0.015, just under the band: the culture is being fed a little lean per cell. Reaching 0.05 needs about 17 LMH, which the membrane allows and which the fouling margin has room for.

Then the arithmetic turns. At 5 LMH the module draws 1.25 mL/min, which is 1.8 L of medium a day and eighteen turnovers of the working volume. At 17 LMH it is 6.1 L a day. For a bench run over a shift that is unremarkable. For a device meant to sit unattended in a field for a week it is the binding constraint, and no membrane calculation touches it. The delivery flux is set by what the plant needs at the root, not by what the module will pass, and the link between the two is the reservoir turnover that has not been specified.

What the plant needs is the one number that closes the loop. ACC deaminase runs first order well below its KM of 1.5 to 17.4 mM [7], so at a shell concentration of 0.88 µg/mL, which is 23 nM of a 37.6 kDa enzyme, the pseudo-first-order rate constant against ACC is 1.6 × 10⁻⁵ s⁻¹ and the half-life of ACC in that solution is twelve hours. Whether twelve hours is fast enough depends on the ACC oxidase competition, where Glick puts the required deaminase-to-oxidase ratio at 100 to 1000 fold [8]. That comparison belongs to the plant model and is made on the Math Model page, not here.

The pressure sensor's error bar is as wide as the pressure it reads

Transmembrane pressure is the mean lumen pressure minus the shell pressure, and it drives permeation through the Darcy-Starling relation J = K(ΔPTM − σΔΠ). With σ = 1 for cells and effectively 0 for the protein, the osmotic term drops out and the reading is a clean measure of membrane resistance. The reactor now instruments it with a BP8G-AGA.

The instrument is the problem. It spans 0 to 1 MPa at 1.0% of full scale, which is ±10 kPa absolute regardless of where the needle sits. Our axial drop at the pump floor is 11.6 kPa and the transmembrane pressure at 5 LMH through a clean 0.2 µm membrane is of the same order. The sensor's error bar is therefore about as wide as the signal, and its analog error dominates the 14-bit ADC behind it by a wide margin. Fitting the voltage divider that brings 0 to 5 V into the Uno R4's 3.3 V reference improves nothing about this.

Two consequences follow. Any transmembrane pressure figure from this build should be published as a value against ±10 kPa rather than as a bare number, so the coarseness is visible. And fouling has to be read off a trend across repeated readings, because a drift of 30 kPa over an hour is real where a single reading of 30 kPa is not. A 0 to 100 kPa sensor would resolve ±1 kPa and is the cheapest upgrade available to this reactor.

The light loop is open this season

The project's control claim is dose-proportional enzyme production driven by green light through CcaSR. The Level-2 assembly has not come together, so the strain now in the reactor transcribes from constitutive pVeg. There is no loop to characterise, and reporting a dead-time ratio for a loop that is not closed would be a fabrication.

The arithmetic stays on file. Loop dead time sums sensor sampling, controller cycle, LED ramp, the CcaSR activation half-time, secretion lag and pump transit, and the 105.1 ± 1.5 min activation half-time [6] dominates everything else by an order of magnitude. The system time constant is the shell exchange time VS/Qp, eighty minutes at 5 LMH and twenty at 20 LMH. That puts θ/τ between 1.3 and 5.3, which is dead-time dominated in every case.

A dead-time-dominated loop still works. It works slowly, and the framing has to match. This device is tuned for drought and salinisation developing over hours to days. It cannot answer an acute shock, and any claim that it can is contradicted by a literature parameter we cite ourselves. Raising the flux shortens τ and makes the ratio worse, which is the sort of coupling a controls reviewer looks for and the reason the delivery rate cannot be tuned on plant grounds alone.

Solar closes at 3 W and fails at 8 W

The deployment claim is a solar-powered device with a week of autonomy. The balance is arithmetic once the draw is known, and nobody has metered the draw. Taiwan's worst-case winter peak-sun-hours is about three per day, which is the figure to design against. The annual average flatters the result.

At the bottom of the draw estimate a 45 W panel returns 101 Wh against 72 Wh consumed and the balance closes with 40% to spare. At the top it consumes 192 Wh and the same panel covers barely half. The spread comes almost entirely from one unmeasured quantity, the recirculation pump running continuously at 230 mL/min, and a clamp meter settles it in five minutes.

There is a design answer hiding in the coupling. Section 6 shows the reactor delivers more medium per day than a field deployment can carry, and section 8 shows the biology responds over hours. Both point the same way: run the recirculation pump on a duty cycle rather than continuously. That cuts the dominant load, cuts the cumulative shear exposure behind the June result, and cuts the medium draw, all from one change. It also adds a settling problem in the fibres during the off phase, which is the reason it has not simply been adopted.

One cost has to stay in the budget: the BP8G needs a 24 V rail for a single low-current sensor, and the converter loss on that rail has to be budgeted.

The membrane retains cells and passes vesicles

The 0.2 µm pore is the primary biosecurity barrier, so it has to carry a number. Log reduction value is log10(Cfeed/Cpermeate), and the expected escape per deployment is Cfeed Vpermeate 10−LRV. A 0.2 µm pore against 1 to 2 µm rods should give a large and defensible LRV.

We have not measured it. Publishing a number we have not measured would undercut the rest of the page, so the entry stays open and the test that closes it is trivial: plate 100 µL of shell fluid on LB at every sampling point. If anything grows, the membrane has a pinhole or the strain is producing forms small enough to pass, and every shell-side protein measurement is contaminated by intracellular protein from lysed cells as well. The June protocol already included this test.

Stated because a reviewer will find it

A 0.2 µm membrane retains whole cells and passes outer-membrane vesicles of 20 to 200 nm along with cell debris. The barrier this reactor provides is cellular, not molecular. Any claim that the device blocks vesicular material is contradicted by its own specification sheet.

Scale by adding modules, never by lengthening the fibre

Flux and sieving vary along a finite flow path, so a longer channel is a different process rather than a bigger one. Van Reis's linear scale-up method holds the scale-independent fluid dynamics constant and increases only channel count: filtrate volume per unit area, membrane material and pore size, channel geometry, and retentate and filtrate pressures all stay fixed, and transmembrane pressure is controlled to hold the wall concentration constant [1]. Applied without a pilot stage it carried a 400-fold scale-up.

Hollow fibres are the easiest format to do this with, because a scale-down module can be built with the same fibre length as the industrial system. Our fixed-length module is already that replicable unit cell, which means the scale-up argument for this project is short: hold 230 mL/min per module and 5 LMH per module, and add modules. The slider in section 1 does exactly that. Eight modules give 1,200 cm² and 10 mL/min of permeate at unchanged shear, unchanged flux and unchanged fouling margin.

Two things do not scale with the modules. Cross-flow pumping is per module, so eight modules need eight times the recirculation duty and the power balance in section 9 fails long before the membrane does. And medium draw scales linearly, so eight modules at 5 LMH is 14.4 L a day. The reactor scales cleanly; the deployment around it does not, and saying which is which is the point of running the calculation.

The physical build, its instrumentation and the three-dimensional teardown of this module sit on the Hardware page.
