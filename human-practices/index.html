Overview: the people we met moved the bacteria off the field and into a box

ReLeaf began as engineered Bacillus subtilis sprayed onto crops. It ends as a sealed hollow-fibre bioreactor that keeps the cells inside and sends only the protectant to the plant. Nearly every step between those two designs traces back to a named conversation.

Farmers in Taiwan lose crops to heat, salt, drought, pests and weather they cannot predict, and many answer with more chemical input because it is the fastest thing to hand. We set out to predict stress and protect the plant before the damage lands, without adding to that load. The question we kept putting to other people was whether our answer was safe, useful and wanted. Plant biologists changed our model organism and our dosing. Two Stanford researchers and a bioprocess engineer changed how we contain the bacteria. An AIoT professor changed how the hardware, the model and the data relate to each other. A natural farmer in Tamsui changed what we built for farmers. We went back to seven of these stakeholders with results in hand, because a second conversation tests whether we understood the first. Iterating with the same stakeholders is what set apart Calgary 2019 and Lambert 2021, the two examples in the judging handbook we modelled our approach on [1][2][3]. Every entry below records what the person said, what we did about it, and where the result sits on this wiki, including the advice we could not act on yet.

Expert engagement: advisors who checked our work twice

We chose each expert for the part of the project they could find faults in, and went back to the ones whose advice we had acted on so they could judge whether we had acted on it well.

We picked experts by the module they could break

We could not ask one person everything, so we split the project into the six areas the map uses and looked for someone who could find the weak point in each. Academic advisors came first, while the design was still open to change. Industry and farm-technology contacts came later, once there was a device to criticise.

Six advisors saw the project twice, and the second meeting went deeper

A single interview tells you what someone thinks of an idea. A second interview tells you what they think of what you did with their advice. Six of those threads are below; CH Biotech and Green Media also came back to us, and their second appearance was at our forum, in section 5. Each tab below sets the first meeting, the work in between and the return meeting side by side. The line at the bottom of each tab says what the second meeting asked of us that the first could not.

Prof. Chang Jo-Shu (張嘉修): from keeping a culture alive to whether a farmer can run the machine

Partly acted on
Visit 1 · 18 June

He came to us to review the hollow-fibre membrane design.

  • Growth conditions that suit B. subtilis.
  • Break foam mechanically, with a part that physically bursts the bubbles.
  • Watch flow rate and transmembrane pressure: a rising pressure is how a caking membrane announces itself.
What we built in between
  • A perfusion reactor on a PES hollow-fibre cartridge: 0.2 µm pore, eight fibres, 150 cm².
  • Recirculation held 94 mL/min for a full run with no leak.
  • Shell-side medium plated in cycle 1 grew no colonies.
  • Not done: the transducers for transmembrane pressure are not fitted, and foaming has no design response yet.
Visit 2 · 4 September

We showed him the running reactor and the wet-lab data, and two problems: protectant, and possibly cells, appearing on the shell side with no driving force, and results we could not yet interpret.

  • Worked through how to isolate the leak and read the data.
  • Asked us to design for the person operating it: AIoT control and a replaceable bacterial capsule.

What went deeper: the first visit was about bioprocess conditions. The second was about a physical device with a leak, and about who has to operate it. The capsule idea now shapes the product design; the leak is the first open item in section 6.

Prof. Cheng Mei-Chun (鄭梅君): first she chose our plant, then she corrected how we grew it

Adopted in set 8
Visit 1 · 27 March
  • Switch the model to Arabidopsis thaliana. She gave us seed and growing guidance.
  • Hydroponics alone is not stable enough to trust. Start on agar plates, then move to soil.
  • Keep heat, salt and drought; drop osmotic stress.
  • Use moderate stress, and ROS staining to see it.
What we built in between
  • Eight experimental sets on plates, in water and in soil.
  • 250 mM salt bleached a whole set; we moved to a 200 mM ceiling, then down to 150.
  • Seedlings were moved at four days old.
Visit 2 · 28 August

Having seen our plates, she gave a set of rulings, written up on 2 September:

  • Use plants ten to twenty days old, and cap salt at 200 mM for that age.
  • Start treatment when the first true leaves are out, whatever the calendar says.
  • Blow seeds dry in the cabinet after stratification.
  • The gradual soil salt ramp we had started was too mild for a month-old plant.

What went deeper: in March she chose the model. In August she corrected how we ran it, using our own data. Experiment set 8 is the first to grow plants to ten days before treating them. Plants, section 3.3

Prof. Chen Wen-liang (陳文亮): the architecture, then the data, then how far to intervene

Three meetings
Meeting 1 · 10 April

“Design the hardware as the printer and the biofertilizer as the ink.”

  • Move from releasing bacteria into soil to a controlled bioreactor.
  • Define the inputs and outputs of the device clearly.
  • Plan beyond the local market; expect resistance on adoption, chemical competition and regulation.
Meeting 2 · 20 June
  • Connect the model to experiments, and refine each model with measured data.
  • Treat a ReLeaf database as the place experimental data accumulates.
  • Test salt concentration against treatment time; run the reactor longer; watch dissolved oxygen.
  • Visit Yes Health iFarm. We went on 11 August.
  • He took apart the auto-filling reservoir we proposed. We have not built it.
Meeting 3 · 5 September, as our forum speaker
  • Presented the engineering side of smart agriculture to farmers and the public.
  • Argued for a balance between intervention and the plant's own resilience: protect a plant according to whether it can keep growing under that stress.

What went deeper: April settled what to build, June settled how to prove it, and September asked when the device should act at all. The model now accepts wet-lab data and is set up to be proven wrong by it: see Bioreactor calculations.

Dr. Elizabeth Sattely: first she questioned our strategy, then she audited our evidence

Controls in progress
Visit 1 · 27 June
  • The proof of concept as planned was unlikely to convince anyone.
  • Validate the protectant on plants before building it into the full system.
  • Do one stress well, and look at other molecule types such as peptides.
  • Write down goals, milestones and measurable outcomes.
What we built in between
  • Direct plant assays of an applied protectant, including CH Biotech's 富肽2號 as experiment set 7.
  • A peptide design track: truncated protective motifs, screened in simulation.
  • A fixed protectant set: ACC deaminase, BoPep4 and LEA14.
Visit 2 · 12 September, with Prof. Drew Endy
  • Keep the engineered B. subtilis and the protectant it delivers clearly apart in every explanation.
  • Show that the bacteria make and secrete ACC deaminase, at protein and RNA level.
  • Add controls that rule out other explanations.
  • Say what each experiment was for and what its result shows.

What went deeper: the first meeting changed which experiment we ran. The second changed how we evidence and explain it, which is why our result pages now say what each result does and does not show. Results

Prof. Huang (黃介辰): timing and cloning online, then expression and secretion in his own lab

Changed a module
Visit 1 · 28 July, online
  • Biostimulants last longest when applied at the seedling stage.
  • Too much stress or protectant harms the plant: set thresholds and validate doses.
  • Run the reactor periodically when stress arrives, not continuously; combine sensors with weather forecasts.
  • Cloning: dense cell pellets and more DNA for electroporation; check ligation; include vector and no-plasmid controls.
What we changed in between
  • We asked him about recombination in Bacillus, the worry behind a terminator swap. His answer was that it is not the problem we thought.
  • Module 4's terminator went back to our original plan, B0015.
Visit 2 · 6 August, at NCHU
  • How to raise expression and secretion.
  • A stated reason for each salt and heat condition across agar, water and soil.
  • Shorter or modified peptides that keep activity, chosen using receptor–peptide interactions.

What went deeper: more secretion is not enough if the protein does not work. After the second visit we evaluate quantity, identity, solubility and function together. Engineering

Dr. Kyle: a weekly review that set the rules our plant data runs on

One rule missed
June
  • Stratify for two days, as standard.
  • Sterilise seed surfaces with chlorine gas to stop the contamination we kept seeing.
  • A contaminated plate contaminates its own data: exclude the whole plate.
July
  • After 250 mM bleached a set: 200 mM is the ceiling.
  • Never top up evaporated medium; the salt stays when the water leaves.
Through August
  • Three readouts, from set 4 onward.
  • Plot chlorophyll with and without fresh-weight normalisation. That is the only reason an artefact in our data became visible.

The rule we missed: we did not sterilise seed consistently, and the recorded cause of losing the first CH Biotech peptide trial is unsanitised seed. The plant page says so. Plants

Two early warnings about the kill switch made containment physical

In March our biosafety plan was a genetic kill switch on bacteria that would live in the field. Dr. Lin asked whether we needed one at all. In April Dr. Brophy set out the problem: kill switches can mutate away, so containment should rest on hardware, and the cells should sit in a closed vessel that only the protectant can leave. Prof. Chen had already pushed us from soil release toward a bioreactor the week before.

Those three conversations produced the current architecture: B. subtilis on the lumen side of a hollow-fibre membrane, green light switching it on through CcaS–CcaR, and protectant crossing into the shell side toward the plant. A kill switch is still on the table as a second layer, but the design no longer depends on one.

Industry and farm-technology visits tested the idea against a market

  • World Vegetable Center22 May

    Smallholders care about climate resilience. To be accepted by traditional and natural farmers the product must be shown to be environmentally safe, and GMO rules need research.

    Changed: we aim at a sustainable-farming label, not organic certification.

  • CH Biotech (正瀚生技)9 July

    Different crops and protectants need different application methods. Prove efficacy under heat or drought with several phenotypic measurements, and confirm Taiwanese regulation first.

    Changed: we compare root irrigation, foliar spray and drone spray, and cost per area. Their product became experiment set 7.

  • BIO Asia-Taiwan18 July

    Several booths told us the same thing: B. subtilis output depends on dissolved oxygen and active aeration, not containment alone.

    Changed: an oxygen sensor and feedback loop moved up the hardware list; GMO rules in Taiwan, the Philippines, Malaysia and the EU went on the research list.

  • Yes Health iFarm (源鮮智慧農場)11 August

    A fully controlled hydroponic farm: environmental monitoring, nutrients, lighting, aeration and circulation.

    Changed: we are reconsidering our 16/8 light cycle against 12/12, adding dissolved oxygen to the reactor parameters, and moving plants between systems less often.

  • Taiwan SMART Agriweek9 September

    Young farmers are the likely first users, and older farmers need a different case. GMO regulation will be hard. One device doing the work of several products is a price advantage, and government programmes may subsidise tools like ours.

    Changed: the target user and pricing case on Entrepreneurship.

  • Green Media (綠媒體)7 July

    Farmers, researchers and media hold different views on farming, and that difference decides how an event goes.

    Changed: our forum gained a closing discussion so every participant could speak. Green Media co-hosted the 5 September event.

Meeting records

Farmer engagement: growers named the problems, and one of them named a tool we then built

Experts told us how to grow a plant. Growers told us what a stressed crop costs them, what they distrust about technology, and how they look for help.

At the Farmer Expo, weather and water came before salt

On 16 May we interviewed smallholder stallholders at the 花博農民市集 farmers' market in Taipei. We asked what goes wrong in their fields before saying what we were building. Salt was not the first thing anyone mentioned. They named pests, pathogen infections, water shortage and unpredictable weather, and then the cost of everything you might spray to fix them. Several were wary of technology on the farm, and those concerns went into our business plan.

The conversation changed two things. We stopped treating abiotic stress as the whole problem and added biotic risk to the forecasting side of the project. And we learned that farmers needed a simpler way to find information, which became the LINE platform below.

A natural farmer in Tamsui warned us against giving plants too much

On 21 July we spent a day on Ms. Chen Hui-wen's (陳惠雯) Happy Farm in Tamsui. We harvested okra and luffa, crouched in the beds while she showed us what she reads in the soil, and ate what we had picked. She farms naturally, and she was sceptical on two counts: whether technology like ours works in a real field, and whether it helps at all.

“Sometimes giving too much is not good for a living thing. It can take away its ability to survive and thrive on its own.”

Ms. Chen Hui-wen, 21 July, translated from Mandarin

We took that seriously. It is the same point Prof. Huang made a week later about periodic operation, and that three speakers made at our forum. ReLeaf is meant to act when stress is forecast, at the seedling stage, and then stop. She also told us how hard it is for small growers to find and swap seed. Two days later the team prototyped a seed exchange, now part of our LINE account.

On the farms and at the markets

農友助手: a LINE account because farmers already use LINE

The Expo and the Tamsui visit showed us the same gap. Farm advice exists on government sites, research pages and social media, but it is scattered, and many smallholders are not comfortable searching for it. LINE is on almost every phone in Taiwan, so we built there: an official account called 農友助手 (Farmers' Assistant), with a six-button menu and nothing to install. Each button opens one tool.

Latest news

Short updates through the year: extreme weather and disaster information, farmers' markets, expos and workshops with dates and places. Each item says what it means for a farmer and what to do, for example checking irrigation, ventilation and heat-stress signs during a heat wave.

Product introduction

A one-page brochure for ReLeaf under the line “An on-site, on-demand bioreactor for smarter crop protection.” It explains what problem the device addresses and how it would sit on a farm, and leaves the biology and engineering to this wiki.

Crop stress alerts

Most of the farmers we met had lost crops to environmental stress. This dashboard splits Taiwan into northern, central and southern regions with one block per city. Each block shows a heat stress index on the left and a biological risk index on the right, and each value maps to a recommended action. Heat data follows the Central Weather Administration's heat-stress information [9]; biological risk follows the Animal and Plant Health Inspection Agency [10].

Crop assistant

A guided questionnaire: the crop, the region (or exact location), the affected part (leaf, root, flower), then weather, symptoms, duration, impact and pests. It returns likely causes, how each factor affects the crop, and prevention steps, with an AI helper for follow-up questions.

It does not replace an agronomist. Every answer points the user to official government resources before they act, with links underneath.

Farmer diary

Growers post up to five entries a day with the weather, crop photos, soil moisture and pest notes, and can ask and answer questions in a separate unlimited thread. Each post earns ten tokens toward LINE stickers.

Feedback

A form asking how satisfied users are, how useful each tool is, and what they want next. This is how the platform continues the feedback loop after the season ends.

Seed exchange, built on Ms. Chen's suggestion

Users post what seed they need and what they can offer, with variety, harvest year and where it was grown. The platform matches the two sides; once both confirm, they contact each other directly. A keyword auto-reply (for salt, heat, pests and more) and a sticker set of our reactor and protectants sit alongside.

Public engagement: a forum and six booths put farmers, experts and the public in one conversation

On 5 September we held a forum and a market event on the same day. More than fifty people attended the forum, and 62 visitors filled in our booth survey.

The forum gave farmers, experts and the public the same microphone

Most outreach puts a team at the front of a room. Green Media advised us in July that farmers, researchers and media see farming differently. So we built the forum, 田間對話 (Dialogue in the Field), in three parts and made the last part the longest.

  • 1 · Experts sharethree speakers

    Dr. Li, from natural farming: build an environment in which plants develop their own resilience, and stop trying to push growth or resistance into them directly.

    Prof. Chen, from smart agriculture: technology should make a better environment for growth, and protection should depend on whether the plant can keep growing under the stress.

    CH Biotech, from biostimulants: plant metabolism is a balance between growth and defence. Maximum protection is the wrong goal.

  • 2 · Our projectthe team

    We explained the bioreactor and the protectants as a farmer would use them: where the device sits, what it senses, and what reaches the plant.

  • 3 · Farmers respondopen floor

    Farmers described their own stress problems and responded to the speakers. Two questions were aimed at us:

    Does using the bioreactor affect organic certification?
    How many bioreactors would one farm need?

    Neither has a final answer yet. Both are open items in section 6.

The three speakers came from natural farming, engineering and industry, and they made the same argument: protect plants in proportion to need. Ms. Chen had said it in July and Prof. Huang in August. That is the design commitment we took from the day: ReLeaf is meant to act on forecast stress, periodically, and only up to a dose we have validated on plants.

Six booths turned plant stress into something visitors could hear, see and play

That afternoon we ran six booths at the Taipei Water Garden Organic Farmers' Market, alongside Green Media's Taiwanese coffee and cocoa event. Visitors collected a stamp at each booth by finishing the activity and answering a short question. Six stamps and an anonymous survey earned team merchandise and a place at the craft table.

  • Coffee infographic

    How climate change affects Taiwanese coffee and cocoa, and why local growers matter to the people who drink them.

  • ReLeaf project introduction

    Posters in plain language, photos of our Arabidopsis over time, and the bioreactor prototype on the table.

  • Data physicalization

    Recordings of plants under drought stress played through headphones [8], and an LED map linking heat stress to rice yield by region.

    Method: Data Physicalization

  • Ring toss

    Bottles labelled with stresses, colour-coded rings for protectants. Match the right ring to the right stress: biotic and abiotic stress need different answers.

  • Online game

    Stresses fall on a virtual crop; shoot the matching protectant before they land.

  • DIY arts and crafts

    Build a fidget clicker, a stress-relief toy, and decorate it with stickers of our mascot.

The data physicalization booth came from a talk on the method that one of us heard at the 2025 Grand Jamboree. Data you can touch or hear reaches people who skip a chart [7]. Hearing that plants emit sound when they dry out was the detail visitors remembered most.

The booths

Knowledge: two in three visitors could explain the device afterwards

We built the booth survey on the same KAP structure [5]. Of 62 respondents, 51.6% picked the region our map marked as the most stressed. Self-rated understanding was highest for the ReLeaf device itself.

Attitude: 98% said the day raised their attention to climate impact on Taiwanese farming

What visitors wrote in their own words

The open question asked for one thing each visitor learned or found most impressive. Five themes came back: that plants make sound under stress, clicking when short of water; that ReLeaf protects crops with bacteria kept inside a device without harming soil; that climate change is already hitting Taiwanese coffee in Yunlin and Yilan; that games made the concepts stick; and the energy of the students at each booth.

The practice (P) questions of the booth survey are not analysed yet.

Conclusion: how this work answers the six IHP judging questions

The judging handbook asks six questions about Integrated Human Practices [1]. Each answer below points to evidence on this page or elsewhere on the wiki.

01

Integrated throughout the projectHow well was Human Practices integrated throughout the project?

Outside advice reached all six parts of the project, from March to September, and we can name the change in each:

  • Cloning: Prof. Huang, module 4 terminator back to B0015.
  • Bioreactor: Dr. Brophy and Prof. Chang, physical containment and the hollow-fibre cartridge.
  • Plant model: Prof. Cheng and Dr. Kyle, Arabidopsis, plate-to-soil, 10-day plants, 150 mM.
  • Modelling: Prof. Chen, a model that takes wet-lab data back in.
  • Protectant: Dr. Sattely and CH Biotech, test purified protectant on plants first; peptides.
  • Deployment: Ms. Chen, the forum and Agriweek, the seed exchange, the LINE platform and periodic operation.
02

Thoughtful, with context and prior workHow thoughtfully was it implemented? Did they explain context, rationale and prior work?

We chose each expert for the module they could find faults in (section 3.1), and we went back to seven stakeholders because Calgary 2019 showed that returning to the same people produces a better design than one round of feedback [2]. We follow the anticipate, reflect, engage and act framing of responsible innovation [4], measured our outreach with KAP surveys [5][6], and took data physicalization from its research literature [7]. Where advisors disagreed, section 3.3 records whose advice we followed and why.

03

Responsible and good for the worldDid Human Practices help create a project that is responsible and good for the world?

The biggest safety decision on the project came from outside it. After Dr. Lin and Dr. Brophy questioned the kill switch, we stopped planning to put engineered bacteria in soil. They now stay behind a 0.2 µm PES membrane, a pore smaller than a B. subtilis cell, and shell-side medium plated in cycle 1 grew no colonies. We report the limits of that result: it is one plating with its replicate count unrecorded, the pore also passes vesicles and debris, Prof. Chang reviewed a shell-side leak with us on 4 September, and the second containment layer does not exist yet. The Safety page therefore describes contained use as a classification we are pursuing.

Farmers shaped the other half. The natural-farming view from Ms. Chen, Dr. Li, Prof. Chen and CH Biotech made proportionate protection a design rule. The crop assistant sends users to official sources before they act.

04

Diverse stakeholder viewsHow well did it incorporate different stakeholder views?

Academic researchers in Taiwan and at Stanford; two biostimulant and farm-technology companies; an international agricultural NGO; a hydroponic farm; a media organisation; conventional, organic and natural farmers; and more than fifty members of the public. The forum put farmers, researchers and the public in one room and gave the farmers the last and longest part of the programme. We recorded disagreements between advisors as well as agreements.

05

Documented so others can build on itTo what extent is the work documented so others can build upon it?

Every engagement on the map has a date, the advice, the before and after, and a link to the page where the change shows up. The survey questions are printed in full above, per audience. The LINE platform's structure is set out tool by tool so another team can rebuild it for its own farmers. Our own failures to follow advice are recorded too, such as unsterilised seed.

06

An inspiring exampleHow inspiring an example is it to others?

Most teams interview a stakeholder once. We went back to seven, one of them three times, and each second meeting asked more of us than the first. A high-school team also ran a forum where farmers questioned scientists directly, and a booth where visitors heard a drought-stressed plant. The practice we would pass on is simple: go back to the people who advised you and show them what you did.
