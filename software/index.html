Why the reactor has to decide

A production batch in the field has no process engineer standing beside it. The decisions a bioprocess technician would make have to be made by the unit itself, or deferred to a person who is told exactly what to approve.

BR-01 is a single unit operation: a hollow-fibre perfusion bioreactor in which engineered Bacillus subtilis is held in the fibre lumen, medium circulates through a peristaltic loop, and the secreted plant protectant crosses the 0.2 µm membrane into the permeate. Upstream of it is inoculum preparation; downstream is the permeate that goes to the crop. Production is switched on by green light through the CcaS/CcaR two-component system, so the single most consequential decision in a batch is when to induce.

Induce too early and the culture spends its resources on protectant before it has the biomass to make much of it; induce too late and the cells are already in decline. In a stirred tank in a pilot plant that call is made by a person reading an off-line OD600 sample and a batch sheet. BR-01 is meant to run for days at ambient temperature beside a field, where nobody will take the sample. At 22 °C one batch of our strain took about 18 days to pass through lag, growth and plateau, so a wrong call costs weeks, not hours.

The reactor also has to tell two situations apart that look the same on a single channel: the culture changed (a process event, which may call for action) and the instrument changed (a sensor fault, which calls for holding still). A controller that cannot make that distinction will act on a broken sensor with the same confidence as on a real growth phase. That is the problem this page is built around, and the reason the twin carries a trust layer and a graded set of permissions rather than a single control law.

What the reactor can sense

The process state vector, variable by variable, with how each value is obtained. Three are measured on line and several are derived; pH, temperature and dissolved oxygen, which a production bioreactor would normally carry, are absent.

Every value that enters the twin is tagged by where it comes from, following the process analytical technology (PAT) idea that a number is only as good as its route from the process [5, 8]. measured means an instrument on the loop reads it. derived means the software computes it from measured values and a model, as a soft sensor. commanded is an actuator setpoint the software knows because it sent it. absent means nothing on BR-01 reads it today. The instruments and rates below are those of the rig software actually running the reactor, the team's Bioreactor v0.3 app on the Arduino Uno Q.

Why dissolved oxygen is absent, and what that costs

A galvanic or optical DO probe needs a calibration and membrane upkeep that we could not make reliable at a 2 L, low-flow scale inside the season, and an unreliable DO reading is worse than none because a controller will trust it. The consequence is stated rather than hidden: without DO, the volumetric oxygen transfer coefficient \(k_La\) stays a modelled quantity. The transport calculation gives 45.7 h−1 against the 60 h−1 the culture is estimated to need, and that gap can be neither confirmed nor ruled out until oxygen is measured.

Critical process parameters and quality attributes

In the language of ICH Q8 [7], a critical quality attribute (CQA) is a property of the product that must stay within a limit, and a critical process parameter (CPP) is a process setting whose variation moves a CQA. The gating rule

The ladder is only useful with a rule for climbing down it. The trust layer (section 5) scores two independent axes every 10 minutes: is the measurement trustworthy and is the culture behaving as expected. Their combination sets a ceiling on autonomy, and the ceiling falls one rung at a time, 2 → 1 → 0 → hold, as confidence drops.

Sensor axisProcess axisCallAutonomy ceiling
NominalNominalBatch on trackTier 2
NominalEventProcess event: the change in OD is real, tell the operatorTier 1; routing and cleaning wait for a person
FaultAnySensor fault: do not act on the faulted channelTier 0 on actions that do not depend on it; everything keyed to OD holds
Link lost or two channels faultedState unknowableHold: last safe state, operator paged

The rule comes from our own long run. On that batch a single-channel threshold would have fired on the instrument failure and stayed silent on the real process event. Tier 3 sits outside the rule altogether: however confident the twin is, it only recommends.

The twin

A physical asset, a virtual asset and automated dataflow between them [1]. Today data flow automatically from the reactor into the model; decisions return through a person. In the maturity terms of the literature that is a digital shadow, not yet a closed-loop twin [2, 3].

What is mechanistic, what is learned, and why the split

The virtual layer is a hybrid model. Wherever physics or kinetics give a law with measurable parameters, that law is used, because it keeps its units and can be carried to a condition the data never covered. A learned component is added only on top, to absorb what the mechanism misses, and it is kept small because it will not extrapolate. With one long batch on record, a purely data-driven model would learn that batch and nothing else.

Growth in the lumen

\[ \frac{dX}{dt} = \mu\,X \]\[ \mu = \mu_{\max}\left(1 - \frac{X}{X_{\max}}\right) \]

\(X\) is biomass as OD600. Cells are retained by the membrane, so there is no washout term; only soluble species leave with the permeate at dilution rate \(D\). Refit at 37 °C: \(\mu_{\max} = 1.31\) h−1, \(X_{\max} = 1.54\). The 22 °C long run is better described by two exponential phases, 0.0318 and 0.0164 h−1, with a carrying capacity of 1.456 (values from the Math Model parameter table).

\[ \frac{dP}{dt} = q_P\,X - D\,P \]

Product in the circulating volume. The structure is standard; \(q_P\) is unknown because no titer has been measured, so this line predicts nothing yet.

Biomass from light: Beer-Lambert with a reference beam

Each sensor on our photometer returns an illuminance value in lux. The sample sensor reports light transmitted through the culture; the reference sensor reports light diverted before the sample. Reported OD600 is derived from the sample-to-reference ratio, which normalizes each reading against the LED light source intensity and thereby corrects for drift in the LED output over the course of a multi-week long run if it degrades.

The step by step process for calculating OD is first calculating K as Sample Sensor (Blank through cuvette and medium no bacteria)/Reference Sensor (Blank not through cuvette or medium).

Once we capture the value of K at the start of our live reading, we can use the following formula to determine every live reading.

OD600 = -log(Sample Sensor (live reading)/(K*Reference Sensor (live reading)))

\[ K = \frac{S_\mathrm{blank}}{R_\mathrm{blank}} \]\[ \mathrm{OD}_{600} = -\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{S}{K\,R}\right) \]

The same formula in symbols. In the firmware \(S\) and \(R\) are dark-subtracted (a 4-reading LED-off frame before each burst), and the result is multiplied by a path-length factor of 5 to express the 2 mm cuvette as the 1 cm path a benchtop OD600 uses. Beer-Lambert, \(A = \varepsilon\,l\,c\), is what makes absorbance linear in cell density, and it holds only while single scattering dominates, which is why linearity ends near OD 1.2.

Induction: CcaS/CcaR kinetics

\[ y_{ss}(I) = y_0 + \Delta y\,\frac{I^{\,n}}{I^{\,n} + K_{1/2}^{\,n}} \]\[ \frac{dy}{dt} = k\,\big(y_{ss}(I) - y\big), \qquad k = \frac{\ln 2}{t_{1/2}} \]

\(I\) is green photon flux, \(K_{1/2} = 4.66\) µmol m−2 s−1, \(n = 1.88\), \(t_{1/2,\mathrm{on}} = 105.1\) min and \(t_{1/2,\mathrm{off}} = 74.97\) min, all from Castillo-Hair et al. [6] and not yet measured in our hands. The twin uses this first-order reduction of their delayed third-order model; its job is to tell the scheduler that induction takes hours to take effect, not to predict a titer.

The hydraulic loop: Hagen-Poiseuille and Darcy

\[ \Delta P_\mathrm{HP} = \frac{8\,\mu_w\,L}{\pi\,r^4}\,\frac{Q}{N} \]\[ \dot\gamma_w = \frac{4\,Q/N}{\pi\,r^3}, \qquad \tau_w = \frac{\Delta P_\mathrm{net}\,r}{2L} \]

Laminar flow in \(N\) fibres of radius \(r\) and length \(L\), with water viscosity \(\mu_w\). At 263 mL/min, 22 °C and 11 open fibres this gives 0.093 bar; the module we measured drops 0.096 bar net, 1.03 times the prediction, so all 11 fibres are open. The wall shear rate there is about 4,100 s−1; the software warns above 6,000 s−1. Inverting the same law on a live \(\Delta P\) gives the apparent number of open fibres, \(N_\mathrm{eff}\), which falls if fibres block.

\[ \Delta P = aQ + bQ^2 \]\[ J = \frac{\mathrm{TMP}}{\mu\,(R_m + R_f)} \]\[ \frac{R}{R_0} = \frac{\Delta P_\mathrm{net}/Q}{(\Delta P_\mathrm{net}/Q)_{0}} \]

The measured module curve (\(a = 2.427\times10^{-4}\) bar min mL−1, \(b = 4.605\times10^{-7}\) bar min2 mL−2), Darcy's law for permeate flux \(J\) through membrane and fouling resistances in series, and the fouling index against the clean baseline. The software warns at \(R > 2R_0\) and raises a critical alarm at \(\Delta P > 2.5\,\Delta P_0\).

The learned part: a Gaussian-process residual on μ

\[ \mu(t) = \mu_\mathrm{mech}(X, T) + \delta(t) \]\[ \delta \sim \mathcal{GP}\!\left(0,\; \sigma^2 \exp\!\left(-\frac{(t-t')^2}{2\ell^2}\right)\right) \]

The mechanistic rate is corrected by a Gaussian process [10] that learns only the residual. Its predictive variance is as useful as its mean: when it widens, the batch has left the conditions the twin has seen, and the trust layer lowers the autonomy ceiling. Status: designed, not trained, because a residual needs several batches and we have one long one. The only GP fitted so far is on the photometer calibration, where it located the linear range on its own (section 5).

One board, two computers

Our finalized Photometer doesn’t use a breadboard but instead uses a soldered PCB connected to an Arduino Uno Q. The Arduino Uno Q is a mini-computer in and of itself, meaning that we can run the code purely by just providing a power source to it and do not need any computer constantly plugged into it. This allows us to move one step farther into creating a cheap and accessible bioreactor but also gives us the luxury of reducing one of the other failure points of our photometer sensing (computer-arduino wire unplug).

With the physical system established, we integrated the photometer with a live dashboard. The system records the sample and reference signals, calculates their ratio, reports OD600, and continuously logs measurements. We also developed a bubble-rejection system so that brief disturbances caused by bubbles would not automatically become biological data.

sketch.ino, Arduino Q

This is the program that runs on the microcontroller chip on the board, focusing on physical measurement. Every measurement cycle it:

  • turns the LED off and takes a few dark readings from both light sensors to know what the ambient/stray light looks like;
  • turns the LED on and lets it settle for a fraction of a second;
  • takes a burst of about 16 rapid readings from both sensors while the LED is lit;
  • turns the LED back off immediately;
  • runs that burst of readings through a filtering algorithm that throws out anything that looks like a bubble passed through and distorted a reading.

After filtering, it subtracts the earlier dark reading baseline, compares the sample sensor’s light level to the reference sensor’s light level, and turns that ratio into an OD600 number using the stored blank calibration point. It also handles small housekeeping tasks like restarting a sensor automatically if it somehow goes unresponsive, and it exposes a menu of functions (gets latest OD600 and raw readings, triggers an extra reading, calibrates new blank, etc.) that the other program is allowed to call.

main.py, Python

This runs on the second, separate computer that’s built into the same board: the Linux side. It’s a completely different environment from sketch.ino with its operating system, ability to run any Python library.

Its job focuses entirely on handling and presenting the data that sketch.ino produces while never touching the sensors directly. On a repeating timer (every 30 seconds by default), it demands the latest reading from sketch.ino and gets back the OD600 value, raw sensor numbers, and a validity flag. It writes all that as one row into a CSV file that keeps growing and updating for as long as the experiment runs, and it’s written so that if the board or the app restarts partway through a multi-day run, it picks up from the same CSV file back up instead of starting over and losing the timeline. On top of that, it runs a small web server with a handful of specific endpoints: one that hands back all logged data points, one that hands back just the latest snapshot, one that lets someone trigger a manual blank calibration, one that lets someone force an extra reading in that instance, one that lets someone wipe the current run and start fresh, and so on.

index.html (claude.ai)

This one is different in kind from the other two in that it doesn’t run on the board. It’s a separate webpage that gets loaded into a normal web browser on whatever’s connected to the same network as the board when you type in its address.

Its job is purely visual and interactive. Every 5 seconds, it requests the latest data from the main.py web server and redraws itself: it updates a set of number cards (current OD600, how long the run has been going, sample and reference light readings, their ratio, bubble-rejection percentage), and it redraws a line chart showing the whole growth curve so far. It also turns button clicks into requests sent back to main.py: clicking “Measure Now” sends a request telling main.py to ask sketch.ino for an extra reading right now; clicking “Calibrate Blank” pops up a confirmation and then sends a request to set the current reading as the new blank, or zero-point; clicking “Reset Run” downloads a backup copy of the current CSV first, then tells main.py to start a fresh log. The webpage itself never interacts directly with sketch.ino and never touches the light sensors. It only ever communicates through main.py.

How the three tie together

The three codes work as a one-way chain, with whom they’re connected to being limited. sketch.ino sits closest to the hardware and only knows about the LED and the two sensors: it doesn’t know or care that a website exists. main.py sits in the middle: it’s the only one allowed to talk to sketch.ino, and it’s also the only one that keeps a permanent record, the CSV file, of everything that’s happened. index.html sits furthest out, in the browser, and is the only piece a person actually sees or interacts with, but it’s not allowed to talk to sketch.ino at all. Every single thing it shows or triggers has to go through main.py first. So the flow for one measurement is always: sketch.ino measures with light sensors → main.py asks for and logs the result → index.html asks main.py what the result was and displays it. And the flow for a button click runs the same chain backwards: you click a button in index.html → that sends a request to main.py → main.py relays the command to sketch.ino → sketch.ino does the real work and reports back.

From the photometer app to the reactor app

The same three-part chain now runs the whole reactor. Bioreactor v0.3 merges the photometer, the flow sensor, the three pressure channels and the pump driver into one App Lab application. Its timing budget:

We found the latency problem on the rig. In the first merged build an OD600 measurement blocked the microcontroller loop for about 2.7 s, and up to 8 s when a light sensor failed to answer. For those seconds the 100 Hz pressure window went blind, the TMP excursion check missed its peaks, and a stop command from the dashboard waited in a queue. The 1 September 2026 build split the measurement into a state machine that reads one sensor value per loop pass, cut the sensor read timeout from 200 to 3 ms, and moved a nested RPC call out of a callback. The self-check that reports pressure samples per second (it should read close to 100) exists so that the same fault shows up in the data if it comes back.

Calibration values live in microcontroller RAM and vanish on a reboot, which in the middle of a batch cannot be redone: the flow cell cannot be emptied for a new blank with culture in it. The Linux side therefore writes every blank, pressure zero and baseline to a file with its source, detects an MCU reboot from its uptime, pushes the saved values back, and keeps a warning on screen until a fresh calibration replaces them. What does not survive yet is the batch record itself (section 9), and the pump holds its last PWM if the Linux side dies; the watchdog that would stop it exists only in the prototype firmware (section 11).

The derived state, alarms and steady-state test run on the board. The trust layer and the model fitting still run off-board in Python on a laptop, and reach the board as constants. Moving the trust-layer scoring onto the Linux side is small (it needs numpy and a 2 h buffer) and is the next software step.

The trust layer

Before the twin acts on a reading it asks two separate questions: is the measurement trustworthy, and is the culture behaving as expected? One long batch shows why the two must be kept apart.

Two kinds of fault

A process event is a real change in the culture: the OD falls because the cells did something. A sensor fault is a change in the instrument: the OD moves because the photometer did. On the OD channel alone they can look identical. The photometer's reference beam separates them, because it never passes through the culture: a reference that holds steady while OD moves says the change is biological, and a reference that moves says the instrument is involved.

The layer scores each axis with its own features over 2 h windows. The sensor axis uses the median and spread of the reference channel and the scatter of OD around its trend. The process axis uses the specific growth rate over 5 h and 20 h. Each axis is trained on the first 120 h of the run only, with its alarm limit fixed in advance at the 99.5th percentile of the training score, and then scored on everything after, which it never saw.

The sensor alarm came at 216.3 h. The OD channel became unusable, defined as the first sustained crossing of three times its healthy scatter, at 243.0 h. That is 26.7 h of warning in which a controller would have known to stop trusting OD. A simple rule of the kind most monitoring software uses, an alarm on any jump of more than 0.05 OD between readings, fired on 23% of the instrument event and on 0.8% of the process event, and could not say which was which.

The false stationary phase at 103 h

The same batch argues against the obvious closed loop. Two induction policies applied to it after the fact, a fixed clock at 100 h and a live trigger on μ falling below 0.006 h−1, both fire at about 100 to 103 h, at OD around 0.90. The culture then falls to 0.629, grows for another hundred hours, and settles at a plateau of 1.473. Both policies induce at 61% of final biomass. The reference channel reads 26.67 to 26.69 lux through that window, so it is not a sensor artefact. Here the threshold loop does no better than the fixed recipe. This is why the state call has to be made by a model of the growth phase, and why arm B of the experiment in section 6 is expected to fail.

Calibration with interval coverage

The photometer's calibration against a BioDrop (45 paired TiO₂ points, 23 August 2026) was fitted two ways. The linear fit in use wins inside its own range (RMSE 0.0235 OD against 0.0254 for a Gaussian process), and is kept. Extrapolated past OD 1.2, as happens in a batch nobody is watching, its error rises to 0.1715; the GP holds 0.067. The GP also found its own validity ceiling at OD 1.55 without being told where the hand-drawn limit was. Its 95% predictive band covered 88.9% of held-out points, short of the 95% it claims, so its intervals are too narrow.

What this evidence is, and is not

n = 1. One batch, one process event and one instrument event. This is one counterexample and one worked demonstration of the method, not an error rate. A detection rate needs faults made on purpose, several of each kind, on batches the layer has not seen. The medium, inoculum and compartment of this batch were not recorded.

Method detailsFeatures, detectors, thresholds and false-alarm rates.

Features per 2 h window. Sensor axis: reference-channel median, reference-channel SD, SD of OD residuals around a local fit. Process axis: μ over 20 h and over 5 h. Two detectors per axis were compared, principal component analysis and a small autoencoder, each thresholded at the 99.5th percentile of its training score. The sensor alarm times were 216.7 h (PCA) and 216.3 h (autoencoder). False-alarm rate on the unseen quiet window 160 to 220 h: 5.6% (PCA) and 6.1% (autoencoder) on the sensor axis. The 2 × 2 call table is a decision rule, not a fitted classifier: with two events there is nothing to fit. Code: src/sentinel.py in the team's model repository.

Closing the loop: the experiment

The claim that a twin improves a batch is tested the way a process change would be: against the current recipe, in replicate batches, with the failure conditions written down first.

The decision under test is induction timing. Three arms run the same strain, medium and light dose and differ only in who decides when the green light goes on.

ArmInduction ruleExpectation, written before the data
A Fixed recipeGreen light on at a fixed time after inoculation, chosen from the training batchesThe baseline. Good when batches are alike, poor when lag varies.
B Threshold triggerGreen light on when μ over 5 h falls below 0.006 h−1Expected to fail: it fires on false stationary phases, as it did at 103 h. Included so the twin is compared with the simple controller, not only with a clock.
C Twin-gatedGreen light on when the growth model calls stationary onset and the trust layer reports both axes nominalLower induction-time error and lower titer variance than A.

Endpoints. Primary: product per batch at harvest, reported as the difference between arms with a 95% confidence interval, not a p-value. Secondary: induction time relative to the stationary onset read from the finished curve, and the spread of induction time across batches.

Power. With a batch-to-batch coefficient of variation near 15%, a two-sample comparison at α = 0.05 and power 0.8 resolves only differences above about 45% with three batches per arm, 36% with four and 30% with five. The plan is n ≥ 4 per arm, five if the schedule allows.

Where. One BR-01 batch at 22 °C takes about 18 days, so twelve or more batches cannot run on it this season. The arms run in parallel small vessels at 37 °C, where a batch takes about 12 hours, and one or two batches at 22 °C test whether the fitted parameters transfer.

Pre-registered: what would falsify the claim
  • The 95% interval of (C − A) product includes zero: the twin does not improve the batch.
  • On held-out batches the state call misses stationary onset by more than ±2 h, or replayed on the 434 h batch it fires at 103 h.
  • Arm B does as well as arm C: the model adds nothing over a threshold.
Define quality before the results

Timing and quantity are measurable; "quality" is not, until it is defined as either purity of the secreted protectant or its activity in a plant assay. The choice decides the CQA and the release test. It also meets the readout bottleneck: an anti-His Western blot per batch is too slow for twelve or more batches, so the loop is first closed on a fluorescent reporter, which is cloning work and the most likely reason this experiment does not finish in the season.

The batch database and the learning loop

Every batch leaves a record in one schema, and every new batch is scored before it is learned from. Across 30 to 40 batches this demonstrates the loop; it does not mature it.

The database is five tables. The first two already exist as files; the other three are written by hand today and need to move into the record.

TableOne row perHoldsToday
batchBatchStrain, construct, medium, inoculum OD and volume, compartment, temperature, operator, firmware and constants versions, outcomeby hand
recordSampling windowThe 41-column row of section 4automatic
eventEventProcess events, deviations and interventions (reservoir opened, tube reseated, browser restarted), each with a wall-clock time and who did itnot kept
calibrationCalibration actBlank, pressure zero, baseline, their values and source (measured, restored, manual)automatic
assayOff-line sampleBioDrop OD, titer, activity, sterility, linked to a window by timenot kept

The learning loop is simple: a new batch is first scored by the frozen models (prediction error, state-call error, trust-layer false alarms), and only then added to the training set and the models refitted. A future team extends the database by adding columns, never by renaming them, and by writing a new constants version rather than editing an old one. The database is what another team can reuse; a learning result drawn from a few dozen batches would be a weak claim.

Toward a GMP-like process

BR-01 is a research rig, not a licensed manufacturing process. Asking what GMP would demand of it is still the quickest way to see where the twin is weak.

The table sets the rig beside the elements a regulated biomanufacturing process would need for its computerised system and its records [11, 12]. It is a gap analysis, not a claim of compliance.

Limits

  • No dissolved oxygen. \(k_La\) stays modelled, and the 45.7 against 60 h−1 question stays open.
  • n = 1 on the long run. Every statement about the trust layer rests on one batch with two events.
  • 37 °C fits, 22 °C operation. The growth parameters that are fast to fit come from 37 °C; the field unit runs near 22 °C. Whether they transfer is a test, not an assumption.
  • Beer-Lambert at scale. The photometer is linear only to about OD 1.2 in its 2 mm cell, and the induction light will be attenuated by a dense culture in a way nothing on the rig measures.
  • The 120 to 160 h event is unexplained. The layer called it correctly as biological; nobody knows what the culture did.
  • Flow sensor not verified, and TMP below the resolution of the pressure transducers.
  • The loop is not closed. Every action on the culture still goes through a person.

One lead on the unexplained event comes from the team's own draft of the photometer's development:

During the run, the photometer even captured a sudden change caused by a recirculation-pump failure, demonstrating that continuous monitoring could reveal problems that would otherwise occur between manual measurements.

Whether that pump failure is the 120 to 160 h event is not on record. If it is, the event was a process event with a mechanical cause, and it belongs in the event table of the next batch rather than in a footnote.

Safety

Graded autonomy is only credible if the hard limits are enforced below the software that makes decisions, independently of it.

The rule is that interlocks live on the microcontroller and override any command, so that no fault in the Linux side, the browser or a model can drive the process past a limit. On the rig today that rule is only partly met:

ProtectionEnforced byStatus
Pump stall guard: a PWM of 1 to 99, which only makes the pump hum and heat, is forced to zeroMCUon the rig
Stop buttonMCU, on commandon the rig
TMP above 0.5 bar, or a TMP peak inside a pulsation cycle above 0.5 bar (fibre collapse risk)Linux side, alarm onlyalarm, no action
ΔP above 2.5 times baseline; shear rate above 6,000 s−1; fast TMP riseLinux side, alarm onlyalarm, no action
No command for 5 s stops the loop pump; TMP above 0.50 bar stops it; any lumen valve shut stops itMCUprototype firmware only

The rig code says of its own alarms that they detect and broadcast but are not yet wired to slow or stop the pump. Moving the TMP ceiling and the command watchdog from the prototype firmware into the rig firmware is the precondition for any tier above 0.

Why antibiotic dosing is recommend-only

Antibiotic selection keeps a plasmid in a culture. In a unit that sits beside a field and releases permeate onto a crop, dosing antibiotic is a release of antibiotic and a selection pressure for resistance in the environment, and it cannot be undone. The twin may recommend it, for example when the process axis suggests plasmid loss, but a person approves. The better answer is to need no antibiotic in production at all: integrating the expression cassette into the B. subtilis chromosome keeps it without selection, at the cost of a lower copy number and therefore possibly a lower titer. That trade is for the wet lab to measure.

Contribution

Four things another team can lift without our reactor.

  1. The batch-record schema: the 41-column window row and the five-table database, with provenance columns built in.
  2. The trust-layer code: two independent axes, trained on the healthy start of a run and thresholded before scoring, with the 434 h batch as a test case.
  3. The calibration method: a dual-beam in-line photometer calibrated against known TiO₂ doses so that both instruments are scored against truth, plus the interval-coverage check.
  4. The tier ladder: actions ordered by reversibility, with an autonomy ceiling set by measurement confidence and a human-approval tier that stays manual at any confidence.
