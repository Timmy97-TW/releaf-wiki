Digital Twin

Abstract

A reactor that runs for weeks with nobody beside it is worth only as much as its own account of itself, so every reading on this page is built to show when it has stopped being true.

BR-01 ran 434 hours unbroken at field temperature, 2,671 readings with no sample drawn, where the two June prototypes had been sampled by hand for 27 and 7 hours. Nothing in that record rests on a single instrument: a reference beam that never sees the culture separates lamp drift from growth, wall shear is computed twice from two different sensors, and the photometer was calibrated against a known TiO₂ dose rather than against another instrument. That is what gave 26.7 hours of warning before the OD channel failed, showed that a forty-hour collapse mid-run was the culture and not the optics, and caught the twin's own fouling indicator disagreeing with itself by 20%. The loop is not closed, and almost every result rests on a single run.

Best Measurement

A bioreactor in a field cannot be sampled by hand, so measuring a batch nobody is standing next to means knowing, from the data alone, when the measurement has stopped being true. Five numbers, each linking to the result behind it.

  1. Continuity. 434 hours of in-line OD600 with no sample drawn, where the June prototypes were sampled by hand for 27 and 7 hours (every run).
  2. Self-diagnosis. A reference beam that never sees the culture gave 26.7 h of warning before the OD channel became unusable.
  3. A second route to the same quantity. Wall shear from pressure and from flow agreed to 2.8%, which is what lets a later 20% disagreement read as a fault rather than noise.
  4. Forecast. Recursive state estimation beats persistence by 41% at 2 h ahead, under a rule fixed before the data were examined.
  5. Transfer between scales. A prior fitted on a 96-well plate predicts 24 h biomass to within 7%; the same data without it miss by 29 to 68%.

Introduction

What is a digital twin?

There is not always a clear boundary between a digital model, a digital shadow and a digital twin. A digital model represents a physical system but is updated manually. A digital shadow automatically receives real-time data from the physical system, but any changes back to the system still require human input. A digital twin closes the loop: information flows automatically from the physical system to the model and back again. The key distinction is how information moves, and whether that flow happens automatically.

Our bioreactor is meant to run for weeks beside farmland, far from laboratories where results can be analysed precisely. Because no one will always be there to take samples or interpret every reading, the digital twin acts as the decision-making “mind” of the system.

The first challenge of this remote workflow is the lack of manual sampling. In a lab, someone draws culture and checks it by hand. In the field, the reactor's own instruments are the only record of the batch, so they have to be checked continuously. Furthermore, machines wear down, with sensors drifting over days. On the 434-hour run, the OD channel eventually failed. The trust layer of the digital twin flagged the reference beam 26.7 hours before the OD readings became completely unusable.

Additionally, the digital twin makes the decision of whether to switch on the green light to activate protectant production, and more specifically, which protectant to release and produce, in what concentration and amount. Drawing from a system of local-scale sensing and regional-scale data, the digital twin weighs what the field needs against what the reactor can deliver. The regional side is the field weather forecast and the crop-stress risk on the Weather tab, together with the maps from Geospatial Analysis. They show when and where plants are likely to come under stress. The local side is BR-01's own instruments. They report how much biomass is in the loop, whether it is still growing, whether the membrane is still passing permeate, and whether those readings can be trusted at all (R3). Induction makes sense when the field is heading into stress and the culture is in a state to respond. The forecast (R4) and the growth prior (R5) then say whether the batch will be ready in time. Once the ACC deaminase activity assay puts a number on the product, the same framework can estimate how long induction must run to reach a target amount.

Leaf wetness is not itself a stress for rice, which sheds water as beads; it matters because it is the condition a fungus needs to infect. The interface applies the BLASTAM rule for leaf blast [7]: a wet period counts when the leaf stays wet 10 hours at 22–25 °C, 11 at 20–21 °C, rising to 17 at 15 °C [8], with none below 15 or above 25, and the preceding five days averaging 20–25 °C. Past hours come from the sensor, hours ahead from forecast humidity above 90% [9]. A met period means the weather allowed infection, not that lesions will appear. The sensor is still on a bench with a default threshold, the rule has never been scored against Taiwanese disease records, and ACC deaminase has not been tested against blast — so blast risk is shown to the grower and switches nothing on its own.

R1 · Module pressure drop calibrated

16 August 2026, water at 22 °C, eight pump settings, one point each, measured with the module fitted and with a jumper in its place. Both fit \(\Delta P = aQ + bQ^2\) through the origin: module R² 0.9954, residual SD 10.1 mbar; jumper R² 0.9950, SD 5.7 mbar. At 263 mL/min the net module drop is 0.096 bar, 1.03 times the Hagen–Poiseuille prediction for 11 open fibres — the agreement is what justifies using the model as a soft sensor for flow. No point was held out.

The same eight points fix the operating point the rig has run at since. 263 mL/min gives 4064 s⁻¹ of wall shear, mid-way through the module's calibrated 2000–6000 band, Re 531 well inside laminar flow, and about 11 effective fibres against a nominal 11, so none are blocked. Only one number has no margin: axial non-uniformity is 28% where 20% counts as uniform. 263 was chosen to hold it there, since lowering the flow would lower wall shear with it.

Wall shear is also where two instruments meet. It can be computed from the pressure drop or from the flow rate, and here the two routes agree to 2.8% — a pressure sensor and a flow sensor confirming each other rather than each being believed alone. That margin is the reference against which the 20% disagreement in October is a fault worth chasing.

To reconcile before the freeze

The 25 August progress report quotes 0.084 bar and 0.90–0.96× theory for the same measurement, against the 0.096 bar and 1.03× above. The two write-ups come from one spreadsheet and have to be reconciled before the freeze; the figures here are the later analysis.

R2 · Photometer and BioDrop calibration against TiO₂ doses calibrated

On 23 August 2026, the experiment gave 45 paired points between our in-line photometer and the BioDrop spectrophotometer from cumulative TiO₂ additions (Figure 3).

Both instruments were scored against the known dose of TiO₂ rather than against each other, which revealed that above 30 mg both instruments degrade, in separate ways. The BioDrop keeps its slope but its readings scatter (R² 0.85, SD 0.12). The in-line photometer stays precise (R² 0.99, SD 0.022), but its response per milligram falls, so it bends away from its low-dose line.

A Gaussian process was fitted as a second check. The linear fit wins inside its own range (RMSE 0.0235 against 0.0254) and is kept; extrapolated past OD 1.2, which is what happens in a batch nobody is watching, linear error rises to 0.1715 while the GP holds 0.067, and the GP found its own validity ceiling at OD 1.55 unprompted. Its 95% band covered 88.9% of held-out points, so its intervals are too narrow and are reported as such. Across the range a culture occupies, OD ≤ 1.2, the photometer regresses on the BioDrop with slope 1.018, R² 0.995 and residual SD 0.024 OD. TiO₂ establishes linearity, not cell number.

R3 · B. subtilis 168 growth log with the photometer scored on unseen data

21 July to 8 August 2026, 434 hours of B. subtilis 168 at 22 °C, 2,671 valid rows, no sample drawn. Two axes are scored from separate features: a sensor axis on the reference beam, which never passes through the culture, and a process axis on the specific growth rate.

Each axis is scored every 10 minutes. Every check gets one number, and if that number is above the limit (the 99.5th percentile of the healthy first 120 hours), that check counts as an alarm. The percentage is the share of checks in the window that were alarms.

That event is a real one: 0.21 OD against a local residual SD of 0.022, optical channel normal throughout. Contamination was the first hypothesis and found no support — the culture still smelled of B. subtilis and four-quadrant streaks gave 168 morphology. Autolysis with cryptic growth, sporulation and regermination, and biofilm sloughing all remain, and one run cannot separate them; the repeat is WB800 expressing ACCD constitutively, under antibiotic selection.

At 216.3 hours, the trust layer flagged the sensor, 26.7 hours before the OD channel degraded completely at 243.0 hours — a sufficient buffer for an automated controller to disregard the faulty feed. Standard monitoring algorithms, typically triggered by any sudden step exceeding 0.05 OD, flagged 23% of sensor anomalies alongside 0.8% of biological shifts, unable to distinguish instrument failure from true cellular dynamics.

Every other part of the twin works from the OD reading. The forecast (R4) projects it forward, the growth prior (R5) is checked against it, and the decision to switch on green light depends on what it says about the culture. Thus, if that reading is wrong, all of them inherit the error. They also have no way to detect it, because a drifting sensor can produce numbers that look like ordinary growth.

R3 gives the twin a check that sits upstream of everything else. Before a reading reaches the forecast or the operator, the sensor axis says whether the instrument can be trusted, and the process axis says whether the culture has changed. On the 434-hour run, this would have given the twin 26.7 hours to mark OD as unreliable, pause forecasts built on it and flag the photometer for service. In that whole period, the readings themselves still looked normal.

For a reactor beside a field, this is how the twin can treat its own readings as uncertain. It can't sample the culture to confirm a reading, but it can know when that reading has stopped describing the culture. The method has been shown on one run, with one event of each kind. A detection rate will need faults made on purpose, on batches the layer has not seen.

R4 · Batch forecasting rules fixed in advance

We ran 102 hours of perfusion from 2 to 6 September 2026 with B. subtilis 168, with OD600 values ranging from 0.83 to 3.77. This is the second of run 4’s two files (Every run so far): the culture was started on 1 September and logged for 3.2 h up to OD 0.24, then went unrecorded for 10.2 h. The comparison starts its clock at the second file, so the exponential phase is mostly in the gap. We compared Kalman filter, local linear, global linear and exponential forecasting with a persistence model (which forecasts no change), generating forecast origins every hour from 12 h, with each forecast only using data up to its origin, and every model scored against the same OD600 observation. Persistence is the baseline a model has to beat to be worth its complexity.

HorizonPersistence RMSEKalman RMSEImprovement
2 h0.0670.04041%
4 h0.1320.08338%
8 h0.2560.19225%
12 h0.3730.31017%

A two-state Kalman filter on level and slope beats persistence at every horizon, and the margin narrows as the horizon lengthens, which is the expected shape. Two of the three simpler alternatives (a global linear fit, an exponential fit) were worse than persistence at short horizons, so the comparison is not a straw man.

On the other hand, global linear gives early data as much weight as recent data. If growth speeds up or slows down partway through the run, its line points the wrong way. Exponential assumes growth keeps accelerating. Over a 102-hour perfusion run the culture doesn't keep doing that, so the curve overshoots. While local linear follows recent level and slope, the Kalman filter also weighs each new reading against how noisy the data is, so it reacts to real changes without chasing random scatter.

The Kalman filter suits this role because it updates with each new reading as it arrives, so the forecast stays current throughout a batch without being refitted, and it tracks its own uncertainty, which the Growth twin tab shows as a band. Its forecasts are only as reliable as the readings fed into it, which is why they sit downstream of the trust layer in R3.

R5 · Carrying a growth prior from plate to reactor held-out arms

On 19 September 2026, B. subtilis WB800 was tested in the light-programmable array wells with six light arms, with OD read on a plate reader at five time points to 24 h. A logistic model was chosen over logistic-with-lag by AICc and fits at NRMSE 1.9% (R² 0.997), against the 15% acceptance criterion used for bioprocess models [3]: \(r = 0.299 \pm 0.017\;\mathrm{h^{-1}}\) (doubling time 139 min), \(K = 1.005\), \(X_0 = 0.0235\). We then tested whether this fit could predict growth it had not seen, comparing forecasts of 24 h OD made from the first 8 hours of data with the fitted curve as a prior and without it.

Two things come out of it. First, green induction costs no growth: r spans 0.279 to 0.317 h⁻¹ across all six arms, within one standard error of each other, and the highest-green arm reaches the highest plateau. Second, the fit can be reused as a prior to predict growth in another vessel. We tested this on each arm in turn: fit on the other five, then predict its 24 h OD from its first 8 hours.

We observed an error of at most 7% with the prior, and 29 to 68% without it, from the same eight hours of data. This is the plate acting as a scale-down model for the reactor, which is how process transfer is done in biomanufacturing [3], and it is the reason the next batch can be predicted before it is run.

It is important to note that the six arms are biologically near-identical, so this measures reproducibility across wells, not prediction under a new condition. It is the right shape of test and not yet a strong one. The run has no sample between 8 and 24 h, where the inflection falls, so K is pinned by one time point.

A reactor run takes days to produce a model of the organism; a plate run takes 24 hours and tests six conditions side by side. R5 shows a fit from the plate can carry into a new batch, so the twin can forecast from the first few hours instead of waiting for most of the curve — the early-batch situation where R4's Kalman filter has least to work with. For now, only the growth rate carries over. The ceiling and starting amount need the plate-reader and in-line OD scales cross-calibrated first, which is why the registered forecast below predicts r alone.

Measuring it in the permeate rather than the culture is the point: expression and delivery across the membrane tested in one number. The software that runs the reactor

Every number on this page was recorded by one program: the Arduino App Lab application our instructor Chars wrote for the Uno Q board on BR-01. It is the instrument chain, the safety interlocks and the data logger at once, and the version below is the one recording the batch now running, B. subtilis WB800 secreting codon-optimised ACC deaminase from a constitutive promoter. Firmware v3.10.9, backend v4.12, interface v4.13 — thirteen releases in the five weeks to the freeze.

What survives which failure

The Uno Q carries a microcontroller and a Linux computer in one package, and which of them holds a job decides what is still true when something breaks. Every interlock is in firmware, so no fault in a model, a backend or a browser can drive the loop past a limit: lumen-in or trans-membrane pressure above 0.5 bar for two consecutive 200 ms windows opens the pinch valve and latches a fault, as do fifteen minutes closed, a servo at 60 °C, and a servo that stops answering. A latched fault refuses to close again until an operator clears it.

Recording is insulated from the operator the same way. Each sampling window is written and fsynced to the board's own disk as it closes, so a jammed command channel costs control but not the record, and closing the browser or sleeping the laptop costs neither; after a restart the application appends to the same file and marks the interruption in a gap_s column. A reading that could not be obtained is left blank, never written as zero, and each row carries the algorithm, configuration and constants that produced it — which is what lets a September run be pooled with an October one instead of merely plotted beside it.

The interface

One file, no build step, no install, served by the board at port 7000 in English or Traditional Chinese, with an explanation and a normal range behind every information mark. The two warnings in the figure are the point of it: the amber banner says the OD600 blank and pressure zeros were restored from a file rather than measured on the spot and will not clear until someone recalibrates, and the red strip says TMP_out has gone negative, so the permeate side is pushing back against the membrane. Neither can be dismissed. The page itself opens full screen →, served here without a board, so it reports its own instruments as absent.

How each reading is made

None of the four instruments gives a number directly; each is a measurement procedure in firmware, and everything downstream inherits its honesty.

What it computes, and what it is checked against

From three pressures and one flow the application reports the module rather than the sensors: TMP_out as \(P_{\text{out}}-P_s\), the lumen drop with the module's water column removed, wall shear computed twice over — once from pressure as \(\tau_w/\mu\), once from flow as \(8v/d\) — and their ratio as the clogging indicator. The module is 11 fibres of 1 mm bore and 0.6 m length, 0.02 m² of 0.2 µm membrane, 1 L of culture at 22 °C, and the thresholds come from the measured operating point in R1.

What is watched for fouling is \(R=\Delta P_{\text{net}}/Q\) rather than ΔP alone, with cleaning at twice baseline, and flux is converted to 25 °C before runs are compared — a 3 °C drift in room temperature otherwise reads as fouling. That makes loop temperature something the maintenance decision depends on, which is why a typed 37 °C in the current batch is a problem rather than a detail. Ratios are suppressed below 10 mL/min, where the flow meter is too coarse to divide by, and the block labels itself provisional because the clean-module baseline has not been re-fitted on this plumbing.

The 8 October batch

Figure 8 is a frame from the run the registered forecast was written against. In-line: OD600 at 0.1758 AU rising from near zero over 89 minutes, pH 6.500 drifting down, three pressures, 386.9 mL/min of loop flow at PWM 145. Derived against the provisional baseline: wall shear 4996 s⁻¹, ΔP ratio 0.83, γ ratio 1.01. Three things on screen are neither.

  • Flux and permeability are typed in. Nothing weighs the permeate, so the 72 LMH shown is a hand-entered 24.00 mL/min over the membrane area, 36 times the design flux, and the 92%-of-initial permeability trend is a trend in that number.
  • The calibration was restored, not taken, as the banner says, at an unknown temperature and plumbing state.
  • Temperature was typed at 37 °C while the constants assume 22. Viscosity enters wall shear directly, so the shear figures carry a typed temperature, and so does any flux comparison between runs.

The frame also catches the twin disagreeing with itself. With \(b=0\) the ΔP ratio and the γ ratio are the same quantity up to a constant of 1.009 and should read almost identically; here they read 0.83 and 1.01. The gap is a flow mismatch, not physics: the ΔP baseline of 10.08 kPa implies 385 mL/min while \(\gamma_{\text{flow}}=4939\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) implies 320, a ratio of 1.205 against the 1.206 needed to explain it. The two halves of the clogging indicator are computed from different flow windows. In August the same two routes agreed to 2.8% (R1), which is what makes 20% a flag rather than noise, and since the γ ratio is what the cleaning decision hangs on, the recording will settle which window each used.

What the frame does show working: a culture growing, measured continuously with no sample drawn and with bubbles rejected rather than averaged in; a pressure chain delivering 95 of its intended 100 samples a second; 145 rows already on the board's disk; and an interface telling the operator its calibration is stale and its membrane is being pushed backwards, instead of quietly reporting numbers.

The next interface

The application above knows everything about the reactor and nothing about the field it will stand in. It has no forecast, no leaf sensor and no way to judge that a crop is heading into stress, and that judgement is the whole point of the twin. The rewrite keeps the split between a browser that displays and firmware that protects, and adds the two inputs the rig has never had: the regional forecast, and the leaf sensor beside the crop. Six tabs — what to do now, the rig as a live P&ID, the growth twin against its forecast band, weather and blast risk, the batch record with provenance in every exported header, and setup.

One idea waiting on it costs no new hardware: a chromoprotein downstream of the protectant, so the photometer already in the loop reports product as well as biomass. Whether two signals in one cuvette separate, and how far from 600 nm the second peak must sit, is unresolved.

The rig application is three months of hardware debugging ahead of this one, and that debt is the work of replacing it: the interlocks, the board-side recording, the bubble rejection and the calibration bookkeeping all exist because a run failed without them. Until the rewrite carries them across and is flashed with matching firmware, the rig application records and the rewrite is tested against the recordings it produces. Nothing on this page was measured by it.

Current limits

  • The loop is not closed. Valves move under software control, but every decision about the culture — above all whether to induce — still goes through a person. In the maturity terms of the literature this is a digital shadow with actuation available [1, 2].
  • n = 1 almost everywhere. Four runs of 168 so far, but only two logged in-line, and they differ in temperature, module and strain: one long run behind R3, part of one perfusion run behind R4, one plate behind R5.
  • Two chassis. R1 to R4 are B. subtilis 168; the production strain is WB800. Whether the method transfers with the strain is the point of the batch running now.
  • 37 °C fits, 22 °C operation. What the 18-day run settles is that the cost is time, not yield: growth at 22 °C is 28 times slower but plateaus at OD 1.454, within noise of the plateau 37 °C reaches in five hours. The rate parameters still do not transfer.
  • Beer–Lambert at scale. The photometer is linear only to about OD 1.2 in a 2 mm cell, and induction light will be attenuated by a dense culture in a way nothing on the rig measures.
  • Trans-membrane pressure is below the resolution of the transducers at the operating point, so lumen ΔP is used as the hydraulic observable instead.
  • The 120 to 160 h event is unexplained. The trust layer called it biological and was right to, and contamination has been ruled out as far as one run allows (R3); three hypotheses remain and nobody yet knows which.
  • The clean-module baseline is provisional, so every fouling ratio is divided by a number not re-measured on this plumbing — which is the likeliest reading of the two halves of the clogging indicator disagreeing by 20% in the current batch.
  • Calibration can outlive its conditions. Blanks and pressure zeros survive a reboot from file. The application says so on screen rather than hiding it, but a restored zero is not a measured one.
  • One leaf sensor, on a bench. It reads a dummy leaf, not a rice leaf, cannot tell guttation from dew, and the blast rule it feeds comes from Japanese fields.

For other teams

  1. The two-axis trust layer. A reference channel that never sees the sample, scored separately from the process, trained on the healthy start of a run with its threshold fixed beforehand. Any team with an optical sensor can run it, and the 434 h batch comes with it as a test case.
  2. Calibrate against a known dose, not against another instrument. Cumulative TiO₂ additions score both instruments against truth. It cost us one afternoon and told us our in-line photometer was the more linear of the two — the opposite of what we assumed.
  3. A plate assay as a scale-down model. Fit growth on a light-programmable array, hand the posterior to the reactor as a prior, register the prediction before the batch. The method is the contribution; the parameters are ours.
  4. A logger that outlives its operator. Each window written to the board's own disk as it closes, the same file resumed after a restart with the gap marked, unavailable readings left blank instead of zero, and every row stamped with the constants that produced it. Any team whose run is longer than their attention span can copy the pattern.
  5. The serial protocol and its test harness. A documented two-line ASCII protocol with a heartbeat watchdog, interlocks on the microcontroller rather than in the browser, and a desktop harness that tests firmware and page together with no hardware attached.

The last of these doubles as the project's cheapest biocontainment layer: a broken fibre shows up as a trans-membrane pressure anomaly, and the transducers that would see it are already in the loop and already logged, so detection is a software change rather than a part to buy. It reports a breach rather than preventing one — the 0.2 µm membrane is still the only thing retaining cells — but it is the first of the four layers on Safety we could actually build.
