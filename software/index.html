Digital Twin

Abstract

ReLEAF's digital twin software aims to help the bioreactor run for weeks with minimal manual instruction, automated control and risk evaluation. It is an AIoT system that connects the bioreactor's sensors and valves, alongside local sensors and online forecasts, to models that learn from new data.

The digital twin reads optical density, pH, pressure and flow from the bioreactor, all live, and commands its pinch and Luer valves from the same interface. We calibrated its photometer against TiO₂ and SiO₂ and ran B. subtilis in the bioreactor six times, the longest for 434 hours. Replayed over that run, the twin flagged the photometer 26.7 hours before its reading failed. On the October run its growth rate came out inside the interval we had registered a fortnight earlier, from a prior fitted on a light plate. Green light, dissolved oxygen and the field sensors have been tested separately and are not yet integrated. The future version adds leaf temperature, leaf wetness and the weather forecast into the full loop, designed but not tested yet. Its Ask tab lets a farmer ask about the bioreactor in plain language and act on the answer, without expertise in biomanufacturing.

Digital Twin and ReLEAF's System

ReLEAF's bioreactor is built to stand beside a rice field and keep producing for weeks at a time. The person responsible for it there grows rice, and nobody with laboratory training is on hand to draw a sample or read a trace. That situation set the list of jobs before any software existed. Somebody has to see what the culture is doing while it runs. Something has to raise an alarm when a reading or the culture starts to drift, because a week can be lost before the next visit. The grower needs to know how much biomass there will be in a few hours, and whether the protectant will be ready when the crop wants it, which means watching the weather forecast and the crop as well as the vessel. And the answer has to come back in plain language, so that acting on it does not depend on reading a dashboard built for a laboratory.

Live readings alone cover only the first of those. A drift alarm needs a model of what a healthy run looks like; a delivery date needs the batch carried forward in simulation; a suggested setting needs that simulation returned to the bioreactor. This is the distinction the literature draws. A digital model represents a physical system but is updated by hand. A digital shadow receives live data automatically, but changes back to the system still need a person. A digital twin closes the loop: data flows automatically from the system to the model and back [1, 2]. The jobs above describe the third one, so that is what we built: a second simulated world running the same batch as the physical one but ahead of it, which is what lets the software hand over a risk assessment and a suggested action before the event instead of a report after it.

ReLEAF is an AIoT system: the bioreactor's sensors and valves are wired to a board that logs every reading, and models learn from those readings. The twin reads optical density, pH, pressure and flow live, and the pinch and Luer valves are commanded from the same interface. Some of what it watches sits outside the bioreactor: a sensor beside the crop reads leaf temperature and leaf wetness, two readings that together say when the weather is letting disease into the field. An autoencoder our team trained on the healthy first 120 hours of the 434-hour run flagged a failing photometer 26.7 hours before its reading became unusable (R2). The models have so far run on recorded data after each run, and a person still makes every decision. The next Digital Twin joins them to the live loop and adds the Ask tab, where a farmer with no expertise in biomanufacturing can ask about the bioreactor in plain language and act on the answer.

Leaf wetness and the rice blast rule

Leaf wetness on its own is not a stress for rice: the leaf sheds water as beads. It matters because a wet leaf is the condition blast fungus needs in order to infect, and whether an infection can start depends on how long the leaf stays wet and how warm it is while wet. That is why one sensor beside the crop reads both channels. The twin applies the BLASTAM rule for leaf blast [3], which counts a wet period as an infection condition when the leaf stays wet for at least 10 hours at a mean of 22 to 25 °C, asks for longer as it gets cooler, and allows no infection below 15 °C or above 25 °C [4]; a dry hour ends the period. Hours already past come from the sensor, and hours ahead are estimated from the forecast, counted as wet when relative humidity reaches 90% [5]. When a period meets the rule, the interface tells the grower to look for lesions five to ten days later.

The sensor has only been on a bench (R3), and its wet threshold is a default until a dry-down test sets it. The rule itself has not been checked against disease records from a Taiwanese field. A met condition means the weather allowed infection; whether lesions appear also depends on spores being present and on the variety and growth stage. ACC deaminase has not been tested against blast, so blast risk is shown to the grower and does not switch the green light on its own.

Run 5 was restarted three times, so it arrives as four files covering 37.5 of its 38 hours. The in-line reading rose from 0.10 to 0.30 over the first nine hours and then fell away while the culture was still there: a sixth of its windows carried more than 20% bubbles, and the reference channel, which never passes through the culture, moved by a factor of 4.8 across the run. Nothing after 10.6 hours can be read as a growth curve. The pH channel then held at 6.90 for twenty hours, which is the probe and not the culture.

Run 6 is the same strain with two changes. The permeate was drawn at half the rate, 12 mL/min against 24, and the photometer was given an acceptance gate that holds the last good value when a window fails its own checks. It ran 41.8 hours without a restart. The gate turned away 31 of 1,083 windows, no window carried more than 20% bubbles, the reference held within 17%, and the trace came out clean: 0.14 at the first window, 1.01 at 7 h, 2.71 at 37 h. After that the sample channel fell to a tenth of the reference and the reading stepped to about 9.9 and stayed there, flagged as low signal, and the twin does not use it. pH fell from 6.93 to 6.41 over the first seventeen hours and then stepped back to 7.0 inside one window, which the log does not explain.

The pressure the module needed beyond what its own geometry needs for clean water rose from 2.0 to 3.8 kPa over those 42 hours. That is the fouling signal the twin watches for, and at 3.8 kPa it is far from the point where the membrane would have to be cleaned. Two excursions break that line, at 1.1 h and 24.3 h. In both the permeate pressure reads zero while the feed rises, so both are the permeate side being opened and neither is the membrane.

Photometer calibration against TiO₂ and SiO₂

TiO₂, 23 August

On 23 August 2026, cumulative TiO₂ additions into a stirred 400 mL loop gave 45 paired points between the in-line photometer and the BioDrop spectrophotometer. Up to an optical density of 1.2 the photometer follows the BioDrop with slope 1.018 and R² 0.995.

SiO₂, 10 October

On 10 October 2026 the same loop ran the calibration iGEM's protocol actually asks for: silica in 2.5 mg steps from 2.5 to 100 mg in 500 mL of 1% SDS, read at every step on the BioDrop and on both photometer builds, and logged in-line every five seconds for 4 h 27 min.

The run carries its own check. At 137.6 min the suspension was diluted and then restored, which tests proportionality without the dose being known: 2.3610 before (SD 0.0033, 29 readings), 0.7943 after (SD 0.0017, 38), and 2.3665 restored (SD 0.0157, 16). The ratio across the dilution is 2.97 one way and 2.98 the other, and the restored reading returns within 0.2% of the original. A reading of 2.36 therefore carries the same proportional meaning as one of 0.79, which is the property a calibration needs for the twin to compare one reading against another.

The leaf and air sensors on the bench

On 10 October 2026 the field sensor rig was tested at three sites with stable air temperature: an incubator at 22 °C, a room at 26 °C and a stairwell at 26.4 °C. The rig is an ESP32 reading two sensors: an SHT31 for air temperature and humidity, and an SN-3001-YM-N01 for leaf temperature and leaf wetness over RS485. Both sensors were held in air beside each other, with no leaf. Each site had three five-minute runs logged every five seconds, giving 540 paired readings.

The SHT31 matched the nominal temperature of each site. Sharing the same air, the two sensors should agree, yet the leaf sensor read 1.2 to 1.9 °C warmer once settled, and the size of that gap changed from site to site, so one fixed offset will not correct it.

In the field the leaf sensor will be on a plant and the two readings are expected to differ: the leaf minus air temperature difference is one of the signals of water stress. A bias of 1.2 to 1.9 °C is large enough to hide that signal or invent one, so it has to be measured and removed before the twin acts on it.

Module pressure drop

On 16 August 2026 we pumped water at 22 °C through the bioreactor at eight pump speeds and measured the pressure lost along the way, once with the hollow fibre module in place and once with a plain tube where the module sits. At 263 mL/min, the flow we run at, the module loses 0.096 bar, 1.03 times what the textbook formula predicts for 11 open fibres, so none are blocked. At that flow the force of the liquid on the fibre walls, calculated once from the pressure sensors and once from the flow meter, agrees to 2.8%, so each sensor can check the other.

Batch forecasting

We ran 102 hours of perfusion, 2 to 6 September 2026, with B. subtilis 168 secreting ACC deaminase constitutively; OD600 rose from 0.84 to 3.77. We compared Kalman filter, local linear, global linear and exponential forecasts against persistence, which predicts no change. A forecast was made every hour from 12 h, each using only data up to that point.

Most of the run is above the linear limit. The 12-hour median OD rose from 1.06 to 3.75, so R5 forecasts the photometer reading, not biomass. The model comparison still holds, because every model forecasts the same signal.

Global linear weighs early data as much as recent data, so it points the wrong way when growth changes speed. Exponential assumes growth keeps accelerating, which it does not over 102 hours of perfusion, so it overshoots. Local linear follows the recent level and slope; the Kalman filter also weighs each reading against the noise, so it follows real changes without chasing scatter.

Registered forecast

To test the forecast on a run it had not seen, we registered it on 1 October 2026, before any bioreactor run of B. subtilis WB800 with constitutive ACC deaminase secretion. The prior comes from the light plate (R6). Run 5 was the first such run; its reading broke down after ten hours, so the forecast is scored on run 6.

Only r is scored. \(K\) and \(X_0\) were fitted on the plate-reader scale in a 150 µL well, and the bioreactor reads a 2 mm in-line flow cuvette with no cross-calibration between the two yet, so they stay out of the primary endpoint instead of being quietly rescaled. The light plate's temperature was not recorded and our 37 °C shake-flask fit is about four times faster, so the criterion does not apply to a batch run at 37 °C.

If the rate fails and the shape passes, the curve is right and the rate is not, and temperature is the first place to look. If both fail, the prior does not cross from plate to bioreactor, and we will report that as a result. The same batch carries the first ACC deaminase activity assay, which gives the product a measured quality attribute; ELISA follows on the CcaS/CcaR version once the western blot band is strong enough.

The scored endpoint passes: the rate fitted to run 6 sits inside the interval registered thirteen days before it, from a prior fitted in a 150 µL well on a plate reader. Two things keep that from being more than it is. The bioreactor passed the plate's ceiling in seven hours, which is the size of that gap.

The Software

The Digital Twin

It has three parts. Firmware v3.10.9 (sketch/sketch.ino) runs on the Uno Q's microcontroller, reads the instruments and holds every interlock. Backend v4.12 (python/main.py) runs on the board's Linux side, computes derived values and alarms, writes the log to the board's disk and serves the page on port 7000. Interface v4.13 (assets/index.html) is one file in the browser: it shows the live readings and sends the valve commands, and holds no interlock of its own. The firmware pushes readings to the backend and the backend calls the firmware for commands, over the board's Bridge link.

The next Digital Twin

The digital twin we ran in experiments has not integrated leaf wetness data and weather forecast predictions into the green light trigger decision, due to time constraints. We did develop this new digital twin for future testing that has six tabs: what to do now, a live dashboard, the growth twin against its forecast band, weather and blast risk, the batch record, and setup. And a self learning function that allows users to record every run's results and data for more precise future predictions.

In the Ask tab, the farmer users can paste an API key from their own AI provider (Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini) and can then ask about the screen in plain language, such as whether the bioreactor is fine or why the green light is still off. With each question the assistant receives the readings, warnings and advice on screen. It can explain them and cannot move a valve or the light. The key stays in the browser tab and is not saved. On the iGEM wiki the chat is switched off, because the wiki allows no request to an outside server.

Current limits

Four things are not yet in the twin. Loop temperature: the probe is fitted but has not been answering, so the runs used a temperature typed in by the operator. Dissolved oxygen: we had trouble calibrating the sensor and neither probe can be autoclaved, so it is not on the bioreactor. Green light on the bioreactor: induction was tested on the light-programmable array and is not built into the bioreactor, so the twin cannot yet switch it on. Induction light in the culture: it is not measured, and a dense culture will dim it by an amount nothing on the bioreactor reads.

The October runs added a fifth. The in-line photometer has a top end: somewhere above an optical density of 2.7 the sample beam fell to a tenth of the reference, and the reading stepped to about 9.9 and stayed there for the last four hours of run 6. The acceptance gate marks those windows as low signal, which is enough for the twin to stop trusting them and not enough to recover a number from them. Above that density the bioreactor is blind until someone draws a sample by hand.
