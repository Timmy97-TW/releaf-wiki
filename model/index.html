Math Model

Abstract

The model turns Taiwan’s weather into a stress forecast, the forecast into a lamp setting for the reactor, and the reactor’s measured output into litres of protectant per hectare and New Taiwan dollars per hectare per year.

Three blocks run as one chain, and each is tested against something outside itself. The stress index was chosen by Taiwan’s own rice harvests since 2001: 32,767 models were scored on years they had not seen, and the one kept has 32% less error than the no-weather trend in the second crop. Light, wind and badly timed water cost rice; hours above 35 °C earn nothing. Our DNPH assay sets the reactor’s output at 7.25 mU per mL, which is 1.056 g of ACC cleared per litre of culture per day. The dose is then anchored on the one protectant dose this project has applied to living plants and measured: a hectare needs about 1,670 L placed at the roots, which carries nearly three times more ACC-cleaving capacity than a stressed canopy produces in a day, so the scarce quantity is litres moved and not enzyme made. Our agar experiment also settles when: given a day before the salt, fresh weight held and chlorophyll stayed flat across the whole salt range, and that is why the system is built around a forecast rather than a sensor. The farm answer follows, and it is uncomfortable. On salt-affected coastal land the hardware returns its capital in 2.4 years; on an ordinary inland paddy it does not return it in 24.7. Efficacy cannot close that gap, because it is already near its ceiling; a cheaper unit or a grain-quality premium could.

On this page

One chain: weather goes in, litres of protectant per hectare come out

Abiotic stress (heat, soil salinity, and vapor pressure deficit) reduces crop yields across Taiwan's farmland long before visible damage appears, by disrupting plant hormone balance and protein stability at the cellular level. ReLeaf addresses this by deploying an optogenetically controlled B. subtilis bioreactor that senses field stress in real time and produces tailored protectant proteins. Our mathematical model asks the question that connects every part of that system: given today's stress conditions in a Taiwanese farm field, how much engineered protectant actually reaches the plant?

Answering that means crossing three physically distinct domains, and no single equation spans all of them. So the model is three blocks run as one chain. Each block takes one quantity from the block before it, and hands one quantity to the block after it.

Block 1 · Taiwan's own harvests decide what a stressed season costs

Our bioreactor makes a crop protectant only when its green lamp is on. So the whole system depends on one number: how stressed is the crop right now?

Most crop stress indices add temperature, water and light with weights chosen by experts, and are rarely checked against what happened in the field. We took a different route: let Taiwan's own harvests decide. The Agriculture and Food Agency (農粮署) has published county rice yields for every season since 2001. A season whose yield fell below its trend was a stressed season. If weather alone can predict which seasons fall, and by how much, that prediction is a stress index, and every harvest becomes a test of it.

Assumptions

The five assumptions the stress index rests on, with the reason for each
#AssumptionWhy?
1A county's yield trend reflects technology and management; the deviation from it reflects weather.Varieties and practices change slowly; weather changes every season.
2The same weather stress causes more damage at some growth stages than others.Rice sensitivity is stage-specific (e.g. cold at heading, rain at tillering).
3Stress can only reduce yield, and only after it passes a threshold.Physical damage is one-sided: a calm day does not add rice.
4The two rice crops respond differently to weather and should be modeled separately.Different seasons, weather and stresses.
5The value of lost rice can be estimated using the current farm-gate price.Official daily price exists; public purchase price is close.

Result 1 · Light, wind and badly timed water cost rice; heat earns nothing

We gathered 18 candidate stresses. For each crop, every one of the 215 − 1 = 32,767 combinations of 15 candidates was fitted and scored on years it had not seen. Each candidate was then judged three ways:

First, by how much the out-of-sample error moved when it was added, averaged over every model it could enter. Second, by how often it helped at all: the percentages on Figure 3 are the share of the 16,384 subsets of the other fourteen variables in which adding this one lowered the error, so 100% means it helped in every model and 50% means a coin flip. Third, by a permutation test, which breaks the link between the variable and the harvest by shuffling it and asks what that costs.

Which stresses were supported by the data. After testing each candidate stress with three different methods, these were the variables that consistently improved the model and were kept in the final version.

Hours ≥ 35 °C helps in 0% of first-crop models and 45% of second-crop ones, and shuffling it costs nothing in either. Taiwan's hottest hours and its warm nights are the stresses a greenhouse experiment would put first, and on twenty-five years of harvests they earn almost nothing. What does cost rice is light (dark spells and radiation deficit), wind (typhoon and lodging gusts) and water in the wrong week.

Methods

The draft carries these equations only inside the block diagram in Figure 1. They are transcribed here. A county-season's weather enters as stage-weighted exposures: a daily variable is spread across growth stages by the probability that day fell in each stage, so a wet week counts differently at tillering and at grain fill.

Stage-weighted exposure \[ x_{\mathrm{stage}} \;=\; \sum_{d} P(\mathrm{stage}\mid d)\; x_{d} \tag{1} \] Summed over the days d of the season. P(stage | d) is the probability that day d fell in that growth stage, which smooths the transplanting-date differences between counties.
The yield model, and the index inside it \[ \mathrm{anomaly} \;=\; a \;+\; \gamma P \;+\; \sum_{i} b_i\, z(x_i) \;-\; \underbrace{\sum_{j} \beta_j E_j}_{\textstyle S} , \qquad \beta_j \ge 0 \tag{2} \] \[ z(x) \;=\; \frac{x - \mu_{\mathrm{county}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{train}}} , \qquad \hat{y} \;=\; \mathrm{trend} \times \exp(\mathrm{anomaly}) \tag{3} \] One fit per crop. P is the sunshine-potential term, z(xi) are the stage terms standardised against the county's own history, and Ej are the damage exposures. The constraint βj ≥ 0 is assumption 3 written as an equation: a damage term can take yield away and never add it. The stress index S is the damage sum alone, which is why it is bounded below by zero and why it reads as a loss.
Index to yield loss, and to the lamp \[ \mathrm{loss} \;=\; 1 - e^{-S} , \qquad S_{\mathrm{addr}} \;=\; \sum_{j} \varepsilon_j\, \beta_j E_j \tag{4} \] The first identity is what makes a harvest a test of the index: predicted yield is the county trend times e−S, and the Agency publishes the realised yield beside it. The second is what Block 2 receives: each damage term is weighted by εj, the share of that stress ACC deaminase can act on (0.58 for salinity, Figure 10), and the normalised result is the demand f.

Coefficients are not tabulated in the draft. Figure 5 reports every term's effect in kg/ha with a bootstrap interval, which is the same information in the units a reader can use, but the raw βj and bi are not yet written down anywhere.

Conclusion

Q1 · Which weather costs Taiwanese rice?

Light shortage, typhoon wind and lodging, and water (heavy rain, rain at tilling). Heat and warm nights barely register.

Q2 · What is the model and what does its index mean?

One equation per crop; its damage half is the stress index. First crop: 0.15 is a normal year, 0.35 has meant ≈ −4%, −290 kg/ha, −NT$7,500/ha.

Q3 · Does it hold up on unseen data?

Yes: 32% less error than the trend (second crop), 8% (first crop), under the strictest test; most of the gain is in bad seasons. A real blind test (2026) is under way: 4 of 16 counties published, county error 7.9% (trend 8.8%).

Block 2 · One litre of culture clears a gram of ACC a day

The reactor holds Bacillus subtilis WB800 carrying the cyanobacterial CcaSR two-component system. Green light at 535 nm activates the sensor kinase CcaS, which phosphorylates CcaR. CcaR~P activates the PcpcG2 promoter, transcribing acdS fused to a secretion signal peptide. The Sec translocon exports the enzyme and cleaves the signal peptide, leaving active ACC deaminase in the culture supernatant.

Result 1 · Useful control runs from demand 0.015 to 0.94, and the lamp sets both ends

The measured transfer function. Castillo-Hair et al. (2019) characterised CcaSR in B. subtilis and fitted the steady-state response to a Hill function with half-maximal intensity K½ = 4.66 ± 0.63 µmol m−2 s−1 and Hill coefficient n = 1.88 ± 0.16, over a dynamic range exceeding 70-fold. The same system in E. coli gives K½ = 1.075 and n = 2.737, so the parameters are host-specific and the B. subtilis values are the correct ones here.

Inversion. Block 1 produces addressable stress Saddr and converts it to a demand f between 0 and 1. The lamp setpoint is the light intensity that drives promoter output to that fraction of maximum:

Lamp setpoint from demand \[ I \;=\; K_{1/2} \left( \frac{f}{1-f} \right)^{1/n} \tag{5} \] K½ = 4.66 µmol m−2 s−1, n = 1.88. This is equation (5) solved for I after setting the Hill output In/(Kn + In) equal to f. It diverges as f → 1, which is why the lamp ceiling, not the biology, sets the top of the control range.

Result 3 · Secretion detected at 7.25 mU mL−1, not yet quantified

Measured activity. ACC deaminase activity was measured by DNPH colorimetry on culture supernatant. The enzyme converts ACC to α-ketobutyrate and ammonia in 1:1 stoichiometry, so α-ketobutyrate production equals ACC cleaved. Reaction conditions were 43.0 mM ACC, pH 8.0, 30 °C, 30 min, with the WB800 host supernatant as blank.

Signal is not a biomass artefact. WB800 has the highest OD600 and the second-highest absorbance; samyQ has lower biomass and higher signal. A background from cell density or secreted carbonyl metabolites would produce matching ranks. This argument is independent of the standard curve and survives any calibration error.

Every number downstream of here inherits this assay, so its limits travel with them. One replicate, one day, one culture. samyQ clears the detection limit and does not clear the quantitation limit, so the correct reading is secretion detected, titre not yet quantified. The rank order (samyQ > csn > host) is what survives any calibration error; 7.25 is a provisional point estimate, and Block 3's dose budget is built on it.

Calibration correction. The standard-curve concentrations were originally computed against a 110 µL reaction volume while the actual standard volume was 160.6 µL. Correcting this reduces every derived activity by a factor of 1.460.

Implied enzyme concentration. Substrate was 43.0 mM against a Km of 3.4 mM, so the assay ran at 93% of Vmax. Correcting to Vmax = 7.82 mU mL−1 and applying kcat = 146 min−1 (P. putida UW4) gives a secreted enzyme concentration of 54 nM, or 1.96 mg L−1.

From activity to protein \[ \frac{v}{V_{\max}} = \frac{[S]}{K_m + [S]} = \frac{43.0}{3.4 + 43.0} = 0.927 , \qquad V_{\max} = \frac{7.25}{0.927} = 7.82\ \mathrm{mU\,mL^{-1}} \tag{6} \] \[ [E] \;=\; \frac{V_{\max}}{k_{\mathrm{cat}}} \;=\; \frac{7.82\ \mathrm{nmol\,min^{-1}\,mL^{-1}}}{146\ \mathrm{min^{-1}}} \;=\; 53.6\ \mathrm{nM} \;=\; 1.96\ \mathrm{mg\,L^{-1}} \tag{7} \] Michaelis–Menten with no inhibition. The mass uses the 36.5 kDa subunit, because kcat is per active site and the active tetramer carries four of them: 53.6 nM of sites is 13.4 nM of tetramer, and both come to the same 1.96 mg L−1 of protein. The published range for B. subtilis heterologous secretion is 4 to 200 mg L−1, so this sits a factor of two below its floor.

Accumulation. Enzyme accumulates while the lamp runs and decays in the medium, so banked activity saturates at production rate × mean lifetime. Running the reactor longer past that point adds nothing.

Culture reached OD600 3.06. At a doubling time of 29 min in LB at 37 °C, that is about 5.9 doublings from a 1:100 inoculum, or 4 to 6 h including the approach to stationary phase. Batch duration was not recorded, so the production rate is inferred rather than measured.

Conclusion

Q1 · What light for a given demand?

Control is useful between f = 0.015 and 0.94. Below that the lamp floor takes over, above it the promoter saturates.

Q2 · How fast does it respond?

105 min to half-maximal transcription, about 7.3 h from decision to enzyme active in the leaf. Uptake is 41% of that. The controller must act on forecast stress.

Q3 · How much enzyme?

7.25 mU mL−1 with the samyQ signal peptide, equivalent to 1.96 mg L−1, below the published range for B. subtilis secretion. In the unit the farm question needs: one litre of supernatant clears 44.0 mg of ACC per hour, or 1.056 g per day.

Key findings

  • Signal peptide selection. samyQ gives detectable secretion; csn does not. The comparison is the basis for carrying samyQ forward.
  • Controller bounds. The lamp ceiling saturates the promoter at f = 0.94, defining the upper limit of useful control.
  • Forecast requirement. A 7.3 h decision-to-protection time makes reactive control structurally late, which is why Block 1 supplies a forward index.
  • Titre is low but not limiting. 1.96 mg L−1 sits a factor of two below the published floor for B. subtilis secretion. It still gives a hectare's proven dose nearly three times more ACC-cleaving capacity than a stressed canopy produces in a day (Block 3, Result 4), so the scarce quantity is litres moved, not enzyme made.
  • The assay's own currency. Everything in Block 3 is built on 1.056 g ACC L−1 day−1 and on one replicate below the limit of quantitation. Both travel with it.

Block 3 · A hectare needs 1,670 L, and pays back only on salt-affected land

Block 2 ends at the reactor outlet holding a number in mU mL−1. No farmer can buy mU mL−1. This block turns it into three quantities a farm decision actually runs on: litres of protectant per hectare, reactor-days per hectare per season, and New Taiwan dollars per hectare per year.

It does that without a chain of literature loss factors. The project has applied ACC deaminase to living plants once, at a recorded dose, and measured what it saved (Result 1). That dose, converted to field units, is the anchor. Everything after it is arithmetic on our own two assays: the agar salinity experiment for what protection is worth, and the DNPH assay for what a litre of culture can do.

Result 1 · The dose that worked, converted to a field rate

Experiment #9 put seven-day-old Arabidopsis on square agar plates and gave each plate either buffer or 0.22 µm-filtered ACC deaminase supernatant, one day before, together with, or one day after 0, 75 or 100 mM NaCl, then grew them 15 days and weighed and extracted them. The protectant went on as three sprays from a calibrated nozzle, 1.67 mL in total.

That is a recorded dose over a known area, so it converts directly into a field application rate.

From the plate to the hectare \[ R \;=\; \frac{V_{\mathrm{plate}}}{A_{\mathrm{plate}}} \times A_{\mathrm{ha}} \;=\; \frac{1.67\ \mathrm{mL}}{100\ \mathrm{cm^2}} \times 10^{8}\ \mathrm{cm^2\,ha^{-1}} \;=\; 1{,}670\ \mathrm{L\,ha^{-1}} \tag{8} \] One application. The plate area is assumption C2: a 120 × 120 mm plate would give 1,160 L ha−1 instead, so the figure is quoted as 1,670 with a floor of 1,160. At the measured titre of 7.25 mU mL−1 that rate carries 12.1 kU of ACC deaminase per hectare, able to cleave 1.76 kg of ACC per hectare per day.

The reason this rate is worth anchoring on is what it bought. Fresh weight is the cleanest measurement in the experiment: one balance, one number per plate, no spectrophotometer and no derived coefficients.

Read as a share of the loss recovered, the point estimates are 1.52, 1.22 and 1.17 for the three timings at 75 mM. All three exceed 1, meaning the treated plates outweighed the unstressed control, so the model caps the efficacy at ε = 1 for Arabidopsis biomass at this salinity and reads the result as salt cost the control half its mass and cost the treated plates nothing. The overshoot itself is informative and unresolved: the supernatant is TB medium plus everything B. subtilis secreted into it, and without an empty-vector arm the growth promotion cannot be attributed to the enzyme.

At 100 mM the data do not support a protection claim. The before-salt arm still holds 1.98 times the buffer, but the with-salt arm shows no advantage at all (1.01×), the after-salt arm was never weighed, and the buffer control itself gained mass going from 75 to 100 mM, which salt does not do. The model therefore costs moderate salinity from this experiment and leaves severe salinity to Geospatial Analysis's literature curve, flagged as such in Result 5.

Result 2 · Both assays say the same thing: it has to arrive before the stress

Fresh weight and chlorophyll were measured on the same plates, by different instruments, and they agree on one operating rule. The before-salt timing is the best arm at 75 mM and the only arm still working at 100 mM. Chlorophyll says it a second way.

Flat is the strongest signal in the dataset. Across a salt range that costs the control half its mass, a plant primed one day earlier shows no chlorophyll response to salt dose at all, and shows it with the tightest replicate scatter on the plate. Two independent measurements picking out the same arm, from an experiment with one plate per arm, is about as much as this design can deliver.

That single finding is why this page has three blocks instead of one. If protection only works when it is already in place, the controller cannot react to a sensor reading. It has to act on a forecast, which is Block 1, and it has to start early enough to clear the 7.3 hour decision-to-leaf horizon, which is Block 2. The wet lab did not confirm the architecture after the fact; it is the reason for it.

The operating rule, in the controller's terms \[ t_{\mathrm{start}} \;\le\; t_{\mathrm{stress}} - H , \qquad H \;=\; 7.3\ \mathrm{h} \tag{9} \] Production must begin at least one response horizon before the stress arrives. With a three-day forecast from Block 1 and H = 7.3 h, the margin is comfortable; the binding constraint is forecast skill, not reactor speed. Equation (4) supplies the demand f for that window, not for the present hour.

What the chlorophyll numbers cannot do is set a dose. Four of the eleven extracts were read above the instrument's 1.0 AU linear range, and chlorophyll a/b runs 0.78 to 5.14 against 2.5 to 3.5 for healthy seedling tissue, so the absolute mg g−1 values are not quotable. The shapes are internally consistent across all three reads; the magnitudes are not. The workbook records both facts in its own QC row.

Result 3 · The dosing route changes the answer twentyfold

1,670 L ha−1 is the dose per unit of ground area. It is the right number only if the protectant ends up as concentrated around the rice roots as it was in the agar. Three routes do three different things to that concentration, and the choice is worth more than any parameter in this block.

Route factor \[ R_{\mathrm{route}} \;=\; R \times \kappa \tag{10} \] \[ \kappa_{\mathrm{root}} = 1 , \qquad \kappa_{\mathrm{flood}} = \frac{V_{\mathrm{paddy}}}{V_{\mathrm{plate}} + V_{\mathrm{agar}}} \Big/ \frac{A_{\mathrm{ha}}}{A_{\mathrm{plate}}} , \qquad \kappa_{\mathrm{foliar}} = \frac{\mathrm{LAI}_{\mathrm{rice}}}{\mathrm{LAI}_{\mathrm{plate}}} \] In the plate, 1.67 mL went into about 50 mL of agar, so the root zone saw 3.2% v/v neat supernatant. Flood irrigation has to reach that concentration in 500 to 1,000 m3 ha−1 of paddy water, which is κ = 10 to 19. A foliar spray must cover leaf area rather than ground area, and rice at panicle initiation carries LAI 4 to 5 against roughly 0.08 to 0.15 on a seedling plate, which is κ = 27 to 62.

Two numbers decide this. Dilution matters because at paddy-water concentrations the enzyme sits far below its Km of 1.5 to 17 mM, which is the first-order regime, where clearance rate falls in proportion to enzyme concentration rather than holding at Vmax. And the cuticle matters because it is shut: aqueous cuticular pores are 4 to 5 nm against a 9.4 nm active tetramer, so a foliar dose can only enter through stomata, and only while the droplet is still wet.

The enzyme's own pH curve points the same way. ACC deaminase peaks at pH 8.5 and has no measurable activity below 7.0. A leaf surface film (pH 5.0 to 6.5) and an unstressed apoplast (5.5 to 6.5) run it at 2 to 3% of assay activity; flooded paddy water and a stressed apoplast (6.5 to 7.5) run it at 23%. Everywhere the enzyme might land by accident is a place it does not work, and the one place it works is a plant already under stress, which alkalinises as part of the stress response. Nobody designed that gate; it falls out of the enzyme.

The recommendation this block makes to the build. Dose into the root zone and keep it there. Drip or sub-surface placement holds the lab concentration and costs 1,670 L ha−1; letting the dose disperse into standing paddy water costs 10 to 19 times more for the same effect; spraying the canopy costs 27 to 62 times more and then throws most of it away on a cuticle it cannot cross. Geospatial Analysis already models dispersion through each soil's pore structure, which is the right model for this route, and it is the reason its unit densities are set by soil rather than by area.

Result 4 · Catalysis is not the bottleneck; moving litres is

Before sizing a reactor it is worth asking whether the enzyme is the scarce thing at all. The DNPH assay says one litre of supernatant clears 44.0 mg of ACC per hour, or 1.056 g per day. A stressed rice canopy does not make nearly that much.

Catalytic headroom \[ \Phi_{\mathrm{ACC}} \;=\; c_{\mathrm{ACC}} \cdot B_{\mathrm{canopy}} \cdot n_{\mathrm{turn}} \cdot M_{\mathrm{ACC}} \;=\; 13\ \text{to}\ 607\ \mathrm{g\,ha^{-1}\,day^{-1}} \tag{11} \] \[ \frac{R \cdot \mathrm{cap}}{\Phi_{\mathrm{ACC}}} \;=\; \frac{1{,}670\ \mathrm{L} \times 1.056\ \mathrm{g\,L^{-1}\,day^{-1}}}{\Phi_{\mathrm{ACC}}} \;=\; \frac{1{,}764}{\Phi_{\mathrm{ACC}}} \;=\; 2.9\ \text{to}\ 140\times \tag{12} \] Tissue ACC 5 to 50 nmol g−1 FW under stress, canopy fresh biomass 25 to 40 t ha−1, pool turnover 1 to 3 per day, all swept rather than fitted. The headroom stays above 1 across the whole sweep, which is the only thing that has to be true for the conclusion.

Even on the most demanding corner of that sweep the dose that worked carried nearly three times more ACC-cleaving capacity than the canopy produces in a day, and on the least demanding corner it carried 140 times more. So the dose is not set by how much enzyme has to exist. It is set by how much liquid has to be moved to put the enzyme where the ACC is. Matching the flux alone would need only 12 to 574 L ha−1 day−1.

What the reactor has to do

The bioreactor as built runs a 300 mL working volume in a 500 mL reservoir. One batch of it holds 2.17 U of activity and 317 mg day−1 of ACC-clearing capacity. A hectare's single application at the proven dose is therefore 5,567 batches, which is the number that decides whether the prototype is a demonstrator or a farm unit.

At the 50 units per hectare that Geospatial Analysis calls for on sandy loam, it falls to 557 mL, which is within a factor of two of the vessel already on the bench.

What ultrafiltration actually buys

Concentration does not reduce the water a field needs. It separates the enzyme from the water, so the farmer carries litres of concentrate and makes the volume up from the irrigation line or the spray tank.

Ten-fold is enough to bring one hectare's application inside a single tank round, and that sets the requirement the membrane has to meet. Retaining a 36.5 kDa subunit needs a molecular-weight cut-off at or below about 10 kDa. The cartridge on record is PES hollow fibre rated at 0.2 µm, which is microfiltration: it is a cell filter, and it would pass the enzyme straight into the permeate. Hardware already records that no rejection figure has been measured across it. This block supplies the specification that measurement should be compared against, and 10 kDa is a different cartridge, not a different setting.

Two numbers on this wiki answer the question “what does a stressed hectare lose?” and they differ by a factor of 8.2. Both are defensible. They are about different fields, and the farmer answer depends entirely on which field is being asked about, so both are carried here rather than averaged.

Rice quality: the term nobody has measured, worth as much as the yield term

Salinity and heat do not only cut how much rice a field yields; they cut what grade it makes, through chalkiness and a lower head-rice ratio. A protectant that keeps a plant unstressed through grain fill should move part of the harvest up a grade. None of this project's experiments measured grain, so the term is written out as a parameter rather than a result.

The quality premium, parameterised \[ P_{\mathrm{quality}} \;=\; \Delta p \cdot Y \cdot \phi \tag{14} \] Δp is the price step between grades in NT$ kg−1, Y the yield (7,354 kg ha−1 at the recent first-crop trend) and φ the share of the harvest that moves up. Every one of the three is unmeasured for this protectant.

Conclusion

Q1 · How much does a hectare need?

1,670 L of neat supernatant per application on the root-zone route, read as an upper bound. It carries 12.1 kU and clears 1.76 kg of ACC a day, against a canopy flux of 13 to 607 g a day.

Q2 · When does it have to arrive?

Before the stress. Fresh weight and chlorophyll independently pick the before-salt arm, and chlorophyll under priming is flat across the whole salt range. This is the finding the three-block architecture exists to serve.

Q3 · Does the route matter?

Twentyfold. Root-zone 1,670 L, flood 16,160 to 32,320 L, foliar 44,533 to 104,375 L. Dose into the root zone.

Q4 · Can the reactor make it?

At one unit per hectare, no: a 100× scale-up from 300 mL. At the 50 units per hectare the soil model calls for, the vessel on the bench is within a factor of two of right. Ultrafiltration at 10× brings a hectare inside one tank round, but needs a 10 kDa cut-off the fitted 0.2 µm cartridge does not provide.

Q5 · What is it worth?

On salt-affected land, NT$126,967/ha/year and a 2.4-year payback at 10 units. On a typical inland paddy, NT$12,140/ha/year and 24.7 years, which does not pay back. Unit cost and land targeting decide this, not efficacy.

What would change these numbers most

One dilution-series plate narrows the dose from a 2.9 to 140× bracket. One grain-quality trial could double the case on ordinary land. One empty-vector arm decides whether any of it belongs to the enzyme.

What the model sent back to the bench and the build

Six results did not stay on this page. Two changed the construct and the controller, two changed the hardware, one changed what the index is looking for, and one changed who the product is for.

samyQ, not csn

Block 2's activity assay is the only side-by-side comparison of the two signal peptides. samyQ clears detection at 7.25 mU mL−1, csn does not clear it at all, and that comparison is why samyQ is the construct carried forward.

The controller runs on a forecast, because priming is what works

Fresh weight and chlorophyll both pick the arm dosed one day before the salt, and chlorophyll under priming is flat across the whole salt range (Block 3, Result 2). A protectant that has to be in place first cannot be triggered by a sensor reading, so Block 1 was built around a three-day forecast and Block 2 around a 7.3 hour horizon.

Dose into the root zone, not the canopy

The same protective effect costs 1,670 L ha−1 placed at the roots, 16,160 to 32,320 L dispersed into paddy water, and 44,533 to 104,375 L sprayed on leaves (Result 3). The route is worth more than any parameter in the model, and the earlier draft of this page was pricing the most expensive one.

The 300 mL vessel is the right size, at the right deployment density

Running the field rate backwards gives a required working volume of 557 mL per unit at 50 units per hectare, which is where Geospatial Analysis's soil dispersion model independently puts the density on sandy loam. Two models built for different purposes agree on one hardware dimension to within a factor of two (Result 4).

The membrane on record cannot do the job the dose budget needs

Ten-fold concentration brings a hectare's dose inside one tank round, and retaining a 36.5 kDa subunit needs a cut-off at or below 10 kDa. The fitted cartridge is rated 0.2 µm, which is a cell filter and would pass the enzyme. Either the concentration step is still missing from the device or the dose budget has no hardware behind it.

Heat stopped being the headline, and the median farmer stopped being the customer

Twenty-five years of harvests say Taiwanese rice loses yield to light, wind and badly timed water, and that hours above 35 °C earn nothing in either crop. The index follows the data. And on an ordinary inland paddy the hardware does not pay back in 24.7 years, while on salt-affected coastal land it returns in 2.4 (Result 5). The product is for the coast.

Conclusion

The model was built to answer one question that no single experiment on this project can reach: given the weather over a Taiwanese rice field, how much engineered protectant does that field need, and is it worth making? Three blocks answer it in sequence, and each one is tested against something outside itself.

The three things worth taking away

1 · The wet lab set the architecture, not the other way round

Protection works when the protectant is already in place and fades when it is not. That single result, visible independently in fresh weight and in chlorophyll, is why there is a forecast block at all. A model that only described the system would not have needed Block 1.

2 · The scarce thing is litres, not enzyme

The dose that worked carries 2.9 to 140 times more ACC-cleaving capacity than a stressed canopy produces in a day. So the engineering problem is distribution and concentration, which is why ultrafiltration and dosing route turned out to matter more than titre, and why a low titre is survivable.

3 · The economics work on the coast and nowhere else yet

At NT$30,000 a unit the system returns its capital in 2.4 years on salt-affected land and not within a working lifetime on an average inland field. Efficacy is near its ceiling and cannot close that gap; a 4.9× cheaper unit, or a grain-quality premium nobody has measured, could.

If one more experiment were possible

One agar plate answers the two questions that most of this page's uncertainty rests on. Before-salt timing at 75 mM NaCl, supernatant at neat, 1:3, 1:10 and 1:30, three plates per dilution, plus one empty-vector arm at neat, with fresh weight as the read-out. The dilution series turns 1,670 L ha−1 from an upper bound into a requirement and collapses a 2.9 to 140× bracket; the empty-vector arm decides whether any of this belongs to ACC deaminase. Every litre, every reactor dimension and every New Taiwan dollar on this page moves with those two answers.

A grain-quality trial carried through to harvest is the second, and it is the only thing on the list that could double the case on ordinary land.

What is still unmeasured

  • Where ε = 0.58 comes from. Our assay measures Arabidopsis fresh weight on agar, and gives point estimates above 1 at 75 mM. The coefficient the index is scaled by is a share of rice grain yield. The published rice studies that bridge the two are referred to but still not named, and that transfer is the largest single assumption on this page.
  • The minimum effective dose. Bracketed between 12 and 574 L ha−1 (flux-matching) and 1,670 L ha−1 (proven). One dilution-series plate closes it, and nothing else on this list moves as many numbers.
  • The empty-vector supernatant arm. Without it the measured effect belongs to our culture supernatant, not to ACC deaminase.
  • Membrane rejection for a 36.5 kDa protein. The fitted cartridge is rated 0.2 µm. The dose budget assumes a 10× concentration that no measured rejection figure supports, and Hardware records the gap.
  • Rice grain quality under protection. Unmeasured in every experiment, and worth as much as the entire yield term on ordinary land.
  • τdecay, the half-life of secreted activity in the medium. Three assumed values span a 30-fold range in what a reactor can bank (Figure 19).
  • Replication of the titre. One culture, one day, below the limit of quantitation. The DNPH report asks for this first, as three replicates on one plate.
  • Km and kcat separately. One operating point cannot split them. A substrate gradient from 0.5 to 43 mM would, and until then Km is a swept parameter and not a value.
  • The pH profile of our own enzyme. Figure 24 is a two-anchor fit to published values, and a different profile moves the delivered activity by about eightfold.
  • The βj coefficients of Block 1. Figure 5 reports every term's effect in kg/ha, but the fitted coefficients are not written down anywhere yet.
  • Plate geometry in the run record. The salinity run does not state its plate size or its spray volume. Both were reconstructed from the heat protocol's nozzle calibration, and every litre figure in Block 3 scales with them.
