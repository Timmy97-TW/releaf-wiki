Can you hear a plant scream?

Not metaphorically. A stressed plant emits real sound — short ultrasonic pops made by its own internal plumbing tearing itself apart. Here's the mechanism, how it was proven, and what it actually sounds like.

The mechanism

Why a thirsty plant pops

Water climbs a stem under tension, pulled upward by evaporation at the leaves — the same physics that lets you draw water up a straw. Drought pushes that tension past a breaking point: air is sucked into a water-carrying channel and the column snaps from liquid into a gas-filled void. That rupture, called cavitation, releases a burst of sound. Cutting a stem causes the same failure faster, by exposing the tensioned water straight to open air.

The evidence

How researchers captured it

1
Ultrasonic microphones. The pops sit far above human hearing, so Khait, Hadany et al. (Tel Aviv University) recorded across 20–250 kHz, about 10 cm from each plant.
2
A soundproofed chamber first. Tomato and tobacco recorded three ways: untouched, five days without water, and freshly cut.
3
Then a working greenhouse. Repeated amid ordinary background noise to prove it wasn't a laboratory artifact.
4
Machine learning. A classifier trained on many acoustic features — not one number — separated stressed from healthy, drought from cutting, and tomato from tobacco.

The honest comparison

Can you tell the stresses apart?

Peak frequency of ~2,500 clicks measured straight from the original recordings, grouped into five conditions. Look at the two shaded zones below: a healthy plant's clicks stay low and separate (gray), while every stressed plant — drought or cut, tomato or tobacco — clicks noticeably higher (orange). Telling those two zones apart is easy. Telling the four stressed conditions apart from each other is the hard part.

Shape = how that condition's clicks are spread across pitch. A taller point means more clicks landed near that pitch. Dot = the median: the "typical" click. Exactly half of that condition's clicks were higher-pitched than this, half were lower.

The gray and orange zones barely touch — but the four orange shapes sit almost on top of each other.

Greenhouse background Tomato, drought Tomato, cut Tobacco, drought Tobacco, cut
That overlap is the point. Scored for separability, background noise splits from plant clicks cleanly (AUC 0.88), while the four stress conditions score just 0.53–0.65 against each other — barely above the 0.50 of a coin flip. That is exactly why the study needed a machine-learning model trained on many features at once, not a threshold on one number. What gives a stressed plant away isn't the sound of any single pop. It's how often they come.

Every frequency shown is the peak frequency of a single click, computed by FFT directly from the original 500 kHz recordings (275–600 clicks per condition in the comparison chart). As a check, 98% of measured clicks fall inside the 20–100 kHz range the paper reports, and 71% inside the 40–80 kHz band its authors described — but the per-click values are our own measurements, not figures published in the paper. Pitch-shifting, click spacing and the hour simulation were produced for this page: true gaps between clicks run to minutes, so spacing here is compressed while keeping its irregular character.
