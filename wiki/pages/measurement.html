Measurement

Abstract

Measuring ReLEAF means making each result traceable: from the physical principle and calibration to the controls, raw data, and protocol another team could repeat.

We developed and documented measurement methods across the entire system so our data could capture failures, improvements, and successes. The photometer reads OD600 directly in the bioreactor loop and converts its short flow-cell path to the conventional 1 cm basis, while software calibration tests the full signal-processing chain against a 1 µm silica standard. Light output is measured and matched across LEDs before optogenetic testing, plant assays use controlled stress and treatment conditions to measure growth and chlorophyll, and protectant assays track protein production, concentration, and activity.

Overview

Can our engineered Bacillus subtilis detect plant stress and respond by producing the right plant protectant at the right time? How well does the protectant aid plant growth?

We break that question into five measurable stages in the order of how the system operates: the bioreactor runs the culture, the software validates the sensor data, light control sets production, protectant assays confirm what was produced and whether it worked, and plant assays measure the response.

Each stage builds on the measurement before it.

  1. 1 Bioreactor assays Does the vessel run, and can we read what is in it?
  2. 2 Software algorithm calibration Is that reading real, or made by the arithmetic?
  3. 3 Optogenetic control Is the light a dose we chose?
  4. 4 Protectant assays Was the protein there, and was it working?
  5. 5 Plant assays Does a stressed plant actually recover?

Bioreactor assays

Continuous in-line monitoring in the bioreactor lets us follow culture growth while also tracking changes across the membrane system, helping distinguish normal operating conditions from signs of pressure buildup, flow changes, or loss of cell retention.

Dual light path inline photometer

To measure culture density continuously without manually withdrawing samples, we built an in-line flow-cell photometer that reads OD600 directly from the bioreactor loop.

In most laboratory workflows, OD600 is measured by removing a sample and reading it on a benchtop spectrophotometer. For long reactor runs, repeated sampling interrupts the system and introduces additional handling steps, which is especially important for ReLEAF because the system is intended to operate outside a conventional laboratory setting.

Our photometer measures the culture as it flows through a 2 mm cuvette using an amber LED near 600 nm. A 90:10 beam splitter directs part of the light to a reference sensor before the main beam passes through the culture. Comparing the sample and reference signals allows changes in LED output to be separated from changes in culture density. Burst sampling and a MAD-based filter are then used to reduce interference from bubbles and transient fluctuations.

Two photometers

We built two versions of the in-line photometer. The Pro uses an amber LED, collimating lens, 90:10 beam splitter, and two lux sensors in a tilted printed body, allowing the reference and sample paths to be measured separately while bubbles move out of the cuvette. The Slim replaces the beam splitter and free-space optics with a fiber collimator feeding the same 2 mm flow cuvette, creating a smaller design that can clip directly onto the loop.

The Pro costs US$371.78 to build and the Slim US$235.29, and that difference is one of the reasons the second build exists.

The two builds show different measurement strengths. The Pro is about 5.3× more sensitive, making it better at resolving small changes at low density. The Slim is less sensitive but stays more linear across the full silica range, with R² = 0.992 compared with 0.974 for the Pro. Within the 0–1 OD range, both perform similarly (0.9947 vs. 0.9874). The Pro is the sensitive build and the Slim the linear one, and the Slim is also the cheaper.

What another team can take from it

Our central contribution is an in-line OD600 measurement with a reproducible relationship to the benchtop scale. Across validation runs, the Pro agreed with the BioDrop to a median paired difference of 3.8%, while the Slim maintained a strong linear response over a wider range.

Four things make that number usable by someone else. The flow cuvette sits in the loop, so OD600 is tracked without withdrawing a sample or interrupting the run. A second sensor watches the LED, which separates a change in light output from a change in culture density. Burst sampling and MAD filtering take out most of the bubble error, and the rejection rate says when a reading should not be trusted. And measurement near 600 nm keeps clear of the green and red that drive CcaSR, so watching the culture does not induce it.

From lux to optical density

Both photometers record raw illuminance and convert it to OD600 in software. Each measurement uses 20 readings per sensor: four dark readings with the LED off and sixteen lit readings. The dark median is subtracted to remove ambient light and sensor offset, the first two lit readings are discarded for settling, and the remaining values are filtered using median ± 3·MAD.

For the Pro, the calculation uses the sample signal S, the reference signal R, and their blank values S₀ and R₀:

OD = log10[ (S₀/R₀) / (S/R) ] × 5

The factor of 5 converts the 2 mm flow-cell path to the conventional 1 cm OD600 basis. Because the raw sensor readings are logged before calculation, every reported value can be recomputed after the run.

How the two optical designs are interpreted

The Pro uses a separate reference channel to remove changes in LED output and fixed differences between the two optical paths. Let P be the LED output, T the sample transmittance, and k the fixed optical difference between the sample and reference paths:

S / R = k × T

Blank referencing then removes k:

(S/R) / (S₀/R₀) = T / Tblank

This allows the Pro to report OD600 directly from the optical measurement without fitting an instrument-specific conversion curve against a benchtop spectrophotometer.

The Slim uses a different optical geometry and therefore does not reproduce the same absolute scale. Instead, it shows a strong linear relationship to particle concentration and to the Pro, with lower sensitivity but greater linearity across the full silica range. The Slim is therefore the build to use when range and a simple optical path matter more than agreeing with the benchtop OD600 scale.

Built-in measurement checks

For the Pro, the reference channel also tracks source stability:

R / R₀ = P / P₀

Values outside 0.85–1.15 trigger a drift flag. Both designs use burst sampling and filtering to reduce transient noise, and raw sensor values are stored so the calculation can be inspected or replayed later.

The blank is the one input that has to be right, since it passes k and Tblank into every later point. It is refused if either sensor is saturated, if too few readings survive the filter, or if either channel reads zero; with no blank stored, the software returns no OD instead of an uncorrected number. On a single optical path the reference terms are unavailable and the software falls back to OD = log10(S₀/S) × 5, where the source intensity does not cancel and any change in it reads directly as a change in OD. Both forms are logged for every measurement.

Both builds are therefore worth having, and for different reasons: the Pro for agreement and sensitivity, the Slim for linearity over a wide range.

A fifth quality-control step, an acceptance gate that withholds unreliable readings, was added after the 8–October 10 reactor run. Its design, performance, and effect on OD600 are covered in the next section on software calibration.

Acquisition and filtering 16 readings, a median fence, one logged point every 10 minutes

Each logged OD600 point is built from a short burst of sensor readings rather than a single measurement.

  1. Discard the first 2 warm-up readings from each sensor.
  2. Record a burst of 16 readings per sensor.
  3. Calculate the median of the burst.
  4. Calculate the median absolute deviation (MAD) from that median.
  5. Reject readings outside median ± 3·MAD.
  6. If more than 50% of the burst is rejected, repeat the measurement and flag the point for excessive bubbling.
  7. Log the accepted result every 10 minutes.

We use the median and MAD because bubbles create one-sided, short-lived drops in transmitted light. A mean and standard deviation can be pulled toward those outliers, while the median-based filter is less affected by them. The dashboard also reports the rejection rate so the user can see when bubbling is affecting measurement quality.

Two standards, two validation runs

We validated the photometers against two independent particle standards, eight weeks apart, using different particles and different experimental setups. Together, the two runs test both agreement with a benchtop reference and reproducibility across optical configurations.

August 23 — titanium dioxide validation: Pro photometer

Titanium dioxide was added by mass to the circulating loop and measured at each step using both the Pro photometer and a benchtop BioDrop. The Pro's raw output agreed with the BioDrop to a 3.8% median paired difference and tracked the independently defined mass standard with R² = 0.9999, compared with 0.9998 for the BioDrop.

The experiment was repeated twice, and both runs independently showed the same agreement with the reference. Two BioDrop points were excluded using predefined criteria, one handling error and one monotonicity violation, without changing the Pro measurements.

Because the Pro already tracked the mass-defined concentration closely, we used the BioDrop as a validation reference and not as the source of a fitted calibration curve.

October 10 — silicon dioxide comparison: Pro against Slim

The second validation used 1 µm SiO₂ microspheres, close to the size scale of Bacillus subtilis. The Pro and Slim were placed in series on the same circulating loop, so both instruments measured the same suspension at the same time. Concentration was defined by the amount of SiO₂ added rather than by either instrument.

Flow and pressure monitoring

Because ReLEAF is designed for operation outside a conventional laboratory, the bioreactor needs continuous measurements that show both culture conditions and system performance.

Flow and pressure provide two complementary measurements of how the hollow-fiber system is operating.

Together, flow and pressure can indicate changes in reactor operation. A sudden drop in flow at the same pump setting may suggest a leak or bypass, while increasing pressure at a similar flow can indicate rising resistance or membrane fouling (Field et al., 1995).

Pressure provides the driving force for permeation across the membrane, while flow determines crossflow through the fibers and the shear experienced by the cells (Wittig et al., 2025). Measuring both therefore helps define the reactor's operating range.

Flow also sets the shear the cells meet inside a fiber (Yuen, 2025). At our operating settings it is about 4000 to 6000 s⁻¹, which we read as comfortably inside what Bacillus subtilis tolerates. That reading is not settled here, and the one published figure we have found points the other way: B. subtilis grew in a Couette flow bioreactor at up to 1482 s⁻¹, but the cells shrank to about half their size and intracellular reactive oxygen species rose 9.3-fold (Sahoo et al., 2003). That was a different vessel, so the number transfers only loosely, but until culture viability has been read after circulation at our own dials, the margin is an expectation and not a measurement.

How the flow was measured

We measured flow in three stages, moving from manual collection to an electronic flow meter and validating the two methods against each other.

First, by hand with no membrane fitted: set the pump dial, catch the outflow in a measuring cylinder over a fixed interval. The pump covers dials 0 to 100 and we tested every tenth, three trials per setting except 70 to 100.

On August 14 we fitted the membrane and read flow and pressure together, still catching the outflow by hand, every second dial from 10 to 24 where we expect to operate: three rounds of three trials.

On August 16 we replaced the hand method with the flow meter, because the hand readings depended too much on who took them. Two configurations ran at the same dials, 10 to 24: a background loop with plain tubing in place of the module and every valve, fitting and cuvette still fitted, and the same loop with the module installed.

The meter is a ZJ-TM02, a Hall-effect type: a magnet in an internal rotor spins with the flow and a fixed sensor emits a pulse each time it passes, so pulse frequency is proportional to flow. Ours gives 2580 pulses per liter, about 43 Hz per L/min. The microcontroller counts rising edges and ignores any pulse arriving within 0.5 ms of the previous one, which rejects switching noise, and reports the pulse count, the time between the first and last pulse and a running total every second. The software converts to mL/min as pulses / 2580 × 1000 per minute.

We validated the electronic flow meter against simultaneous manual collection, with the two methods agreeing at R² = 0.995.

How pressure was measured

The current system measures pressure at three points so true transmembrane pressure can be calculated during operation.

Pressure is read by three transmitters, Jingfeng BP8G-AGB, on the feed (lumen in), the retentate (lumen out) and the permeate (shell side). Each runs from 12 V and outputs a voltage proportional to the pressure on its sensing element, 0 V at 0 bar to 5 V at 10 bar. The board's analog pins tolerate only 3.3 V, so each signal first passes a 15 kΩ / 22 kΩ divider, which scales 5 V down to about 2.97 V.

The microcontroller reads the three channels 100 times a second, each sample the sum of 16 readings to average out noise, and sends them to the computer in batches every 200 ms. The software converts each count to a pin voltage, undoes the divider and rescales 0 to 5 V onto 0 to 10 bar. The three pressures from the same instant are then combined as TMP = (Pfeed + Pretentate) / 2 − Ppermeate (Millipore Corporation, 2003), and the value reported is the median over a 30 second window.

The current system calculates TMP from feed, retentate, and permeate pressure. However, the August calibration runs below were recorded before the permeate channel was logged, so those data show lumen pressure drop rather than true TMP.

Conclusion

Flow and pressure can be measured reliably enough to act on. The flow meter matches manual measurement with R² = 0.995, and flow rises by about 22 mL/min per pump dial up to dial 50. The 4000 to 6000 s⁻¹ shear band we work to corresponds to 260 to 390 mL/min, or roughly dials 16 to 22, which is where the reactor is run.

That is enough to set the two alarms the farmer actually needs. A leak shows as a sudden drop at a fixed dial, well below the flow normally seen there, which is about 355 mL/min at dial 20. A blockage shows as pressure rising while flow does not move: with the module installed the drop runs about 0.19 to 0.37 bar across 260 to 390 mL/min, so we propose flagging anything above roughly 1.3 times that, about 0.24 to 0.48 bar, at the same flow. Both are thresholds proposed from these calibration runs; neither has yet fired on a real fault.

What the two readings still owe is a TMP. Every pressure figure in this chapter is the lumen drop, and the August 14 series stayed positive across 0.01 to 0.26 bar, which is consistent with filtrate being pushed out of the lumen but does not demonstrate it, because the permeate reading is not in those files. The permeate channel is now logged and the formula is in the software, so the next run on this module can close the one gap this chapter leaves open.

Hollow fiber membrane breach detector

The hollow fiber is where the culture and harvest stream touch, and a 0.2 µm pore is what keeps Bacillus subtilis on the lumen side. If breached, the cells would be able to come through, so a detector is installed on the shell side to watch for signs that may indicate unwelcomed particles.

Fitted, but no breach test is on record. It currently rests on an offline proxy that was run once. During version 1, 2 mL was drawn from the shell side and spread on LB agar, and no colonies were recovered, so no culturable B. subtilis reached the shell side in that run. It is a single plating, and the replicate count, incubation time and incubation temperature were not recorded.

No detectable B. subtilis colonies were observed in the shell-side sample under the tested conditions. That is preliminary evidence, for this one run, that the hollow fiber held the cells back.

What this plate can support is a containment claim for one run; it cannot stand in for the detector, because a plate takes a day to answer and an instrument has to answer while the reactor is running. The build is on Hardware → Bioreactor and the containment argument it belongs to is on Safety.

Software algorithm calibration

An OD600 value only becomes a reliable measurement if we understand how the raw light signal is converted into that number. We therefore opened the photometer firmware, traced each step in the calculation, and tested the resulting biases against a non-growing particle standard.

Unlike the other sections, which validate physical measurements against controls or standards, this section validates the software that processes the sensor data. Using the raw bursts from the SiO2 run, we replayed the signal through different reduction methods, tested how quantisation and bubbles affected the reported OD600, and defined acceptance rules for when a reading should be trusted or withheld. The full raw data and replay scripts are published so another team can repeat the analysis on their own photometer.

What the instrument had already recorded

Our 434-hour photometer run contained repeated step changes in OD600. Re-examining the raw data showed that these steps came from the reference channel, not from changes in the culture.

Between July 21 and August 8 the photometer logged 2671 valid readings. The reference channel remained near 32 sensor counts for most of the run. Because the detector resolves whole counts, low-count signals lose precision quickly: at 32 counts and a path-length factor of 5, a change of just one sensor count shifts the reported OD600 by 0.067.

The photometer's microcontroller firmware takes sixteen sensor readings for each measurement and averages the readings that pass the outlier filter. In principle, this averaging should preserve values between whole sensor counts. Instead, 91.5% of the logged reference readings fell exactly on an integer, showing that sub-count information was being lost before the value reached the log.

That loss appeared directly in the OD600 trace: between hours 40 and 230, the reference channel changed count 29 times, and 26 of those changes coincided with an OD600 jump greater than 0.04. This showed that the step changes came from the signal-processing pipeline rather than from the culture.

Reading the firmware explained the first figure. The outlier gate keeps samples within three median absolute deviations of the median. When more than half of sixteen samples share the same integer, the median absolute deviation is zero, so the fence has zero width and every sample that differs from the median is discarded as a bubble. The filter collapses into taking the most common count. Our firmware offers two further estimators in its interface, qn and biweight, and all three share a scale floor of 10−6, so on quantised data all three do the same thing. The rejection percentage the dashboard had been reporting as "bubbles" was, in that regime, counting quantisation flicker.

The hypothesis

Every step between the light and the number introduces a bias whose direction and size can be written down before anything is measured. We derived six of them: resolution lost at the sensor (ΔOD ≈ 0.434 k/N), the gap between averaging and taking the logarithm (0.217 kσr2), one-sided occlusion by bubbles, the degenerate outlier gate above, a stray-light ceiling at −k log10(s), and the delay a smoother adds. The sharpest prediction fell out of the third: if bubbles only block light, then an estimator that trusts the brighter half of each burst should scatter far less than a symmetric one. That prediction is what the calibration run was built to overturn or confirm.

One data set, eleven algorithms

To compare algorithms fairly, we recorded each raw sensor burst once and replayed every algorithm on the exact same input. This removes burst-to-burst variation, so any difference in output comes from the algorithm itself.

Switching algorithms between runs would give each one a different sensor burst, making the comparison confounded by burst-to-burst variation. We therefore modified the photometer firmware to log every individual sensor sample, record the active algorithm profile, and save the outputs needed for offline replay.

Eleven algorithms, including seven controls

The algorithm list, five evaluation metrics, selection rule, eight predictions, and exclusion criteria were committed to our repository before the first calibration measurement. Only documented hardware failures or operator errors could be excluded; data were not removed simply because they looked unusual.

Calibration against a non-growing standard

To separate instrument error from biological variation, we calibrated the photometers using a non-growing particle standard. On October 10, both photometers ran for 4.5 hours on a controlled SiO2 concentration ladder while every raw sensor sample was logged.

A concentration standard independent of the instrument

We used monodisperse 1 µm SiO2 microspheres, close to the size of a Bacillus subtilis cell. Forty-one concentrations were generated using a withdraw-one, add-one protocol, so concentration was determined from pipetted volume rather than from any photometer reading:

Cn = Cstock × [1 − ((V − 1)/V)n]

The concentration axis is defined entirely by pipetted volumes and the measured 50 mL loop volume, not by any instrument under test. The BioDrop therefore serves as an independent comparison rather than as the source of the calibration scale.

The Pro and Slim photometers ran in series on the same loop, so both measured the same suspension at the same moment. Raw bursts and all estimator outputs were logged for every reading.

As a software control, replaying the raw bursts reproduced all eight logged OD outputs within 0.005 OD, confirming that the offline analysis matches the firmware calculation.

In clean flow the estimator is not the lever

Across the forty concentrations with the loop running clean, the largest gap between any two of the eight estimators was 0.0039 OD on V1 and 0.0018 OD on V2. The residual scatter of our own August calibration was 0.024 OD, so in clean flow the choice of estimator is worth less than a fifth of the noise. H5 held. Everything that had been argued about in the interface turned out not to matter when nothing goes wrong.

With bubbles the premise fails

H6 predicted that upper, which trusts only the brighter half of each burst, would scatter at most half as much as mad. It scattered 0.77 against 0.78 on V1 and 0.15 against 0.16 on V2, and on V1 its mean error was twice that of mad. The reason is visible in the raw bursts: a bubble does not only block light. On V1, at OD 3.2, a bubble replaces a slice of scattering liquid with air and up to 2.8 times more light arrives; on V2, whose fiber collects over a wide angle, a bubble refracts light out of that cone and the reading darkens. The two units disagree in direction, so any estimator that assumes a direction is betting on the geometry it happens to be sitting in. No estimator put more than 35% of bubble readings within ±0.05 of the true level.

Worse, the firmware's own bubble flag catches the wrong failure. When the fourteen samples in a burst disagree, the flag fires on 94% of them on V1 and 79% on V2. That makes the failure detectable, if not correctable. When the flow cell fills completely with air the fourteen samples agree perfectly and are all wrong together: V1 read 0.74 to 0.91 against a true 3.18, and the flag caught none of it. Nothing inside such a burst looks abnormal. Only a comparison with the previous reading reveals it.

The lever is the gate after the estimator

We replayed the same data at the bioreactor's 30-second cadence through five reporting pipelines, from the configuration that was running on October 10 to one with two gates added: a spread gate that rejects a reading when mad and the 90th percentile disagree by more than 0.03 OD, and a jump gate that withholds any reading more than max(0.05 OD, 5%) from the last accepted value and accepts a new level only after three agreeing readings.

Testing reference normalization during a real disturbance

During the run, an external disturbance reduced both Slim channels to about 60% of their previous brightness. With reference normalization, the reported OD drifted by only −0.0005 OD/min, compared with 0.025 OD/min using the sample channel alone. That is the Slim's reference channel correcting a source-intensity change, caught in the act. The Pro reference channel did not show the same behavior, indicating that its two optical paths were not responding equally to the disturbance.

What this calibration does not establish

Silica calibrates the instrument, not the organism. A 1 µm bead is a sphere of refractive index 1.46 and density 2.3 g/cm³; a vegetative B. subtilis cell is a rod, often in chains, nearer 1.33 to 1.41 and 1.1 g/cm³. The ladder therefore converts a reading into equivalent particle counts and not into cell mass, and a dilution series of washed cells is still owed before the two can be related. The gates have a stated limit of their own: when an air slug lasts longer than 90 seconds, three agreeing readings make the wrong level acceptable. That is why line 3 above raises an alarm instead of silently correcting: a growing culture does not move 0.05 OD in 30 seconds, so any accepted step that large deserves a human look. On October 10 the real cause was an inlet tube that had fallen out of the beaker, and one alarm would have had someone put it back inside the first minute.

Optogenetic control

Can the light plate deliver a controlled signal for optogenetic activation? ReLEAF depends on light as the external control signal connecting environmental prediction to biological production. We therefore tested the light plate to determine whether it could provide reproducible illumination across the culture area.

A predicted environmental condition is turned into a light input that tunes production of ACCD in Bacillus subtilis through the CcaSR red and green light-sensing system (Castillo-Hair et al., 2019). For that to work, light has to act as a reliable dial. We need to know whether low, medium and high intensities give clearly separated levels of ACCD expression, and whether the light itself affects cell growth. The Light Plate Apparatus is an open-hardware, 24-well platform with independently programmable LEDs in every well (Gerhardt et al., 2016), so several intensities of red and green can run side by side in one experiment on one culture. Following the established CcaSR protocol for B. subtilis (Haller et al., 2025), we track the response over time with OD600 and Western blot. The settings that give a reproducible, graded response become the intensity levels ReLEAF uses to control ACCD production, and because every conclusion depends on the plate, the plate is what we characterized first.

What the measurement contributes

The Light Plate Apparatus is a general platform for characterizing light-inducible gene expression, not a CcaSR instrument. A team can survey light-responsive genetic modules beyond CcaSR and fit LEDs matched to each module's activating wavelength, which turns the same plate into a characterization tool for a wide range of optogenetic systems. What we add is the step before that: a worked method for matching cheap single-bulb LEDs by measured output, so the wells in one column receive the same light and the columns differ from one another by a measured amount.

The harder question for a team starting out is which LED to buy. Protectant assays

Three measurements follow the protectant from presence to quantity to function. Western blotting asks whether the intended protein is produced and secreted at the expected size. ELISA then measures how much target protein is present, while the DNPH assay tests whether ACC deaminase retains catalytic activity. These measurements answer different questions and are therefore used together rather than interchangeably.

A plant assay gives the most complete answer to whether a protectant helps the crop, but it is slower and more sensitive to variation in plant growth and stress conditions. These biochemical measurements let us evaluate production directly at the protein level before moving to plant testing.

They are ordered by cost. The Western blot is the cheap first screen: it says whether a band of the right size appears under a given production condition. Once the conditions are set, the ELISA is what turns that into a yield in µg/mL. Which assay settles a protectant then depends on what the molecule is.

  • Enzymes. ACC deaminase has measurable catalytic activity, so after confirming its presence, the DNPH assay can test whether the enzyme is still functional.
  • Non-enzymatic proteins. LEA14 does not catalyze a reaction, so its evaluation depends on measuring how much protein is present rather than enzyme activity. ELISA is therefore used for quantification.
  • Peptides. BoPep4 is too small to be resolved reliably by a standard Western blot, so ELISA is used as the main method for its detection and quantification.

Western blot

To verify whether the engineered Bacillus subtilis could produce and secrete protein or peptide products, we performed a Western blot to detect the secreted protein via the His tag we added at the C-terminus.

Western blotting separates proteins by molecular weight using polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, transfers them onto a PVDF membrane, and uses a specific antibody to visualize the target. Standard quantification assays return a total; they cannot tell our protein from the host's. The blot answers a narrower question that the total cannot: is a band present, and is it the size the construct predicts.

What the measurement contributes

Bioreactor supernatant is too dilute to blot as it comes, so the first thing this chapter contributes is the workflow that separates and concentrates it. With that in place, the anti-His antibody answers the two questions the blot is for: whether ACC deaminase is outside the cells at all, and whether the band sits where the construct says it should. We have written down the band positions that surprised us as well, since a fusion tag or incomplete denaturation moves the apparent weight and the next team should not have to rediscover that.

Sampling every two hours then turns the blot into a time course, which is how we found when production was highest before committing to the quantitative assays.

Competitive His-tag ELISA

To compare protein production across strains, constructs, and culture conditions, we needed a measurement that could quantify the target recombinant protein, rather than total protein in the sample. Bradford-type assays cannot distinguish our protectants from native Bacillus subtilis proteins, while Western blotting is better suited to confirming presence and molecular weight than assigning an absolute concentration. We therefore used ELISA to convert antibody binding into a quantitative protein concentration.

Why we chose a competitive ELISA

Several ELISA formats could be used for protein quantification, but they require different detection strategies. A sandwich ELISA can provide high specificity, but it requires a matched capture-and-detection antibody pair for each target. Direct and indirect formats are simpler, but would still depend on target-specific detection for each of our protectants.

We chose a competitive His-tag ELISA because ACC deaminase, LEA14, and BoPep4 all contain the same polyhistidine tag (Bornhorst & Falke, 2000). One antibody and one plate format therefore cover all three proteins, with no separate antibody system per target, and many constructs and culture conditions can be compared on a single plate.

How the measurement works

In this format, His-tagged protein in the sample competes with a labeled His tracer for antibody binding. As target concentration increases, the signal decreases, and a standard curve converts that signal into protein concentration.

What the measurement contributes

This assay adds the quantitative step that Western blotting cannot provide. It allows us to compare the amount of ACC deaminase, LEA14, and BoPep4 produced under different experimental conditions and to separate the question of how much protein is present from whether that protein is functional.

To evaluate the measurement itself, each plate includes a six-point standard curve, duplicate wells, blanks, non-specific-binding controls, and maximum-binding controls. These controls let us judge whether the assay is operating within its valid range before interpreting the samples.

The same workflow can also be transferred to other microbial systems expressing His-tagged recombinant proteins, making it useful beyond ReLEAF as a common quantification method across multiple targets.

ACC deaminase activity by DNPH assay

Western blotting shows whether ACC deaminase is present; the DNPH assay asks whether that protein is still catalytically active. ACC deaminase cleaves ACC into α-ketobutyrate and ammonia, so we measure the α-ketobutyrate produced as a direct readout of enzyme activity.

Under acidic conditions, DNPH reacts with the carbonyl group of α-ketobutyrate to form a hydrazone that is quantified at 540 nm (Penrose & Glick, 2003; Kozaeva et al., 2022). We chose this product-based measurement because ACC substrate does not interfere strongly with the readout, culture-medium background is lower than for ammonium detection, and the method is sensitive enough to compare enzyme activity across constructs and conditions.

Paired with expression measurements, it distinguishes protein that is merely present from protein that remains functional after secretion.

What the measurement contributes

We scaled the conventional protocol of Penrose and Glick (2003) down twenty-fold while holding the reaction proportions, which moves the assay onto a 96-well microplate and makes screening a strain library practical. With it we compared ACC deaminase activity across constructs, tested what pyridoxal 5'-phosphate supplementation does to that activity, and checked that recombinant ACCD secreted by our engineered Bacillus subtilis still functions outside the cell. We also set up the standard curve and the arithmetic that converts an absorbance into an α-ketobutyrate concentration, a total product yield and an activity, so the comparison between strains rests on a stated calculation.

  • Hydrochloric acid, 0.56 N. Dilute concentrated HCl (12 N) with ultrapure water. Used to quench the reaction and to stabilize the acid-catalyzed step.
  • Sodium hydroxide, 2 N. Dissolve 8.0 g of NaOH pellets in deionized water to a total volume of 100 mL. The reaction is exothermic, so prepare it in an ice bath.
  • ACC substrate, 0.5 M. Dissolve 50.55 mg ACC per 1.0 mL deionized water, store at −20 °C, and pre-equilibrate at 30 °C for at least 3 minutes before use.

Reading the light switch as activity rather than as a band

The optogenetic work in the light plate section asks whether green light turns ACC deaminase on, and answers it with blots: expression rises with time under green but does not order itself by intensity, and red at full power is not silent. A blot can only say a protein is there. This assay can say whether what is there is working, so we ran the same question through it three times, on cultures held in the dark and then illuminated. All three are single plates of a few wells, and the error bars are technical replicates within a plate.

Green light does something, and it does not last. Three hours after the lamp comes on, the illuminated culture is at about 19.7 µg/mL of α-ketobutyrate; three hours later it is at about 5. Whatever the switch releases is spent, degraded or outrun by the culture within the first few hours, which is a different failure from never switching at all and matters for a reactor that is meant to dose on demand.

How long the culture has grown changes which color wins. At 13 hours, green returns 0.1249 mM against red's 0.0089 mM, a fourteenfold separation and the cleanest light response this assay has produced. At 16 hours the order inverts: red reads 0.0923 mM and green 0.0130 mM. Three hours of extra culture is enough to turn a fourteenfold green preference into a red one, so how long the cells have grown sets what the assay reads as much as the color does. Neither point is repeated, and the 13 hour green error bar runs from about 5 to 16 on the activity scale, so the separation is wide but the value is not.

With a shorter pre-culture, both colors climb together. Twelve hours in the dark and then light gives the one monotonic series in this set: every illuminated group is above the pre-illumination baseline of about 2.8 µg/mL, and both arms rise at 3, 6 and 8 hours to end near 18 and 16. Green sits above red at each matched time in the figure, but the gap is inside the error bars at 3 and 8 hours, and the draft's own numeric write-up of this plate puts red above green at those two points. We have not quoted a fold difference per color here because the two records disagree on which way round it runs; what the plate supports is that illumination raises activity several-fold over a dark baseline, and that it keeps rising through 8 hours.

Taken together these three runs say more than the blots do and less than the circuit needs. Activity above a dark baseline is real and repeats across plates. A clean, ordered, color-specific response is not: it appears at 13 hours, inverts at 16, and washes out against red in the pre-cultured series. The honest statement is that the switch moves ACC deaminase activity and that we have not yet established the conditions under which it moves it predictably. The experiment that would settle it is the obvious one and is not in the record: the same culture, one maturation time, both colors, repeated.

This method is also what read the reactor permeate on 7 and 8 October, where it gave the only activity figure the project has for material coming out of the bioreactor, 7.25 mU/mL. Those readings are reported under the runs on Digital Twin and carried into the validation table on Development; like the plates here, each is a single sample.

Plant assays

Four measurements share two growing platforms. The agar assay sets the stress and the dose on a flat surface; the hydroponic assay puts the same protectants in front of a root system standing in liquid. The chlorophyll extraction reads how much photosynthetic machinery survived either one, and the physical measurements give the root length and the fresh weight that every chlorophyll number is normalized against.

Agar plate stress test, horizontal and vertical

To know whether a protectant rescues a stressed plant, and whether the timing of the dose matters, we needed a platform where the stress dose, the protectant and the timing could each be changed one at a time. Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings on ½ Murashige and Skoog agar (Murashige & Skoog, 1962) are that platform.

Salinity stress damages plants in readable ways. Exposure of Arabidopsis to NaCl alters stress-response gene expression (Cheng et al., 2013; Rolly et al., 2020) and, at the whole-seedling level, stunts root growth, bleaches leaves through loss of chlorophyll (chlorosis), and turns leaves purple through anthocyanin accumulation. Stress ethylene contributes to these effects as it inhibits root elongation (Růžička et al., 2007) and impairs photosynthesis (Ceusters & Van De Poel, 2018), which is the reason why the bacterial enzyme ACC deaminase (ACCD), which breaks down the ethylene precursor ACC, is one of our protectants (Glick, 2014). With the damage showing up in root length differences, biomass and leaf pigment, a protectant that works should be able to lengthen roots, raise biomass, and preserve chlorophyll compared with a stressed and untreated plate.

Agar buys three things soil cannot. Every seedling gets identical nutrients and an exact stress dose, where soil varies in moisture, pH and minerals from pot to pot. Physiological changes show within days, so many conditions can be screened quickly. And it fixes the salinity window, the protectant dose and the protocol before any of them are carried into soil.

Vertical square plates

The plates are designed to let roots grow along the surface under gravity, so primary root length and lateral roots can be measured from photographs. We used them for the trehalose experiments in Phase 1 and planned them for the heat tests.

Horizontal circular plates

The plates are designed to hold liquid protectant on the agar surface, which vertical plates cannot fulfill. We used them for the CH bio peptide and ACCD experiments in Phases 2 and 3, where treatment timing of salinity was a variable (pre-treatment, co-treatment, post-treatment). Horizontal circular plates also provide higher capacities of n values in each trial which minimizes inconsistencies; for example, vertical square plates can allow the testing of around 9 to 10 Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings while horizontal circular plates can accommodate around 40 seedlings.

The experiments ran in three phases, ordered so that each one checked the platform before the next relied on it. Phase 1 used trehalose, a disaccharide reported to protect Arabidopsis under salinity stress (Kim et al., 2025), to find a NaCl level that gives a readable signal and to confirm the platform can show a rescue. Phase 2 used the CH Bio peptide, a commercial product from CH Biotech, as the reference protectant and to test application timing. Phase 3 used ACC deaminase, LEA14, and BoPep4 secreted by our engineered Bacillus subtilis. A heat-stress version of the assay, based on published thermopriming protocols (Lee et al., 2023; Auverlot et al., 2025), was designed for the same protectants.

What the platform is useful for

Seedlings on agar are one of the most widely used formats in plant biology and the platform described here applies beyond our project in several ways.

  • Screening for salt-tolerance of plants. Arabidopsis grown on agar supplemented with NaCl is a standard way to test how genes, mutants, or treatments change salt tolerance (Cheng et al., 2013; Rolly et al., 2020). Our dose-response data tested salinity concentrations of 75, 100, 150 and 250 mM NaCl, read against the control group on the same plate. At 75 mM, fresh weight and total chlorophyll stayed near control levels. At 100 mM growth was clearly reduced and the rescue effect was still visible, which is what makes it the working concentration. At 150 mM growth was stunted to about the same degree as at 100 mM but with little rescue, total chlorophyll fell sharply, and anthocyanin accumulated enough to purple the leaves. At 250 mM close to every sample showed chlorosis and severe bleaching and none recovered. The boundaries are approximate percentages of control and not fixed thresholds, because each trial differed. The discovery allows future researchers to skip the pretests and begin in the 75 to 100 mM window where growth is reduced but rescue is still visible.
  • Testing plant growth-promoting bacteria and biostimulants. Microbes that lower plant ethylene with ACC deaminase are used to promote plant growth under stress (Glick, 2014). Plate assays are a common way to test bacterial effects on Arabidopsis stress tolerance (Lee et al., 2023). Our design compares bacterial products at defined timings and with equal liquid controls. The standardized protocols allow a reusable screen for any protectant testing, so other teams and researchers can test their own molecule or microbe with the same readouts, plates, and analysis.
  • Testing exogenous protective compounds. Sugars such as trehalose have been tested on Arabidopsis thaliana under salinity stress (Kim et al., 2025). Our trehalose sets show how to find the usable dose range of a candidate compound. Too high of concentrations might inhibit and harm plants as well because it triggers the toxic accumulation of an intermediate signaling metabolite, trehalose-6-phosphate (T6P), which disrupts normal carbon allocation and energy signaling, causing growth arrest such as stunted roots and undeveloped leaves (Schluepmann et al., 2004).
  • Heat-stress assays. The same plates and readouts can be used with thermopriming protocols (Lee et al., 2023; Auverlot et al., 2025).
  • Timing studies for delivery driven by sensors. The protectant is applied before, with, or after stress; therefore, the platform can test whether early delivery matters. This directly connects to the question behind AIoT-based stress prediction and sensing in the field.
  • Teaching and iGEM labs. The equipment is inexpensive (plates, a camera in a light box, a balance, and free ImageJ software) and every step is described below in the procedural steps.
  • Honest record of failure modes. Especially for contamination issues or failure to see effectiveness in protectant doses, so other teams can avoid repeating them as to also provide improvements and suggestions to protocols for higher quality readouts.

Results

Across eight experimental sets the agar platform answered the three questions it was built for. The stress dose has a usable window: rescue is readable between 75 and 100 mM NaCl, 150 mM stunts growth to roughly the same degree as 100 mM but leaves little room for a protectant to show an effect, and 250 mM bleached close to every seedling past recovery. Protectant dose matters in both directions, with 1 mM trehalose toxic and 0.5 mM too weak before the range was narrowed. And timing is readable: in the heat series above, ACC deaminase applied a day before the stress gave the longest roots of the four timing arms, which is the observation the whole delivery argument depends on.

Hydroponic stress test

We transplanted Arabidopsis into a hydroponic box to evaluate the efficacy of protectants on plants. We use hydroponics to test the effectiveness because we can control the environmental factors precisely. The agar in the lid and the medium in the box between them feed the plant completely, and humidity and temperature are held in a constant range, so the outer conditions cannot move a result.

Agar settles the dose and the timing on a flat surface; hydroponics is where the same protectants meet a root system standing in liquid, which is the condition a field application has to work in. The two platforms share their readouts, so a number taken here can be set beside a number taken on a plate. What hydroponics does not give is root length: the roots grow into the well and cannot be photographed against a scale, so the readouts are fresh weight, chlorophyll and the daily photographs.

Two vessels

Transparent tip box. After testing several prototypes of the hydroponic box, we settled on a 1000 µL tip box with a customized 3D printed board. The box is transparent, which lets us watch the plants and contains thirteen Arabidopsis seedlings. We used the tip box for the pretests of salinity and heat stress rescue by the CH peptide.

Tube rack. We first used the tube rack only to grow seedlings before an experiment began, then began running experiments in it directly. One rack accommodates forty-eight plants, enough to test ACCD and BoPep4 against both salinity and heat stress on a single rack. We moved to the rack because of time, and the plants in it grew at least as well as those in the tip box with less contamination. The build history of both vessels is on Plants; what follows is the measurement they supply.

What the platform is useful for

Hydroponics fixes nutrient availability, water purity and stress intensity, which is why a team doing protectant work might want it. We built two vessels and record the materials, the assembly, the nutrient medium and the environmental conditions for both, so either can be rebuilt. Four boxes failed before the fifth, a 1000 µL tip box with a 3D printed board and a black cover to keep light off the medium; that one we offer as a prototype to modify, with the caution that it is less stable than the rack.

Both stress protocols come with a dose already found. The salt concentration is from Cortés-Jiménez et al. (2014), and after several pretests 100 mM NaCl is where plants are damaged and a protectant can still bring them back. For heat we followed the thermopriming designs of Lee et al. (2023) and Auverlot et al. (2025), the same two sources the agar assay uses, and exposed plants at 37 °C and 42 °C. Nothing at 42 °C survived the day; at 37 °C the CH peptide rescued them. Start at 37 °C and treat 42 °C as past recovery.

The dilution work gives a ceiling that transfers without qualification: the carrier medium, and not the protectant, sets the strongest dose a plant will take. We tested 1/40, 1/60, 1/80 and 1/100 of both protectants; the plants tolerate all four, and the team reads 1/40, 1/60 and 1/80 as the best rescue, ACCD under heat and BoPep4 under both heat and salinity.

One finding was not something we set out to measure. Plants in the tube rack were contaminated less often than plants in the tip box, and our explanation is the transfer step: tip box plants were moved off the rack at four days old and, although a group would usually last three or four days after that, most ended in severe contamination. Roots in the rack are never handled.

Finding a dilution the plants survive

TB at full strength kills the plants, so the first run was a pretest on TB medium carrying B. subtilis and no protectant, to separate the carrier from anything the protectant might do. We tested 1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 and 1/16, with 0 as the control, at pH 5.7 and pH 7. The optimum for Arabidopsis is 5.7 and ACCD works best at about 7.5 (Liu et al., 2022), which Arabidopsis will not tolerate, so 7 is the compromise we tested against.

Every TB concentration in the pretest was too strong for the plants to survive, including 1/16. That is a failed rescue window, and it is still the reading that set the next run: the damage falls off steadily with dilution, so the usable range had to be below 1/16. The pH arm came back clean, though. Plants at pH 7 were no worse than plants at 5.7, so every later ACCD run was made up at pH 7, which is as close to the enzyme's own optimum as the plants will go.

The salinity run that followed tested ACCD at 1/10, 1/20, 1/40 and 1/60 against 0 ACCD, each with and without 100 mM NaCl, and carried a separate group of 1/20 TB alone at both salt levels. The spacing was deliberate: the pretest had already ruled out everything down to 1/16, so most of the new series went below that, and 1/10 was kept as the single dose stronger than anything the pretest tolerated, to check that the damage really did continue to rise with concentration rather than plateau. We had expected 1/20 to be dilute enough for the plants to live and 1/40 and 1/60 to be almost without effect.

The expectation was wrong in one place and right in the other. The 1/20 WB800 negative control itself showed a clear stress response, smaller plants and anthocyanin accumulation, so 1/20 of the carrier affects growth on its own. ACCD at 1/10 and 1/20 bleached leaves even at 0 mM NaCl, while 1/40 did not, and the 1/40 group carried the highest fresh weight and chlorophyll content of the unstressed groups. We therefore fixed 1/40 as the strongest dose in every later run, and these two runs together narrowed the working range from undiluted TB to 1/40 through 1/100.

BoPep4 across the same dilutions, under salt

BoPep4 was then run over 1/40, 1/60, 1/80 and 1/100, with and without 100 mM NaCl. The series starts at 1/40 because that was the strongest dose the ACCD salinity run tolerated. The controls are the same two: 1/40 WB800, which is the carrier without a protectant, and 0.1× MS medium alone.

Without salt, the BoPep4 wells show slight anthocyanin accumulation at every dilution from 1/40 to 1/100, while neither 1/40 WB800 nor plain 0.1× MS medium reddens. On an unstressed plant, in other words, BoPep4 looks mildly costly rather than beneficial.

Under 100 mM NaCl the result reverses. The reading we take from the set is narrow: BoPep4 at 1/40 improves salinity tolerance, and the weaker dilutions do not separate from their controls.

Both readouts are single plates with no technical replication, so the gap between 1/40 BoPep4 and the rest of the salted block is what the run supports, and the ordering among 1/60, 1/80 and 1/100 is not.

Chlorophyll extraction

To assess the physiological response of Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings under stress, and evaluate protectant treatment rescue effectiveness in our project, chlorophyll extraction is introduced as a precise tool of measurement to strengthen the analysis of each experiment's results.

Chlorophyll is the pigment that captures light energy for photosynthesis, so how much of it a leaf contains tracks how well the plant is growing (Roberts, 2023). The metabolic pathways that govern the chlorophyll biosynthesis are complex, which abiotic stress such as salinity stress, drought, extreme temperature variations can substantially perturb these processes (Li et al., 2024).

As connected to our project, we mainly focus on two abiotic stress types: salinity and heat. Given this category, these abiotic stressors trigger the overproduction of reactive oxygen species, leading to lipid peroxidation and chlorosis. Hence, extracting the chlorophyll as a form of data readout and measurement allows quantification on the degree of chlorosis, and permits accurate evaluation of plant physiological response in our experiments that cannot be visually and directly assessed through bare eyes.

Comparison of fresh weight-normalized and non-normalized chlorophyll content

Dividing by fresh weight is the standard way to report chlorophyll, and it is the wrong way round when stress has destroyed the biomass that sits in the denominator. The case that showed us this is the ACC deaminase heat stress assay described in the physical measurements chapter, and the numbers below come from that one experiment.

Seedlings given no protectant and held at 37 °C for 5 days lost 92 to 97% of their fresh weight: the stressed groups weighed about 0.02 g against about 0.67 g unstressed, weighed per group on the analytical balance. Expressed per gram, those flattened seedlings then appeared to contain about three times more chlorophyll than the unstressed plants, 0.366 against 0.121 mg g−1, computed from the same absorbance readings as every other value in this chapter. Total chlorophyll in the extract, the same readings without the division, had fallen by about 91%. The two measures point in opposite directions, and only one of them agrees with the photographs.

Fresh weight also includes roots, which contain no chlorophyll, so a per-gram value moves with the shoot-to-root ratio as well as with pigment. We therefore report both measures and choose between them by the situation: chlorophyll per gram of fresh weight where biomass is comparable across groups, and total chlorophyll in the extract where it is not.

The physical measurements of Arabidopsis thaliana

Alongside the chlorophyll extraction data collection, both fresh weight and root length data have been collected for selected experimental sets.

What went wrong, and what we changed

Plates leaning back in the light box shortened the roots. A plate resting against the back of the box is photographed at an angle, which foreshortens everything on it. We 3D printed a rack that keeps a 12 to 13 cm square plate upright, so the plate is square to the camera and the ruler beside it calibrates ImageJ at the distance it claims to be.

Reflections obscured the measurement. Turning off the room lights and covering the opening of the light box with black cloth was not enough, because the box's own lights reflect off the plate. Moving the rack within the box and changing its distance from the camera left a reflection only at the extreme edges, clear of the roots.

Roots grew over each other in the denser groups. In the control groups especially, the primary root could not be told from its neighbors. For the last two or three sets we lifted the plants onto a thin sheet of black foam, laid parallel; pulled from the agar they clump, and the primary root is the longest, so it can be followed. Those photographs were taken from directly overhead with a 15 cm ruler beside the plants for calibration.

For future projects

Many of the methods above were developed because existing tools did not answer the measurement question we had. We documented them so other teams can reuse the same approaches with different organisms, stresses, and instruments.

  • Audit the signal chain before calibrating the instrument. Our 434-hour run showed that integer sensor counts were creating artificial steps in OD600. Plotting the raw reference-channel distribution is a quick way to check whether averaging is preserving sub-count resolution before interpreting a long time course.
  • Replay algorithms on identical raw data. Log the individual samples inside each burst and every estimator can be tested on exactly the same input, which takes burst-to-burst variation out of the comparison. Any flow-cell sensor that can print its raw bursts can be worked on this way.
  • Add quality control after estimation. In clean flow, estimator choice changed OD by less than 0.004, while post-estimation spread and jump gates reduced the largest bubble error from 2.44 to 0.016 OD. For continuous optical measurements, deciding whether a reading should be accepted can matter more than choosing among similar estimators.
  • Calibrate turbidity with a non-growing particle standard. Our SiO2 ladder sets concentration from pipetted volumes, not from a second instrument, which separates optical bias from biological variation.
  • Measure LED output before assigning light treatments. LEDs sold as identical were not. Measuring each one and grouping them by output is what gave us defined light levels to dose with.
  • Report chlorophyll with biomass context. Normalizing by fresh weight alone can turn the trend upside down when stress has destroyed the biomass in the denominator. Print the total from the extract beside the per-gram value and the artifact is visible.
  • Establish the stress window before testing rescue. Our pretests found the conditions where stress reads without swamping the assay: 75–100 mM NaCl on agar, 37 °C in hydroponics. Above those, the damage does not come back.

Several of these recommendations came directly from measurements that behaved differently from what we expected. That is also why the raw data, controls, and analysis methods are included throughout this page: another team should be able to see what worked, and also how we knew when a measurement had to change.

Conclusion

Could another team repeat these measurements?

Every chapter gives its own materials, its quantities per plate or per well, and its procedure in the order the steps are carried out, so each measurement can be rebuilt without reference to us. The runs that failed are reported beside the runs that worked, because a protocol that does not say how it stopped failing cannot be repeated: the 14 August pressure series does not reproduce from one run to the next, five of our eight pre-registered predictions about the photometer were overturned by our own data, and heat stress in hydroponic culture flattened the treated plants along with the controls. For the measurement that is wholly ours we publish the raw material instead of describing it. The CSVs from both photometer builds, both bubble tests, the hand-written log sheet, the eleven-algorithm replay script, the line-for-line firmware ports and the pre-registration are in od600-signal-processing/ on the iGEM GitLab, and that protocol runs in four working days on any photometer that can be made to log the individual samples inside a burst. The limits are stated where they apply: the plant stress windows rest on one plate per group, and the reactor concentration and activity time courses on single wells.

Is the protocol well described?

Each block names its materials with the manufacturer and the catalog or lot number where one exists, gives the amount needed per plate or per well, and then lists the steps. The arithmetic is described to the same depth as the bench work. The conversion from illuminance to optical density is given as equations, including the path-length factor of 5.0 that converts a 2 mm flow cuvette onto the 1 cm basis OD600 is quoted on, and the firmware that performs it is ported line for line into the analysis, so what is tested is what ships, not a description of it. Image analysis is calibrated from a 150 mm ruler standing in every photograph, so the scale comes from an object in the frame instead of an assumed camera distance. Eleven problems that shaped the agar sets are tabulated with the set each appeared in and what we changed in response. No protocol on this page is offered as a separate file to download; each is written out in full where it is used.

Is it useful to other projects?

Each measurement block closes with what transfers out of ReLEAF. The central one is an in-line OD600 measurement that needs no instrument-specific transform fitted against a benchtop spectrophotometer: the raw output agrees with the benchtop reference to a median paired difference of 3.8%, and on the silica run its slope against that reference is 1.001, consistent with unit scale, so another team can rebuild the instrument and use the number it gives. Four further pieces are written for lifting out. The burst filter and the two acceptance gates took the worst bubble error from 2.44 to 0.016 OD and apply to any flow-cell sensor. The silica ladder calibrates a turbidity reading against a particle that does not grow, on a concentration axis set by pipetted volumes. The agar platform is a general screen for protectants and plant growth-promoting bacteria, with the usable stress window already found, 75 to 100 mM NaCl, so another team need not repeat the pretests. And chlorophyll is reported both per gram and in total, because per-gram normalization inflates the number when stress has suppressed biomass, by a factor of three in one of our own heat assays while total chlorophyll had fallen by about 91%.

Were controls used to validate the process and calibrate the units?

Every optical reading here is referenced to a blank, and the blank is refused if either sensor is saturated, if too few readings survive the filter or if either channel reads zero, so the software returns no OD rather than an uncorrected one. The units are tied to physical quantities and not to another instrument: the path-length factor converts the flow cuvette to the conventional 1 cm basis, and the concentration axis of both calibration runs is set by weighed mass and pipetted volume. The instrument was then checked against two independent mass standards eight weeks apart, titanium dioxide on 23 August and 1 µm silica on 10 October, with a benchtop BioDrop reading in parallel at every step. Those comparisons are tested, not asserted: against the reference the slope is 1.001, p = 0.98 against a null of unit slope, while the intercept is −0.123 OD, p = 0.005 against a null of zero, which locates the one remaining discrepancy in the stored blank and not in the optics. The flow meter was validated against outflow caught by hand over the same dials, R² = 0.995. In the assays the controls are a plate control on every set, equal liquid volumes in the untreated arms, an unstressed-plus-protectant arm to separate rescue from toxicity, blank-subtracted ELISA readings quoted as %B/B₀ against a standard curve with the coefficient of variation between replicate wells, and DNPH values taken net of the untransformed WB800 host background and read only over the five standard levels at and below 2.049 mM. Where a control defeated the measurement we report that as the result: the untransformed WB800 control returned 5.452 and 4.562 µg/mL in the ELISA, the same range as four of the six constructs beside it, so no difference in that table can be attributed to expression.
