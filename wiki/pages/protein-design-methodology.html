Three protectants run through one pipeline: the peptide BoPEP4, the folded protein LEA14 and the enzyme ACC deaminase. Each arrives as a sequence and leaves as coordinates with a grade attached. GRAFT predicts the complex a molecule forms with whatever it has to act on, grades that prediction against experimental structure where one exists, and hands the verdict back to design. It answers whether a molecule can engage its partner; whether that engagement produces a biological response is a question for the bench. Five stages, each built around one or two load-bearing modules, with supporting tools feeding them and a redesign loop running back from the verdict.

What this model cannot tell you

The limits are part of the model. A well-scored, stereochemically clean complex says the sequence is capable of the molecular recognition step: the one thing this kind of modeling is able to answer. It does not say the peptide is taken up, survives the apoplast, or that binding fires the downstream immune output. Pattern-triggered immunity is read out in the cell, not in the coordinates, so the redesign loop can only ever optimize for predicted binding, never for function.

Established here

Shape complementarity, plausible interface chemistry, a ΔG estimate, and reproduction of known anchor contacts.

Not established

Ca2+ influx, ROS burst, MAPK activation, PEPR1–BAK1 phosphotransfer, growth inhibition.

Only in the wet lab

Binding assay (ITC / MST / SPR), receptor co-immunoprecipitation, and a functional defense-marker readout.

Two parts of the map are flagged ◇ inferred: the PEPR2 structure, and every PEPR–BAK1 interface. Both rest on homology plus convergent conservation evidence. Report them as models, and say so in the figure legend.

1Introduction

1.1 Background

Computational tools give real insight into protein engineering and act as decision-making engines for wet lab work, but the process is easy to oversimplify: convenient analysis of inputs and outputs in place of thorough analysis, and no methodology tailored to the project it serves.

Their outputs depend heavily on the quality of inputs, parameters and underlying assumptions, and that dependence is worse for short, disordered peptides such as Brassica oleracea Plant Elicitor Peptide 4 (BoPEP4), one of our three protectants alongside Late Embryogenesis Abundant protein 14 (LEA14) and 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate deaminase (ACC deaminase). A workflow that holds up has to account for how and why these tools generate their predictions, not merely run them.

The same workflow runs on all three protectants, with the evidence available deciding how far each one gets. For BoPEP4 it predicts and evaluates the ternary signaling complex of the receptor-like kinase PEPR1, the co-receptor kinase BAK1 and the peptide itself. For LEA14 it co-folds the protein against its F-box partner AtPP2-B11, with ASK1 added as a third chain. For ACC deaminase it works from a crystal structure of the enzyme with cofactor and substrate bound, and the engineering question is stability, not recognition. The workflow was designed on the principles of integrative structural biology established by Alexandre Bonvin at Utrecht University, whose lab produced most of the software below.

2.1 Choosing the protectants

Candidate selection begins with the functional requirement, not with a molecule, and surveys gene families instead of single proteins, because a family groups sequences that arose by duplication and therefore tend to share a mechanism even where triggers differ. Four criteria set the shortlist: mechanism match against the stress actually targeted, assayability in the available wet lab, engineerability, and novelty against risk. Candidates failing one are recorded as considered and excluded, because the exclusions define the design space as much as the selections do.

The PEP family, and why BoPEP4 over AtPEP1

The BoPROPEP family tree identified candidate PEP peptides including BoPEP4, AtPEP1 and AtPEP3, and let us place BoPEP4 in relation to Arabidopsis instead of taking the peptide directly from the plant. PEP-family peptides are perceived by the same pair of receptor kinases, PEPR1 and PEPR2, so receptor recognition is conserved across the family: a reasonable first signal for which candidates behave similarly, before any experiment.

The best-characterized peptide was not assumed to be the best target. AtPEP1 is known mainly for biotic stress, while the project needed measurable abiotic stress, which weather data and sensors detect well. Drought is prominent in Taiwan and is directly signaled by BoPEP4. Abiotic focus also gives direct plant phenotypes such as root length and biomass, where biotic assays would add a second organism and more confounders.

Finally, BoPEP4's variable N-terminal region alongside its conserved C-terminal motif (SSG-x2-G-x2-N) allows targeted N-terminal edits while preserving the conserved core, and its relative obscurity means the sequence variants are genuinely uncharacterised.

The LEA family, and why LEA14

LEA proteins are associated with abiotic stress and can show substantial intrinsic disorder: some lack a single stable fold and adopt different conformations depending on environment or partner. Ordered or globular members were therefore separated from intrinsically disordered ones, because mutation methods designed for folded proteins cannot be read the same way for disordered peptides, and the family contains both.

The ordered, globular LEA14 was chosen, with demonstrated protection against drought, salinity and osmotic stress. Its defined structure gives a stable scaffold for the protein-protein interactions involved in molecular stabilization. Unlike BoPEP4, it does not depend on recognizing one dedicated receptor; its stabilization function can involve many cellular proteins, so a larger structured protein suits the question better. A disordered LEA would instead act through broader, less specific molecular-shield mechanisms.

It also gives a measurable phenotype for wet lab testing, and at over 150 residues it poses a fundamentally different design problem from a short receptor-binding peptide.

ACC deaminase

Suggested by Stanford bioengineering assistant professor Jenn Brophy in interview. It fills a real gap: both LEA14 and BoPEP4 fail against heat stress, one of Taiwan's largest abiotic stresses.

The enzyme is largely specific to ACC, the direct precursor of the plant hormone ethylene. Acting on that one signaling molecule lets it reduce stress-induced ethylene accumulation, and so the growth inhibition that follows, without interfering with other cellular chemistry. It also functions both inside plant cells and outside the roots, which widens the application routes.

Considered and set aside: proline, CAP peptides

Trehalose was set aside despite its established role in osmotic tolerance: it is already heavily engineered in synthetic biology and offers little differentiation. Proline was excluded because excessive accumulation is associated with cytotoxic rather than purely protective effects, a risk for a system producing protectant continuously. CAP-derived peptides were deprioritised on timeline, since synthesis and validation would not fit the development window.

2.2 Literature: understanding the mechanism

Literature review here supplies the parameters every later computational step needs, and establishes whether a design is new. Three source classes do the work: sequence databases for canonical sequence and domain boundaries, structural databases for coordinates and the residue range actually resolved, and primary functional literature for the assays and effect sizes. A design citing only the first two can be built but cannot be interpreted.

Tang et al. 2015

Resolved the AtPEP1-receptor structure and showed the peptide binding in an extended conformation. This is why our docking inputs treat BoPEP4 as a set of possible conformational states instead of one rigid structure, and it is the reference the receptor-binding interface is read against.

Yu et al.

Covers broader strategies for designing and engineering plant-derived peptides. It supported truncation and residue substitution as exploratory axes, and it was used to identify which modifications would produce interpretable changes, since not every mutation is equally informative.

Pearce et al. 2008

An alanine scan of AtPep1(9–23) and the basis for several of our experimental design concepts. It motivated systematic peptide modification to establish which residues contribute to activity, and it later settled a design question directly: [A18] is fully active in the alkalinization assay at 2.5 nM, ten times the EC50.

Truncation series, as a method

A truncation series progressively removes portions of a sequence, so comparing variants of different lengths shows whether a region is necessary for the expected interaction. Truncation was therefore both an engineering strategy and a mechanistic probe.

Alanine scanning, as a method

Replaces selected residues with alanine, keeping a simple side chain while removing the original chemistry. The purpose is diagnostic: a substitution that degrades the measured interaction marks a load-bearing position, and one that does not marks a degenerate position available for other modification. With truncation it is the conceptual foundation of the mutation-design workflow.

2.3 Interviews

Expert consultation is only useful if the record separates three things that are easy to merge: what the expert stated, what they recommended, and what was decided afterwards. An open-ended question returns an open-ended answer that cannot be acted on.

Dr. Jenn Brophy, Stanford Bioengineering

Her interview shaped decisions beyond the choice of ACC deaminase. Asked how to apply stress-sensor and protectant-synthesis logic so plants tolerate heat and salinity, she recommended an OR-gate architecture, and advised working backward from application needs: select a logic gate that is activated under the conditions you care about rather than starting from circuit design. She pointed to two- and three-input logic gates in E. coli as a resource.

Her ACC deaminase recommendation was scoped to drought and heat. She made no direct claim about salinity, so the OR-gate was researched further to see whether it extends to both heat and salinity.

2.4 Protectant families: sequence alignment

Sequence-level analysis is the cheapest first pass at conservation, variability and potential engineering sites, before any structural modeling. It identifies candidate positions without ranking them, since comparison alone cannot separate a position that varies because it is unimportant from one where two peptides solve the same problem differently.

PEP sequence alignment and BoPEP4

Multiple AtPEP sequences were aligned to find shared residues and conserved patterns, then compared with BoPEP4. That gives both halves of the engineering brief at once: conserved residues are features to preserve, and the positions where they differ are candidates for mutation and truncation. The AtPEP1–PEPR1 structure provides the context for reading those conserved residues, especially in the C-terminal region that makes concentrated receptor contacts.

Both peptides are 23 residues and align without gaps, so every position has a direct counterpart and numbering transfers one to one. This is what allows AtPEP1 structural data to be read onto BoPEP4 at all.

Ten positions are identical: 7, 9, 12, 15, 16, 17, 19, 20, 22 and 23, all in the C-terminal region. Identity is 10 of 23 across the full peptide, 10 of 17 within residues 7–23, and zero across residues 1–6. That boundary matters because an independent method draws it in the same place: 5GR8 resolves AtPep1 only from residue 7, and our contact analysis found both PEPR1 and PEPR2 engage residues 7–23 while the first six make no receptor contact.

BoPEP4 also conforms to the conserved motif SSG-x2-G-x2-N at positions 15–23: Ser15, Ser16, Gly17, Gly20 and Asn23 are all present. The two positions differing inside that window, 18 and 21, both fall in the motif's degenerate positions. The peptide satisfies the family signature and differs only where the family permits variation.

The N-terminal divergence is chemical as well as positional. AtPEP1 opens with ATKVKA, charged; BoPEP4 opens with GILIGS, uncharged and hydrophobic. The whole segment differs in character, not at scattered positions.

Across the three engineering axes the alignment fixed a starting point for each. N-terminal truncation is supported by two independent lines, zero conservation and zero receptor contact across 1–6. Point substitution has a defined candidate set inside 7–23, with positions 18 and 8 differing from AtPEP1. The conserved repeat analysis has a defined unit at 15–23. Which of those candidates survived is reported in Sprints and Results.

Why a family alignment was not run for LEA14

Sequence conservation alone was less informative for the questions being asked. The LEA family contains substantial structural diversity, including both intrinsically disordered and folded members, so similar sequence patterns do not necessarily correspond to equivalent structural or functional roles.

LEA14 was instead analyzed as a specific folded protein: its experimentally characterized structure, residue environment, interaction surfaces, and the vulnerabilities created by our delivery route. That let us connect each proposed mutation to a defined structural hypothesis. The two protectants therefore needed different forms of sequence analysis.

2.5 Engineering axes

Designed substitutions fall into two categories, and the distinction determines how each is justified. Interface substitutions aim to change a contact and are usually drawn from a structurally characterized homolog, so the substituted residue is one already observed to work in that position. Liability substitutions aim to remove a predicted failure mode: a specific chemical feature that a specific process condition is expected to attack.

BoPEP4 axes
Truncations

The uncharged six-residue GILIGS segment carries zero conservation and zero receptor contact, making the N-terminus the only end that can be trimmed. The C-terminus is off limits: Asn23's free carboxylate is the anchor, and deleting the last residue is documented to compromise binding. Fewer residues to synthesize and export also implies faster secretion, so shortening is a manufacturability gain if binding is retained. The wet lab took this up as a testable claim and is measuring secretion for truncated constructs against wild type.

Point substitutions

The axis came from a simple idea: BoPEP4 residues not in contact with PEPR1 could be swapped for the residue AtPEP1 has at the same position, restoring residues present in the literature-backed 5GR8 structure. Alignment identified positions 18 and 8 as the candidate set inside the contact region.

Conserved repeats

BLAST scans suggested the conserved 15–23 region drives receptor binding, motivating a tandem repeat analysis. The countervailing risk is that lengthening the peptide makes it less active-site specific, filling other parts of the receptor.

Outcomes for all three axes, including which designs were withdrawn and why, are reported in Sprints and Results.

LEA14 axes
Point substitution

These address liabilities identified in the sequence, not contacts at an interface. LEA14 contains a single cysteine, Cys58, at the start of β3 with no partner. In the reducing plant cytosol an unpaired thiol is inert, but our route sends the protein through the oxidizing extracellular compartment where it can form intermolecular disulfides and dimerise. C58S substitutes a serine of almost identical size that cannot. The test is a gel run with and without reducing agent, so the hypothesis fails visibly if a paired band persists.

The sequence also contains exactly one Asp-Pro bond, at 78–79, the most acid-labile peptide bond type. D78E removes it while preserving the negative charge. This is insurance against a process condition, not a gain in activity, and was ranked as such.

A third group targets the flexible N-terminal extension at 2–19. Helical wheel analysis gives that segment a hydrophobic moment near 0.37, periodic but below the range typical of membrane-binding amphipathic helices. Face-consistent substitutions could raise it into that range and turn a flexible tail into a membrane anchor. This is recorded as a hypothesis, with the N-terminal truncation built as its matched negative control.

Surface hydrophilicity substitutions were deliberately not pursued. LEA14's GRAVY of +0.056 is unusual for a stress-protective protein and could be lowered, but doing so works against the fold, and the bulk hydration shielding it would serve is the mechanism our feasibility analysis argues is unavailable at any deliverable concentration.

K-segment translocation

LEA14 belongs to the atypical folded LEA_2 group and lacks all of the repeat motifs characterizing the disordered groups. The K-segment is the best-characterized membrane-protection module in the family and occurs in dehydrins, not here. The design fuses one or two K-segments, or Group 3 LEA 11-mer repeats, onto the folded core, giving a stable scaffold with a disordered arm.

The precedent is documented: the mitochondrial LEA protein LEAM is disordered in solution and folds into an amphipathic helix that binds and stabilizes membranes as the cell dries. If the motif confers that behavior instead of the whole protein, grafting it should carry the behavior with it. Two constraints bound the graft: added mass reduces the fraction crossing the cell wall, and every fusion junction is a new site to audit for degron compliance and protease susceptibility.

Truncations

The published solution structure defines residues 24–145. The first 23 have no fixed conformation in the ensemble and are where a protease attacks first. Removing them does three things at once: removes the most exposed proteolytic target, lowers mass and hydrodynamic radius, and leaves the folded β-sandwich core intact. It is also the negative control for the amphipathicity hypothesis above, so the two constructs read against each other. The test is a trypsin challenge comparing how quickly full-length and truncated bands disappear.

Restraints on the map →3.1 Protectant structural input

BoPEP4 structural input

The input was based on two sources: 5GR8, the crystal structure of PEPR1 with AtPEP1, and AlphaFold. 5GR8 confirmed the extended conformation of AtPEP1 along the inner surface of the superhelical PEPR1 LRR, supporting the inference that BoPEP4, from the same PEP family, adopts a comparable extended structure when bound. Structures were generated with AlphaFold 3, cross-checked against 5GR8, and used as docking inputs, with 5GR8 itself as the control.

AlphaFold on the free peptide returns no single usable fold: fraction_disordered = 1.0, pTM 0.18–0.35, and five seeds giving five different secondary structures. That is the expected answer for a peptide that only orders on binding. The alternative route is threading onto the AtPep1 backbone of 5GR8 chain J, which the gapless alignment permits: the backbone transfers unchanged and only the side chains at the differing positions are rebuilt.

3.2 Partner and template structures

Each protectant is modeled against something different: BoPEP4 against two receptors and a co-receptor, LEA14 against an F-box protein and a third chain, ACC deaminase against a cross-organism crystal carrying its cofactor and substrate. Nine molecules in all, of which two are experimental structures of the thing they represent. The peptide side is set out here; the other two are in 3.4 and 3.5.

Two receptors and one co-receptor make up the BoPEP4 signaling complex. PEPR1, an LRR receptor-like kinase acting as an innate immunity pattern recognition receptor, has its extracellular domain in 5GR8 chain A, which was used as the docking receptor input.

Given the similarity between PEPR1 and PEPR2, PEPR2 was considered as an alternative. PEPR2 is more specific toward AtPEP ligands where PEPR1 shows broader recognition. A PEPR2 structure was generated with AlphaFold 3, the only structure-prediction tool this project used, and PEPR1 was selected as primary on the strength of its experimental structure, with PEPR2 kept as a complementary model.

BAK1 came from 4MN8 chain B, the FLS2–BAK1 complex bound to flg22. BAK1 is a shared LRR co-receptor for multiple plant immune receptors including the PEPRs, and its conserved ectodomain provides the framework for receptor–co-receptor association. 4MN8 does not contain PEPR1, but the resolved BAK1 ectodomain transfers on that conserved architecture.

3.3 Restraint mapping

Restraints mark the residues expected to participate in an interaction, constraining the search toward interfaces that are physically and biologically plausible. Given the limited and heterogeneous evidence for the molecules in this complex, cross-validation was carried out across several tools before anything was written into a restraint file.

MUSCLE alignment and WHISCY

To supply WHISCY with evolutionary information for PEPR1, homologs were collected by BLAST and 352 sequences aligned with MUSCLE. WHISCY weights each surface residue by conservation, on the reasoning that conserved solvent-exposed patches are more likely to be functional interfaces. It was run both with and without the alignment so the effect of the evolutionary input could be measured.

Results · what the scan and the predictor returned

41 active residues, 7 supported by both

The primary restraint set is contact-derived from 5GR8: every PEPR1 heavy atom against every AtPEP1 heavy atom at 4.5 Å, across both crystallographic copies, unioned. That gave 41 active residues covering most of the concave LRR face. Passive residues were solvent-exposed positions (relative SASA ≥ 0.40) with a Cα within 8 Å of an active residue, giving 9. The passive set is thin because the active interface is unusually extensive, as expected for an LRR wrapping its ligand.

The same scan independently confirmed the anchor reported by Tang et al.: Arg487 salt-bridges the C-terminal carboxylate of Asn23 at 2.40 Å and 2.59 Å across the two copies. Arg491 makes no hydrogen bond and no salt bridge on the PDBePISA run; it buries 12 Å² and sits roughly 5 Å away. It is recorded as an interface residue, not a second anchor, and is not used as a pass criterion.

On the peptide side the scan returned 16 of the 17 resolved AtPEP1 residues, and the peptide was treated as fully active, standard for a short linear ligand enclosed in a groove.

Run blind, WHISCY's active tier recovered little of the crystal interface, 1 of 41 and 2 of 41, agreeing with each other on a single residue, so it was not treated as a reliable independent check. The passive tier behaved differently: it recovered six crystal-contact residues blind and nine with the alignment, the same six plus 417, 441 and 443. Seven residues (347, 348, 369, 371, 372, 393, 395) appear in the crystal contacts and in both runs; all 41 were kept as active and those seven flagged as the highest-confidence core.

For PEPR2 the lists were transferred by pairwise alignment, giving 39 active and 9 passive. 21 of the 41 PEPR1 active residues are identical in PEPR2. Arg487, the anchor, is conserved as Arg464. These lists are a starting hypothesis for the PEPR2 model, not a validated restraint set.

US-align and the BAK1 interface

No structure of PEPR1 bound to BAK1 exists, so the co-receptor interface had to be predicted by structural analogy with FLS2.

Results · the transferred interface

23 residues mapped onto PEPR1

FLS2 and PEPR1 share only 28% sequence identity, too low for sequence-based transfer, but US-align returns TM-score 0.840, well above the 0.5 threshold for the same fold. Residue equivalences were read directly from the alignment block, without approximating by nearest neighbor, and then mapped FLS2's 25 BAK1-contact residues onto PEPR1, giving 23 predicted BAK1-site residues.

An initial placement rotating the whole BAK1 domain produced a physically impossible intertwined model. A local Kabsch refit restricted to matched interface residues brought interface RMSD from 4.00 Å to 2.09 Å and raised plausible contact pairs from 61 to 95.

Two of the 23, residues 372 and 395, fall inside the seven-residue peptide-interface core, and several sit near the 487–491 region. In the FLS2 system the ligand's C-terminal region and BAK1's footprint are structurally adjacent, so this is what the architecture would predict. It is reported as consistent with known biology and not as evidence for it, since a geometric transfer could land generically nearby.

On the BAK1 side the restraints are contact-derived from 4MN8: 24 active and 4 passive, comprising contacts with FLS2 and with flg22, where Val54 and Thr58 are shared. Koller and Bent (2014) had been expected to give a second, conservation-based line of support. On a full reading it reports the opposite: the conserved cluster it identifies did not impair FLS2–BAK1 interaction under mutagenesis, and the paper concludes FLS2 and BRI1 use receptor-specific docking sites. The BAK1 restraints therefore rest on a single line of evidence and were weighted accordingly.

DisVis

Before committing to full runs, DisVis tests whether a restraint set describes a geometrically possible arrangement at all. It scans every rigid-body placement of one partner around the other and reports how many restraints can be satisfied simultaneously, with the volume of accessible interaction space. Each line in a DisVis file is independent, with no OR grouping, so a broad active set is not expected to be fully satisfiable; what matters is how much of the set and how much space stay consistent, measured against a case where the answer is known.

Results · both runs returned

The co-crystal control is four times more satisfiable than the inferred interface

Because active residue lists were available and no known pairs, the full combinatorial matrix was generated for each run, as Cα–Cα distances bounded 2.0 to 8.0 Å. Run 1, PEPR1 against AtPEP1, is the positive control drawn from a solved co-crystal. Run 2, PEPR1 against BAK1, is the test: its two sides were derived independently and never checked for mutual compatibility.

Both completed at 9.72° rotational sampling, 1.0 Å voxels, 7416 rotations. The control admits 15.43% of placements on at least one restraint against 3.62% for the test, and the gap widens with depth: at ten restraints it is 3.38% against 0.32%, and at thirty 0.037% against 0.0004%.

Neither set is fully satisfiable, which is a property of the matrix and not of the interface. An all-against-all construction asks one receptor residue to sit within 8 Å of every partner residue at once, so most pairs are mutually exclusive by design. The ceiling is 62 of 656 for the control and 41 of 552 for the test.

Contact-derived restraints from the crystal structures

Structural superposition gave reference geometry for each binary interface, which is what lets us judge docked models on structure instead of score alone.

Results · the reference poses

fnat 0.974 on the threaded peptide

For the peptide interface BoPEP4 was threaded onto the AtPEP1 backbone from 5GR8 chain J. The gapless alignment means numbering transfers one to one. The threaded model reproduces the native interface with fnat 0.974, so it is the reference pose for BoPEP4 in the groove. Note that this figure is largely a consequence of construction: grafting onto the native backbone recovers native contacts at conserved positions by definition, so it confirms the threading was executed without distortion; it is not evidence that BoPEP4 binds this way.

The C-terminal anchor is a five-contact clamp on Asn23, not a single salt bridge: OXT–Arg487 NH1 at 2.40 Å, O–Arg487 NE at 2.80 Å, ND2–Asp441 at 2.71 Å, N–Asp441 at 2.75 Å, and O–Asn465 at 3.14 Å.

For the co-receptor interface FLS2 (4MN8 chain A) was superimposed onto PEPR1 along the LRR β-ladder at 1.59 Å over 74 Cα pairs and BAK1 was carried with the transform. As a check, the transformed flg22 lands within 1 to 2 Å of AtPEP1, confirming both ligands occupy the same register on their respective receptors.

HADDOCK score did not reliably track structural accuracy in these runs: one cluster ranked fourth by score returned fnat 0.000. Every cluster was therefore rescored against the 5GR8 and 4MN8 reference geometry and models were selected by agreement with those references.

Results · the cross-validated core

7 residues survive every method

347, 348, 369, 371, 372, 393, 395. These appear in the crystal contacts and in both WHISCY runs, at either tier. They sit in one tight stretch of the receptor, and two of them, 372 and 395, are also in the US-align co-receptor list, which is the adjacency the FLS2 architecture would predict. All 41 contact residues were kept active; these seven are flagged as the core.

The predictor's own active tier is unstable: supplying the alignment moved it from 6 residues to 19, and the two runs share a single residue between them. The passive tier is steadier, and it is the tier that recovers the crystal contacts.

Results · which restraints survive

Both cores are coherent, and the control's is the crystal's

Thirty-four restraints score below z = −2.0 in each run, and in neither case are they scattered. In the control they concentrate on receptor residues 295, 297, 299, 321, 324, 345 and 347 against peptide residues 14 to 20. Every one of those receptor positions is in the 41-residue contact list, and 347 is in the seven-residue core that the crystal contacts and both WHISCY runs already agree on. The single most satisfiable restraint is A299–J17, violated in 63% of placements at z = −4.49.

In the test they concentrate on receptor residues 509, 533, 534, 555, 557, 558, 581, 603 and 605 against BAK1 residues 43 to 48 and 60 to 61. All of those receptor positions are in the 23-residue US-align transfer and all the BAK1 positions are in its 24-residue contact list, so the geometry picks out a coherent sub-patch of the proposed interface instead of rejecting it wholesale. The most satisfiable is A557–B45 at z = −4.81.

What the pre-screen establishes is therefore narrow and worth stating plainly: the homology-inferred co-receptor interface is not geometrically impossible, and the part of it that survives is internally consistent. It remains the restraint set with the least independent support in the project, since the citation meant to be its second line of evidence reports the opposite of what was assumed.

A citation that did not survive reading

The BAK1-side restraint was planned to rest on two independent lines: the structural transfer from 4MN8, and a repeat-conservation analysis reported by Koller and Bent (2014) as a shared SERK-docking signature. The paper reports the opposite. Site-directed mutagenesis of that conserved cluster did not impair the FLS2–BAK1 interaction, and the residues the paper does validate as required are Gln530, Ser554, Gln627 and Asn674, none of which lie in the cluster. No PEPR1 residue numbers appear in it at all. The co-receptor restraint therefore rests on one line of evidence, the structural transfer, and is weighted as homology-inferred, not cross-validated.

3.4 LEA14 structural input

The protein was taken from 1XO8, the solution NMR structure of At1g01470, deposited by the Center for Eukaryotic Structural Genomics. Two features of that deposition matter. It is annotated UNKNOWN FUNCTION, so nothing in the record says where the protein binds anything. And the construct is the whole protein: the deposition cross-references UniProt O03983 across residues 1 to 151, and the coordinates include all 151 with no gaps and no expression tag. What is docked is the complete mature protein.

Results · what the 20-model ensemble contains

A rigid core and one mobile tail

Over the first ten models the mean pairwise Cα RMSD across all 151 residues is 3.94 Å, maximum 5.55 Å. Restricted to the folded core, residues 24–145, the same comparison gives 0.66 Å, maximum 0.84 Å. The spread is almost entirely in the N-terminal tail; the β-sandwich core is one conformation reproduced twenty times.

Per-residue fluctuation agrees. Superposing all 20 models on the core, Cα RMSF averages 8.75 Å across residues 1–16 against 0.42 Å across 24–145. The transition is abrupt: Leu16 fluctuates by 3.97 Å and Ala18 by 1.16 Å, a factor of three across two residues. The C-terminal six residues sit at 1.61 Å, mobile without being disordered. The most rigid position is Cys58 at 0.26 Å.

Those figures reproduce the ones already in the LEA14 design documents to the same decimal, which is the cross-check required before building on them. They also set the truncation boundary: a cut at residue 16 removes the mobile segment and leaves the ordered region intact, where a cut at 23 would remove six residues belonging to the folded core. Glu23 is 10.9% solvent exposed on our own calculation, which is to say buried.

3.5 AtPP2-B11 structural input

No experimental structure of AtPP2-B11 exists, so this one had to be predicted. The full 257-residue sequence of UniProt Q949S5 was folded with AlphaFold 3 on the AlphaFold Server, templates enabled, and the server returned five models from the submitted seed.

Results · confidence and what the five models mean

pTM 0.89, mean pLDDT 90.8

All five models report pTM 0.89 with ranking scores 0.89 to 0.90, no clashes, and a disordered fraction of 0.00 or 0.01. Mean pLDDT on the top model is 90.8: 92.8 over the F-box region, 89.9 over the linker, 90.6 over the PP2 domain. Three stretches fall below pLDDT 70: residues 1–2 at the terminus, and two loops inside the PP2 domain at 179–186 and 196–202.

As an independent check the top model was compared against the AlphaFold Database entry for the same protein, computed separately. The two agree to 1.27 Å Cα RMSD over all 257 residues, and both place the same two loops in their low-confidence regions.

The word ensemble needs qualifying. Mean pairwise Cα RMSD among the five models is 0.94 Å, maximum 1.14 Å, and restricting to the PP2 domain changes nothing at 0.95 Å. The five models are the same conformation five times: they sample the predictor's uncertainty, not the molecule's conformational space. This is the opposite of LEA14, where the deposited models carry genuine variety in the tail.

Which part is docked

InterPro places the F-box domain at residues 1–44 and the PP2 lectin-like domain at 92–255, with an unassigned linker between. The F-box is the surface that binds ASK1 inside the SCF complex, so it is not available to LEA14 in the cell. Docking the full chain would let the search place LEA14 on a face that is occupied in vivo, so the input is residues 45–257 with the F-box removed, numbering preserved. That leaves the PP2 lectin domain as the surface under test, which is also the substrate-recognition module by analogy with the solved plant F-box complexes.

Assembly on the map →4Assembly: structural docking

4.1 Binary docking

Binary docking reduces the complexity of the full system and characterizes one interface at a time. Most of our runs, including the alanine scans and truncation ladders, are built on this workflow because of its relative simplicity.

BoPEP4 was docked against the PEPR1 ectodomain in HADDOCK 2.4. The conformations generated during structure preparation were treated as alternative representations of the flexible peptide, and the active and passive residues from restraint mapping guided the search toward plausible interfaces.

Sampling was 1000 rigid-body, 200 semi-flexible and 200 water-refined models per run, FCC clustering at 0.60, minimum cluster size 4, peptide fully flexible. Those settings were constant across the nine main binary runs, so their scores are comparable with each other. Confirmation runs used 40/10/10 on the same protocol, because they ask a different question: the cost of a single edit on a fixed pose. Their scores come from far less sampling and are not scored against the main set.

Models were clustered by structural similarity and evaluated by HADDOCK score and subsequent structural assessment. The highest-scoring structure was not treated as correct, and structurally plausible clusters were retained for comparison.

4.2 Threaded ternary docking

BoPEP4's activity is not determined solely by PEPR1. BAK1 is the signaling kinase balancing growth and innate immunity, and PEP perception involves receptor–co-receptor association, so the question asked was whether the predicted BoPEP4–PEPR1 interaction could be accommodated in a PEPR1–BoPEP4–BAK1 complex.

Docking all three components simultaneously was attempted first and did not run, so the binary result was used as a structural starting point instead: a selected PEPR1–BoPEP4 complex was retained as established and treated as one body, and BAK1 was docked onto it on the interface identified during restraint mapping. This preserved the geometry of the modeled ligand–receptor interaction while searching for a compatible BAK1 orientation. Resulting models were assessed for steric clashes, interface plausibility and consistency with the restraints.

Two caveats. The ternary run inherits its binary input, so a peptide-register error would propagate uncaught, which is why the binary complex was validated against 5GR8 first. And its two restraint sets are not equally certain: peptide-to-receptor is contact-derived from a solved co-crystal, BAK1-to-receptor transferred from 4MN8 by analogy. The output is a structural hypothesis consistent with the published heterodimerisation biochemistry, not a residue-level prediction.

4.3 AI co-folding

HADDOCK searches under restraints that are supplied; a co-folding predictor builds from sequence with none. The two fail for different reasons, so agreement between them is informative. AlphaFold 3 was run on the AlphaFold Server, templates enabled, five models per seed, with nothing from the HADDOCK workflow entering the job. The two tracks run in parallel and never seed each other.

ipTM, pTM, the ranking score and the chain-pair ipTM matrix with interface PAE are recorded, since a single ipTM averages across every interface and a confident pair can hide a failed one. Tracks are compared on interface residue identity, not global RMSD. Solved complexes of the same class are run first under identical settings, and the acceptance threshold is fixed from what they return.

Results · confidence is not activity

ipTM 0.73 for a peptide 4000-fold inactive

Our July 30 audit found AlphaFold 3 returned ipTM 0.73 for a peptide published as more than 4000-fold inactive, against 0.74 for BoPEP4. The metric does not separate a working ligand from a dead one, so it is used only as a structural confidence estimate. Multiple models from one seed are the predictor's uncertainty about one answer, not a conformational ensemble.

Results from the docking and co-folding runs are reported in Sprints and Results.

Flex on the map →5Flex: molecular dynamics

This stage earned its place by disagreeing. The K18R design rested on Lys18 carrying the largest electrostatic contribution in the peptide; a trajectory found that contact present in 53.7% of frames across a 2.55 to 9.37 Å swing, and the design was withdrawn. The position-8 design went the same way when a 30 ns run on the wild type ranked Lys8 nineteenth of twenty-three residues by contact occupancy. In both cases the docked pose said one thing and the trajectory said another.

That is the point of watching a trajectory instead of scoring a pose. Every design decision above rests on poses that have only been scored, and a contact that appears in a single static model is a hypothesis about the complex, not a measurement of it. Flex is where that distinction is forced.

The work is nine all-atom trajectories, including an AtPEP1 benchmark and an apo receptor control alongside the BoPEP4 constructs. It is reported in full on the Molecular Dynamics page: the systems and their parameters, what each run changed about the design, and the limits of a 30 ns window.

Triage on the map →6Triage: structure scoring

Docking returns hundreds of models and will always return a best one, whether or not any of them are right. Triage decides whether a model is worth carrying forward, and it is deliberately built from methods that fail for unrelated reasons. A model passes only if it passes all five. When one fails, which one fails is the diagnosis that shapes the next round of truncations and substitutions.

  1. Did the search converge?
  2. Is the model actually right?
  3. Is this the size and chemistry of a real interface?
  4. Is the geometry chemically legal?
  5. Does it reproduce contacts already known to exist?

6.1 HADDOCK score and clustering

HADDOCK groups similar models into clusters, each representing one binding mode. The funnel shape and the cluster population are read before the single best-scoring model, because a lone good score with no supporting cluster usually means the search sampled a lucky pose without converging. A well-populated cluster at the bottom of a funnel is the shape being looked for.

Why this is not fully applicable: the score discrepancy

HADDOCK score is a weighted sum of energy terms in arbitrary units, designed to rank models within a run. In our own validation it did not track structural accuracy: one cluster ranked fourth by score reproduced none of the native contacts. This is the single most important reason triage exists as a multi-check process. If the score alone were reliable the other four would not be needed. It is used to judge convergence and to rank within a run, never as evidence that a model is correct.

The prohibition is stronger across constructs of different length, where a longer chain buries more surface and scores better for that reason alone. Comparisons across a truncation series are made per unit of interface, not raw.

6.2 CAPRI

CAPRI is the community standard for grading a predicted complex against a known answer, sorting models into acceptable, medium and high accuracy on contact recovery and ligand placement. It is the only check that measures correctness directly, and it only works where a reference structure exists. For us that is 5GR8. Every protocol change is graded against it, so that any claim about a model with no reference rests on a protocol calibrated on one that has it.

A caution: the grade is an aggregate. A model can reach a medium tier while the single most conserved contact in the system is far out of place, which is what the literature check below exists to catch.

6.3 PDBePISA

PISA reports buried surface area, chemical composition, and whether a contact looks like a genuine assembly or a crystal packing artifact. It answers a question the docking score cannot: is this the right size and character for a complex that exists in solution? A model can score well while burying far too little surface to be real.

The 5GR8 reference list

PISA was run on the crystal first, so every later number has a reference point. Our docked models are read against those values, which matters for a 23-mer peptide where expectations built on globular interfaces do not transfer.

Results · the reference interface

2039 Å² and 25 unique interactions

The 5GR8 interface buries 2039 Å² in total, composed 21% apolar, 41% polar, 38% charged, with an inventory of 19 hydrogen bonds and 10 salt bridges amounting to 25 unique interactions once pairs listed in both tables are counted once.

One property of this interface governs how PISA is read here: its own hydrophobicity test is negative. The peptide side returns a solvation gain of +2.5 kcal·mol−1 and a P-value of 0.585, meaning the interface is less hydrophobic than a random patch of that size. What keeps it together is the polar network, so area and bond inventory are read in place of the ΔiG verdict.

6.4 MolProbity

MolProbity validates the stereochemistry of the coordinates themselves, independent of any docking score. It reports a clashscore for atoms in impossible overlapping positions, rotamer outliers for side chains in conformations real residues do not adopt, and backbone geometry. A model can satisfy every restraint and still be chemically illegal, particularly after the side-chain repacking refinement performs, which is why this check sits outside the energy-based scores.

The Ramachandran plot

The plot maps each residue's backbone dihedrals against the regions physically accessible to a polypeptide. Most residues should fall in the favored regions, and outliers mark strained conformations. For threaded and refined peptide models this is a particularly direct check, because threading imposes a backbone taken from another sequence and this is where the strain from that imposition would show.

6.5 Literature check

The final check asks whether the model reproduces contacts that are already documented. For the PEPR1 interface that is Arg487 engaging the peptide's free C-terminal carboxylate, which was verified directly in the crystal coordinates before using it as a test. A model that fails to reproduce it is wrong regardless of how it scored on everything above, because it has placed the peptide in the wrong register.

The criterion is calibrated on the crystal, not assumed. Arg487 binds the carboxylate at 2.40 Å; Arg491 is often named alongside it, but on the PDBePISA run it makes no hydrogen bond and no salt bridge at all, so it is reported as an interface residue and not scored.

This check is cheap, it is the hardest to fool, and it is the one that caught register errors the energy scores accepted.

What triage cannot establish

A model passing all five checks shows the sequence is capable of the molecular recognition step. It does not show that the peptide reaches the receptor, survives the apoplast, or that binding produces the downstream immune output. Calcium influx, ROS burst, MAPK activation and receptor complex phosphotransfer are all read out in the cell. The redesign loop optimizes for predicted binding; only the wet lab closes the gap to function.

Per-run triage outcomes, with each sprint stating which checks it passed and which it failed, are reported in Sprints and Results.

7Codon optimization

7.1 ViennaRNA

ViennaRNA was used on the assumption that reducing mRNA secondary-structure stability around the MF001 ribosome binding site and the start codon improves translation initiation. Performance was assessed by minimum free energy and unpaired-probability predictions, with codon adaptation index values against Bacillus subtilis 168 codon usage. Recoding codons 2 to 31 within the SamyQ signal peptide and the early CDS breaks inhibitory structure in the start+15 and SD core regions, making the start site easier for ribosomes to attach to.

The approach follows Castillo-Hair et al. (2019) on optogenetic control of B. subtilis gene expression. All analyses ran on the RS-inclusive Window A, modeling the pre-recombination transient mRNA state: RS (28 nt) + MF001 RBS (24 nt) + spacer (1 nt) + SamyQ signal peptide (93 nt) + first 90 nt of CDS.

MFE
Minimum free energy (kcal/mol) of the most stable secondary structure.
start+15 / start+30
Mean unpaired probability over the first 15 or 30 nucleotides from the start codon.
SD core
Mean unpaired probability over the 7-nucleotide Shine-Dalgarno core (AAGGAGG).
CAI
Codon adaptation index relative to B. subtilis 168 usage from the Kazusa database.

Phase 1 held the 93-nt SamyQ sequence static and optimized the first 15 codons of each passenger gene independently across 8000 random synonymous variants, maximizing a balance score of start+15 plus SD core. Phase 2 addressed elongation via CAI and appended a C-terminal 6×His tag; this was the June and July design, and the tag moved to the N-terminus for BoPEP4 constructs ordered after August 16. Full-length optimization degraded initiation by lowering start+15. Phase 3 departed from standard host adaptation by making SamyQ codons 2 to 31 editable, 45 editable codons once the first 15 CDS codons are included, and drew roughly 50 000 random synonymous variants per construct, each fully re-folded, further reducing structure around the initiation site. The larger library is the larger editable region: Phase 1 sampled 8000 draws over the CDS codons alone.

Across all four cargoes the pattern is the same: the upstream RBS baseline is largely unchanged, while the initial ORF section undergoes a localized rise in unpaired probability. The targeted breakdown of double-stranded stems opens the start site without needing to unwind the entire preceding sequence.

7.2 SignalP 6.0

SignalP predicts whether a sequence carries a signal peptide, which secretion pathway it targets, and where signal peptidase cleaves. It is used for two purposes.

First, to confirm each signal peptide and mature sequence junction is cleaved where intended, so the released mature protein has the N-terminus intended. This matters because the mature N-terminal residues follow charge rules affecting secretion efficiency, and one of our substitutions sits in that region.

Second, to check that no cleavage site is cryptically predicted inside the mature sequence, which would cut the protectant itself. A construct that passes structural triage and fails this check is not orderable.

Predictions were run across the whole signal peptide panel instead of one assumed choice, because the best signal peptide differs for each cargo and cannot be predicted from first principles.
