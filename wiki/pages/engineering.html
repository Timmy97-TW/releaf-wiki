Cloning

Three cloning pipelines ran side by side all season, and the write-up below keeps them apart. One put the four-module light circuit together. One built every protectant cargo behind a constitutive promoter, so that a cargo could be tested without the light gate on top of it. One worked out how to get a plasmid through the wall of Bacillus subtilis at all. Twelve DBTL cycles between them, and each pipeline handed the other two something it could not have made alone.

Pipeline 1, the inducible circuit, is five cycles that put four transcription units into one plasmid so that green light gates the protectant. Pipeline 2, the constitutive output module, is five cycles that build every cargo with no light gate in front of it, so a cargo can be tested on its own. Pipeline 3 is two cycles of electroporation, one per strain, measured in colonies recovered. They are not independent: pipeline 2 is where a protectant cassette is first sequence-verified, and those verified inserts are what pipeline 1 assembles into Level 2, with the codon-optimized ACC deaminase that pipeline 2 designed as the cargo the finished light circuit delivers. Pipeline 3 is the last step for both, and it is also where the project changed host: the voltage sweeps there are what showed the assembled 10 kb plasmid could be recovered at all.

The inducible circuit

The first plan was to let the bacterium sense stress for itself. The reason it is light-gated instead is that a stress threshold is not a property of the stress, it is a property of the crop. At 30 °C a potato is already in trouble, because tuber formation is fully inhibited above 25 °C [16], while sorghum is still inside its optimal range and only starts losing yield above 32.5 °C [17]. A circuit tuned to one of those crops is wrong for the other, so the threshold was moved out of the cell and into a microcontroller that watches the field and decides when to switch the light on. That is what the four-component optogenetic circuit below is for.

Five cycles took the CcaS/CcaR system from a standard that would not assemble to one plasmid with every junction checked in three separate clones. Each cycle fixes a specific thing the one before it could not tell apart.

  1. DDesign

    Based on the characterization of the CcaS/CcaR optogenetic system reported in Castillo-Hair 2019 [1], we redesigned the optogenetic circuits with the coding sequences of the green/red light-responsive two-component system (CcaSm3 and CcaR), the PCB production enzyme (ho1-pcyA fusion) and our target output and reporter coding sequences (LEA14, ACCD, BoPep4) by the modular cloning (MoClo) system [3], because we were unable to obtain the SubtiToolKit.

    To assemble these functional transcription units into the MoClo 2.0 system provided from the distribution kit, we designated custom PCR primers incorporating type II restriction enzyme recognition sites (BsaI for Level 1 assembly) flanked by specific standard overhangs following every Level 1 construct. This was designed to allow sequential assembly of promoters, RBSs, CDSs and terminators into single transcriptional units (Level 1), which could subsequently be directionally combined into multi-gene circuits at Level 2. The initial design planned to PCR-amplify the coding sequences of interest to be cloned into Level 1 destination vectors (pJUMP29-1A, -1B, -1C, -1D). Following this, the modules were to be assembled into a Level 2 pNW33N shuttle vector and introduced into Bacillus subtilis to enable light-induced expression of the target protectants.

  2. BBuild

    The DNA fragments from IDT and the plasmid were ordered from Twist and GeneScript. For the DNA fragments, we performed PCR amplification using primers designed to introduce the required restriction sites and overhangs into each individual part to make them compatible with the MoClo assembly system. The plasmid already contained the BsaI enzyme recognition sites and was prepared by miniprep. The resulting purified PCR products and plasmid were then used in Golden Gate assembly to generate Level 1 single modules using the pJUMP-29 destination vectors. The assembly mixtures were transformed into E. coli DH5α cells and plated on selective media.

  3. TTest

    Colony PCR was performed to screen transformants for successful Level 1 Golden Gate assembly; however, the cloning efficiency was low and the results were inconsistent. Amplified fragments were analyzed via agarose gel electrophoresis to assess insert size. A subset of colonies yielding bands near the expected sizes were supposed to be selected for plasmid isolation and subsequent restriction digest verification. All trials were unsuccessful and as a result, data is not shown.

  4. LLearn

    Colony PCR screening and restriction digests revealed high background, including potentially empty vectors and incorrect inserts, making it difficult to distinguish from assembly failures and PCR artifacts. We learned that attempting Level 1 assembly straight from unverified PCR products introduced considerable ambiguity, highlighting the need for an intermediate cloning step to generate clean, sequence-verified Level 0 entry parts to optimize cloning success.

The coding sequences for the green and red light components, ccaR, ccaSm3 and the ho1:pcyA operon, were taken from the CcaS/CcaR characterization of Castillo-Hair and colleagues [1] and paired with the cargo sequences the project needed, LEA14, ACCD and BoPep4. To get those into MoClo 2.0 as transcription units, each module was given a standard set of regulatory parts, and every part got a custom primer pair carrying type IIS sites: BbsI and BpiI for Level 0 entry, BsaI for Level 1, each flanked by the standard overhangs. That hierarchy is the one the build kept, even after the kit under it was swapped.

The mixtures were transformed into E. coli DH5α and plated on selective media, screened by colony PCR and read on agarose for insert size. Colonies with a band near an expected size were marked for miniprep and a confirming digest. Colony PCR and restriction digest both came back with high background: empty vectors and off-target bands that could not be told apart from an assembly failure or a PCR artifact.

The other half of the fix came from outside the team. Dr Joaquín Caro-Astorga supplied the SubtiToolKit [4], a bioengineering kit for Bacillus subtilis and Gram-positive bacteria. That was luck rather than planning, and it is recorded here as luck.

  1. DDesign

    In response to the issue identified in DBTL cycle 1, we realized that an intermediate cloning step, in which the inserts are first cloned into Level 0 vectors, was necessary. Since we had unreliable outcomes in our earlier assembly, including inconsistent colony PCR results and unconfirmed clones, we revisited the cloning strategy at the PCR and vector-entry stage to improve reliability before moving into Level 1 Golden Gate assembly. The overall part composition for each module remained unchanged from DBTL 1; the redesign targeted the intermediate cloning steps used to generate clean, verified Level 0 parts.

    Moreover, the lessons learned in the DBTL 1 cycle regarding low assembly efficiency of our desired Level 1 constitutive constructs led us to conclude that the circuits needed to be redesigned. We received the SubtiToolKit (STK) from Dr Joaquín Caro-Astorga [4]. Considering the poor assembly efficiency obtained with MoClo in DBTL cycle 1, we decided to switch to the STK system. To convert the coding sequence from our original MoClo design into an STK-compatible Level 1 part, we designed the forward primer (Ho1 F) and reverse primer (pcyA R) to PCR-amplify each CDS as a single amplicon directly from our existing MoClo construct: Ho1 F/pcyA R for the Ho1-pcyA fusion, ccaSm3 F/ccaSm3 R for ccaSm3, and ccaR F/ccaR R for ccaR. Additionally, we also had to PCR-amplify the promoter for our light-responsive expression module from the original MoClo construct utilizing PcpcG2-veg F/R. We designed the primers for each CDS with 5′ extensions containing the STK 4 bp overhang and a BsaI recognition site, and gene-specific annealing regions at the 3′ ends. Similarly, we designed the corresponding forward and reverse primers for each output module CDS (csn-ACCD and csn-LEA) following the same modular tail architecture as for other parts from MoClo: a 5′ spacer, a BbsI site defining the Level 0 positional overhang, a BsaI site defining the Level 1 positional overhang, and a gene-specific annealing region. However, to avoid delays while awaiting delivery of the STK, the parts were inserted into pGEM-T Easy instead of pSTK-0.

  2. BBuild

    We amplified each module’s coding sequence by PCR and cloned the amplicon into a pGEM-T Easy vector [5]. pGEM-T Easy provides a direct, rapid method for cloning PCR products through TA cloning: the vector is supplied pre-linearized with single 3′-thymidine (T) overhangs, which are complementary to the single 3′-adenosine (A) overhangs left on PCR products by Taq and other non-proofreading polymerases. This complementarity allows the amplicon to ligate directly into the vector without any additional processing, making it a fast and efficient intermediate cloning strategy compared to attempting Golden Gate entry directly from a PCR product.

    We executed the Level 1 Golden Gate assembly following the SubtiToolKit workflow. The verified pGEM-T Easy Level 0 plasmid, along with STK library parts as well as MoClo 2.0 converted STK parts, were assembled into the corresponding pSTK-1 vector. Specifically, we used Level 1 vectors pSTK-1A-sfGFP, pSTK-1B-sfGFP, pSTK-1C-sfGFP and pSTK-1D-sfGFP from the STK library for our PCB-production module, light-sensing module, transduction module and light-responsive expression module, respectively. We utilized the type IIS restriction enzyme BsaI to digest the plasmids and T4 DNA ligase to ligate the fragments in a single one-pot reaction. The resulting Golden Gate assembly reaction mix was transformed into E. coli DH5α competent cells and selected on ampicillin media based on the insert vector.

  3. TTest

    Colonies from the pGEM-T Easy transformations were screened by colony PCR, and PCR products were run on an agarose gel alongside a DNA ladder to assess assembly success. A correct colony PCR band was one that ran at the expected size, calculated as the length between T7 promoter and SP6 promoter amplified by the screening primers; this confirms that a DNA fragment of the correct length was successfully ligated into the vector at the expected position. Colonies producing a single, clean band at this expected size were considered positive candidates for the correct insert. In contrast, colonies producing bands of incorrect or unexpected sizes were classified as negative and excluded from further analysis, since these patterns indicate either an empty vector or a truncated or mis-priming product rather than the intended insert.

    Subsequently, colonies from Level 1 Golden Gate transformations were likewise screened by colony PCR. Then, either purified plasmid DNA or liquid cultures were sent for Sanger sequencing to confirm the success of the assembly of the independent modules in our optogenetic system.

  4. LLearn

    The pGEM-T Easy TA cloning step proved to be a fast and reliable way to generate verified Level 0 entry material before doing Golden Gate assembly. Because colony PCR alone can only confirm that an insert of approximately the correct size is present, and cannot rule out point mutations or small indels introduced during PCR amplification, we relied on sequencing rather than enzyme digestion to confirm the fidelity of each cloned insert; we did not perform additional restriction digest verification at this stage, since correct band size on colony PCR, combined with full-length sequencing, was considered sufficient to confirm the sequence accuracy of each insert before proceeding to Level 1 assembly.

    This step did add an extra day to our workflow compared to attempting direct entry from the PCR product, since colonies had to be picked, screened, and miniprepped before sequencing. However, it reduced the ambiguity we had encountered in DBTL cycle 1, where using PCR products directly made it harder to distinguish true assembly failures from PCR artifacts. However, even after this modification, we found that it was relatively hard to successfully complete cloning of our module 1 construct (pSTK-1A-Pveg-RBS0-Ho1:pcyA-B0015); as a result, we later started considering substitutes for the Level 0 parts of that construct.

The four modules divide the work of the circuit. The PCB production module initiates chromophore production by expressing the heme oxygenase 1 and phycocyanobilin:ferredoxin oxidoreductase fusion enzyme, which converts endogenous cellular heme into active phycocyanobilin, the chromophore the photoreceptor needs: Ho1 oxidizes heme to biliverdin IXα, and PcyA then reduces the biliverdin to PCB. The light-sensing module contains the ccaSm3 gene, an optimized variant of the green-light responsive histidine kinase photoreceptor CcaS. Instead of being induced by xylose or IPTG as in Castillo-Hair 2019 [1], this module is driven by the constitutive promoters PlepA and PliaG, so the receptor is expressed constantly and the system responds continuously to green light. The transduction module encodes the CcaR response regulator; on photon absorption the activated CcaSm3 kinase transfers a phosphoryl group to CcaR, switching it into its active, DNA-binding state. The light-responsive expression module contains the target gene cassette behind the green-light inducible promoter PcpcG2-veg. Phosphorylated CcaR binds that promoter to drive expression of the protectant only when green light is present, which reduces leakage and lowers the metabolic burden on the bacteria when plants are not under stress. We equipped an E. coli expression blocking device as our composite ribosome binding site to prevent leakage of toxic genes before the construct reaches Bacillus subtilis.

  1. DDesign

    After DBTL 2, we further optimized our STK cloning strategy (v2) in response to a compatibility issue identified with the pGEM-T Easy intermediate cloning step: the pGEM-T Easy vector uses the same antibiotic resistance gene (ampicillin) as our Level 1 destination vectors (1A, 1B, 1C, 1D), which complicated downstream selection and increased the risk of background contamination from carryover vector during Golden Gate assembly. To resolve this, we redesigned our cloning approach to construct each module’s coding sequence directly using STK Level 0 primers, skipping the pGEM-T Easy intermediate altogether. This step was only relevant to the CDS not cloned into pGEM-T Easy, as the ones from DBTL 2 were still usable in Level 1 Golden Gate assembly.

  2. BBuild

    We amplified the coding sequence directly with primers carrying the STK Level 0 (BbsI) and Level 1 (BsaI) tail architecture, as described for Modules 1 to 4, and cloned the amplicon directly into the Level 0 entry vector pSTK-0C rather than through pGEM-T Easy. This removed the antibiotic resistance conflict, since pSTK-0 and our Level 1 destination vectors use distinguishable selection markers (chloramphenicol versus ampicillin), allowing a more obvious difference between correctly assembled Level 1 clones and the vector.

  3. TTest

    To verify the Level 0 parts cloned directly into pSTK-0C, colonies were screened by colony PCR. All 16 colonies screened for pSTK-0C-Csn:LEA14 gave a single band at the expected 811 bp. For the SamyQ-Bopep4 2.1-Histag constructs, 18 of 19 colonies carrying the 2.1 variant gave the expected 536 bp band, with lane 6 excluded because its band was a different size, and all 4 colonies carrying the 2.2 variant gave the expected band. A second screen of additional 2.2 variant colonies also gave bands at 536 bp. Colonies with a single band of the expected size were considered positive candidates.

  4. LLearn

    Although this approach resolved the antibiotic resistance conflict, a separate concern arose regarding potential recombination events in Bacillus subtilis [7]. Because several of our modules share repeated regulatory sequences (for example Pveg, RBS0, B0015) across multiple constructs, we became concerned that homologous recombination between repeated elements, once all four modules were combined in B. subtilis, could destabilize parts of the assembled optogenetic circuit, potentially weakening the CcaS/CcaR signals. Specifically, if a construct contains repeating sequences, such as duplicated promoters or terminators, the host’s recombination system can recognize them as matching regions and recombine them. This deletes the DNA lying between the repeated parts, rearranging the construct or shuffling them so that the cells end up carrying a shortened or altered version of the original design. These deletion variants can take over the culture, leading to loss of expression and inconsistent results between experiments. This concern motivated us to reconsider the repeated part usage across our modules going into the next design cycle.

Once all four modules sat in one cell, long stretches of identical sequence could give recombination something to act on, and the CcaS/CcaR signal path is exactly what would be damaged. The redesign written at the end of this cycle is one line: diversify the promoter, ribosome binding site and terminator across the modules so that no two of them share a long identical stretch. That is what cycle 4 was run on.

  1. DDesign

    To address the recombination risk identified in DBTL 3, we developed a third version of our SubtiToolKit design (v3) aimed at reducing sequence redundancy across the four modules of our optogenetic circuit. Rather than reusing identical regulatory parts (promoter, RBS, terminator) across multiple modules, we diversified these elements in module 1 to minimize the length and number of repeated sequences that could serve as substrates for homologous recombination in B. subtilis.

  1. DDesign

    Having successfully verified the independent Level 1 modules (PCB production, CcaS light sensing, CcaR transduction, and ACC deaminase light-responsive expression) through colony PCR and sequencing in previous cycles, the next objective was to combine them into a fully functional, multi-gene optogenetic circuit. For Level 2 assembly, the four individual Level 1 transcription units needed to be cloned into a single Level 2 shuttle vector from the STK library. The design also integrated experimental validation in two Bacillus subtilis strains. We initially enacted functional testing using our 168 strain, but we later received the B. subtilis WB800 strain from Professor Huang, which has eight extracellular proteases deleted (NprE, AprE, Epr, Bpr, Mpr, NprB, Vpr and WprA) [12]. Because the 168 transformations were limiting our progress, we decided to use the WB800 strain as an alternative host for the Level 2 construct. The construct was left unchanged, and only the host strain and the electroporation conditions were reconsidered. WB800 is deficient in extracellular proteases, which is also expected to benefit a secreted output such as ACC deaminase. Specifically, WB800 is a derivative of the six protease-deficient strain WB600, in which the Vpr protease and the cell-wall protease WprA were also inactivated; the commercially available version was built to increase the stability of secreted proteins by removing all eight. Because ACC deaminase is a secreted, His-tagged output, a protease-deficient host reduces degradation after secretion and makes the protein easier to detect on Western blots. Western blotting and ELISA were used to verify and quantify light-inducible protein expression, while a DNPH assay measured ACC deaminase activity under controlled green against red light.

    To construct the integrated Level 2 optogenetic system, we performed Golden Gate assembly, combining the sequence-verified STK Level 1 transcription units: the PCB production module, the ccaS-m3 light sensing module, the ccaR transcriptional module, and the PcpcG2-veg light responsive expression module. Following cloning in E. coli and sequencing confirmation, the fully assembled Level 2 expression plasmid was electroporated into Bacillus subtilis strains 168 and WB800 at 2.1 kV to 2.3 kV.

  4. LLearn

    Although successful, we ultimately learned that the electroporation of Level 2 Golden Gate assembly constructs was extremely difficult, as the constructs are over 10 kb, and that in future we should look towards other alternatives such as natural transformation, which might have made the process easier and faster. Furthermore, we also learned that in the process of determining whether or not we have the correct construct, one module being positive does not necessarily determine the state of the rest of the modules; as a result, it is imperative that we validate the accuracy of all modules following the cloning process.

    Unfortunately, we were unable to recover the Level 2 Csn-AcdS-Histag strain (ABDI, the wild-type cargo) in liquid culture from its glycerol stock, so we inoculated cultures directly from colonies on the electroporation plate instead. Although this worked, it means that future experiments should rely on freshly transformed colonies, and we will need to revisit our glycerol stock preparation and storage to make the strain reliably recoverable.

No Level 2 construct has been sequenced end to end. The junctions and the double digest both say the four modules are in the right order and the plasmid is the right length; neither reads the 10 kb between them, and the build record’s Level 2 sequencing cell is empty for both devices.

Module 2 was built on two promoters and both survived, so the Level 2 assembly was run twice: ACDI contains the PlepA photoreceptor and ABDI the PliaG one. Until a graded induction curve exists there is no way to choose between them, so both were carried. ABDI is the weaker of the two: clone V7 has been checked one junction at a time on separate gels and never all three at once.

One oddity is on record and unexplained. On our Western blots, the Level 2 Csn-AcdS-Histag construct ran at a lower apparent molecular weight than the Level 2 SamyQ-AcdS 2.1-Histag construct. The two constructs encode the same mature ACC deaminase sequence and differ only in their signal peptides and codon usage, and neither difference is large enough to explain the size shift we observed. We hypothesize that it may reflect differences in how the protein assembles: ACC deaminase can form oligomers, and the Csn-AcdS construct may represent a dimer, whereas the SamyQ-AcdS 2.1 one may form a higher order oligomer or aggregate that runs larger. We were not able to quantify aggregation, so this theory remains untested.   1. DDesign

    To engineer Bacillus subtilis to produce and secrete bioprotectants, the plasmid constructs carrying those sequences have to get into the host cells. Electroporation applies a high-voltage pulse across the cell membrane, forming the transient pores a plasmid needs to enter. While standard transformation methods in Bacillus subtilis can be significantly constrained by strain variability in efficiency, electroporation provides a more controllable, high-efficiency technique critical for expressing recombinant stress-mitigating proteins [15].

    The first design focused on introducing the construct Pveg-EEBD-RBS0-SamyQ-ACCD-v2t1-B0015 into Bacillus subtilis 168 through electroporation. The experiment aimed to evaluate whether the plasmid could be introduced successfully and determine which electroporation voltage produced the greatest number of bacterial colonies. Two voltages, 2.1 kV and 2.3 kV, were selected for testing in 2 mm gap cuvettes, with the resistance maintained at 300 Ω and the capacitance at 25 µF.

  2. BBuild

    Electrocompetent Bacillus subtilis 168 cells were prepared following the protocol from the DTU-Denmark iGEM team [14]: the overnight culture was diluted 16-fold and grown at 37 °C and 200 rpm to OD600 0.85 to 0.95, cooled on ice for 5 minutes, centrifuged at 5000 × g for 10 minutes, washed twice with ice-cold electroporation buffer and resuspended in one twentieth of the original culture volume. Aliquots were stored at −80 °C.

    For the pulse, plasmid DNA was mixed with 100 µL of electrocompetent cells and transferred to a pre-chilled 0.2 cm cuvette. The Gene Pulser Xcell was set to two voltage conditions, 2.1 and 2.3 kV, with resistance and capacitance held at 300 Ω and 25 µF. After the pulse, 2 mL of prewarmed SOC recovery medium was added immediately, the cells were recovered for 3 hours at 37 °C and 200 rpm, then plated on LB agar containing 5 µg/mL erythromycin and incubated overnight at 37 °C.

  3. TTest

    Colonies were counted on each plate to compare transformation efficiency across the electroporation conditions. Transformants were plated on erythromycin-supplemented LB agar so that the pulse voltage could be read against both cell viability and transformation frequency. 2.1 kV gave 21 distinct colonies; raising the pulse to 2.3 kV cut recovery to about 8, which we read as field-induced cell death.

  4. LLearn

    Transformation yield fell as field strength rose. 2.1 kV yielded 21 colonies, while 2.3 kV dropped sharply to 8. Higher voltages do permeabilise the membrane more, but at 2.3 kV the field strength most likely caused excessive cell lysis and irreversible membrane damage, compromising post-pulse viability. At the fixed 300 Ω and 25 µF, 2.1 kV balances pore formation against survival better, so we standardized the protocol at 2.1 kV.

Bioreactor

A hollow-fiber loop that keeps engineered cells on one side of a membrane and lets the protectant across. Eight cycles, from a loop that leaked at the pump head to four integrated runs on cells, the last of them holding a culture of Bacillus subtilis WB800 for seventy-seven hours with the loop correcting its own pH. The first three integrated runs were each ended early by a different failure; the fourth ran to a complete growth curve. What they produced between them is a strain that secretes detectable protectant, a hydraulic loop that reads reliably, a sterilization procedure we had to break something to get right, automated pH control, and a harvest that can be asked for: closing one valve lifts the pressure across the membrane and the permeate comes out. The reactor record sorts the same work into three tracks across three prototypes, and cycles 5 to 8 here are its integrated runs. The machine those cycles converge on is specified end to end in the user manual and built stage by stage in the assembly guide, and where those documents and an older cycle disagree on a number, the cycle below says which one the machine has.

What the two machine builds added

September’s run was every subsystem switched on at once with a person watching. What went on the machine after it is the part that lets the person look away, and cycles 6 and 7 are the runs on it. Five bus servos replaced hands: one on the pinch valve and four on the membrane’s own Luer valves, each with three positions, among them the shell sampling valve and the feed valve. pH control came with them, and it is deliberately one-sided. It adds base, with no acid and no carbon dioxide, so a pH that climbs raises an alarm and nothing else. It doses below the target minus the deadband, then waits at least a minute and until the pH has stopped moving before it doses again, and if three pulses in a row fail to shift the pH it latches a fault and stops: an empty base bottle and a blocked line look the same from the controller, and both of them want a person. Everything is driven from one web interface on the board, in English or Traditional Chinese, with a seven-inch screen on the machine for the times nobody has a laptop.

Where the interlocks live matters as much as what they check. A pinch that drives lumen-in pressure or transmembrane pressure past its limit for five samples running, a pinch held past the maximum pinch time, a servo over its thermal cut-out, a servo that fails to answer three times: each releases the valve and latches a fault, and pinching is refused until a person clears it. A pressure trip is separate and harder: any one of the three pressures over the trip limit for five samples stops both pumps, releases the pinch and refuses every pump command above zero until the pressure has come back down. These run on the microcontroller, not in the browser and not on the Linux side, so they keep working when the interface dies; and rebooting clears the microcontroller’s latch but not the other, which is the detail that stops a reboot from restarting a pump in an empty room.

The same document lists what the machine still will not do. There is no level sensing and no closed-loop feeding, so the reservoir and the permeate bottle are watched by an operator. No cleaning protocol for the membrane exists: a fouled module is replaced, not cleaned. Pressure monitoring is not allowed to stand in for looking: the bench and the incubator floor are checked by eye at every round, and again before a run is left alone. And the shell side is plated on the first run after every module change, with the harvest treated as live culture until that plate has grown nothing, because a 0.2 µm pore sorts by size and the only thing it is known to hold back is a whole cell.

The light plate apparatus (LPA) is a testing apparatus, not part of the control loop. It exists to answer one question: how the wavelength and intensity of applied light affect protectant production in engineered Bacillus subtilis. This is the apparatus the induction series ran on, and the build another team can reproduce from the assembly guide.

  1. DDesign

    The final build combines what the three cycles established: LEDs matched by measured output, intensity set by PWM in code, enclosed electronics, a fixed tube position, and a black holder that shields each well. Two changes carried in from cycle 3 define it. The base plate is screwed to the housing at four points instead of two, which spreads the load across the whole edge, and every soldered connection is reinforced. The tube holder is printed in black PLA at four wall loops, with a 1 mm wall between bores, so light from one well stops at the wall instead of reaching its neighbor.

    The geometry follows from the question the apparatus answers. Four wells per condition give four replicates; six columns on an 18 mm pitch give two wavelengths at three intensities; and a tube bore of 17 mm over its own LED fixes the distance between source and culture, so a condition is a number in the source and not a position on a bench.

  2. BBuild

    Six parts print in black and gray PLA: a base plate that closes the bottom and supports the perf board on four stands, two sliders that pass through the housing and bear both holders, the housing itself, the LED holder on its 18 mm grid, and the tube holder. The bought part is the perf board under the LED holder, which mounts the three IRLZ44N logic-level MOSFETs. Assembly takes four M6, four #10-32 and four M3 screws, and a screwdriver is the only tool it needs.

    Twenty-four LEDs sit in PCV-220 sockets, matched in fours from forty measured one by one. An Arduino Uno R4 WiFi drives the three MOSFETs, one per tier, and each MOSFET switches a green and a red column together.

  3. TTest

    The matching is measured. All forty LEDs were read one by one on a light meter in a printed fixture, at the same distance and orientation, and sorted into matched fours. Within a group the four LEDs differ by 0.4 to 1.8 %, against spreads of 26 % for green and 22 % for red before sorting.

    Three bench tests are built into the assembly, one after the LEDs are soldered, one after the MOSFETs are wired, and the second again once the stack is screwed together, so a column that stops answering its MOSFET is caught before a culture is in the tube.

    The apparatus then ran cultures. Bacillus subtilis WB800 grew under all six arms to OD600 0.91 to 1.01 at 24 hours, close to the fitted plateau of 1.005, and the induction series that followed is on Results.

  4. LLearn

    A commercial light plate apparatus is an expensive instrument, and the point of this one is that it is not. Six printed parts, one Arduino, three MOSFETs and US$65.52 give twenty-four cultures under six defined conditions, and the whole build is written down stage by stage in the assembly guide, with the duty cycle of each tier and the three bench tests, so another team can reproduce it rather than buy it.

    What the apparatus reports is the duty cycle it was set to, which is a stable and repeatable dose but not a photon flux. Putting the tiers in absolute units, and measuring uniformity between wells, is the measurement that would let these conditions be compared with another group’s. That is the next thing this instrument is for.

Hydroponics box

Where the protectant finally arrives. Four cycles on the tank and the printed plate that supports thirteen Arabidopsis seedlings at one depth however much medium is left. What was then grown on it is on Plants.

Three vessels that each hid something the operator needed to see

A hydroponic tank has to be dark for the roots and legible for the person running it, and the first three boxes could not be both at once.

Result
Three boxes tried and dropped: a 21.1 × 14.1 × 11.4 cm opaque tub, a 7.5 cm bought box that leaked, and a 6.5 × 6.5 × 10 cm clear box too narrow to grow in. A 1000 µL tip box replaced all three.
Changed next
Keep a clear tank and find the darkness somewhere else.

  1. DDesign

    The vessel has to contain enough ½ MS medium to last a run, keep light off the roots, leave board area for as many seed positions as a run needs, and come back from the autoclave intact so it can be used again.

  2. BBuild

    Prototype 1 was a large opaque tub, 21.1 × 14.1 × 11.4 cm, filled with about 500 mL of ½ MS medium under a foam board cut from sheet and taped black, carrying five agar-containing seed holders. Prototype 2 was a small bought box, 7.5 × 7.5 × 5.3 cm, with holders for the lids and labels on the box lid. Prototype 3 was a tall clear box, 6.5 × 6.5 × 10 cm, with a square black-taped foam board.

  3. TTest

    The first box wasted most of what it offered: the board had room for more seed holders than the five we used, the foam was too fragile to reuse, and cutting and taping it was slow and hard to scale up. The depth of the box made the root system difficult to observe, and the black wall made it difficult to tell when the medium went turbid. Prototype 2 leaked from two sites when the box was moved, the joint and the bottom, and black tape over the hole did not stop it; the lid fell into the box unless it was placed at a specific angle. Prototype 3 held opacity and depth together, and then the foam board fitted so tightly that it was hard to reach when the medium was changed, and the box was too narrow for the plants to grow and limited how many we could run in it.

  4. LLearn

    A 1000 µL tip box answered all of it at once. It is 12.8 × 10.1 × 9.5 cm, which does not take up much space and still gives the roots room; it is transparent, so the root system and the turbidity of the medium can both be read off the wall; it survives cleaning and autoclaving, so it is reused; and one board in it takes anything from 5 to 13 holes. Contamination was less frequent than in the two boxes before it.

    What a clear wall gives up is the darkness the roots need, and that debt is paid in cycle 4 from outside the tank.

A board cut by hand, set to a level by eye, and softened by the medium it sits on

Every hydroponic run starts with the same question: where are the roots in the liquid? A fixed board answers it once, on the day the run is set up, and then the answer drifts for a fortnight.

Result
Foam boards for every box, and three plates cut, taped and drilled at 300 mL, 1.5 L and 2 L. The level was set by hand before each run and topped up during it, and the foam went soft and broke in the water.
Changed next
Print the board, and put the flotation inside it so there is no level left to set.

  1. DDesign

    Labs that grow Arabidopsis in liquid medium improvise the holder: a piece of foam cut to size, a mesh taped over a container, or a fixed plate sitting above a reservoir. Conn and colleagues [6] set out the version we started from, seeds germinated on agar in punched tube lids and then moved onto a board. We did the same, and sized a plate for each of the three box volumes we had on the bench.

  2. BBuild

    Foam cut to the box, taped black, and drilled on a grid of seed positions; the agar-containing seed holders dropped into the holes. Three plates, for the 300 mL, 1.5 L and 2 L boxes, each hand-labeled in red tape. The medium sat underneath at a level set by eye before the run.

  3. TTest

    The level turned out to be a narrow window. It has to sit just under the board and as high as it can be got: too low and the roots lose contact, too high and the board floods. And it does not stay where it is put. The plants drink and the medium evaporates, so a level that was right at the start of a run is wrong by the end of it.

    The foam failed separately. It became soft after it stayed in the water for a long time, and a softened board breaks in the middle of changing the medium.

  4. LLearn

    Two faults with one answer each. Somebody has to watch the reservoir and top it up, and every top-up is a chance for conditions to drift between replicates, which is the variable the board was there to remove in the first place. And a board cut, taped and drilled for one box is not a part another lab can make the same way twice. So print the board, and stop setting a level at all.

Sealed air inside the frame, and four corner poles to trim what the arithmetic got wrong

If the board floats, the roots stay at the same depth however much liquid is left, and nobody has to top anything up. The question that replaces the level is the freeboard: how high the plate rides.

Result
A printed plate that floats on its own frame. The calculation did not get the freeboard right; washers did.
Changed next
Run the displacement calculation again on every design change until the poles are not doing anything.

  1. DDesign

    Move the flotation into the frame: sealed air chambers that are part of the structure, with nothing to glue on and nothing to come loose in the medium. Sizing them is a displacement problem. The plate has to displace its own mass, plus the seedlings, plus whatever water the printed part takes on, while sitting at the freeboard that puts the roots in the medium and the crowns above it.

  2. BBuild

    Printed in PLA on a standard FDM machine, pontoons hollow at 15 % gyroid infill with a teardrop cross-section that prints without support. A pole at each corner took washers, so the freeboard could be trimmed after printing without reprinting the whole part. A handle went on each side of the board, so the plate could be lifted out to change the medium without anyone touching the seedlings.

  3. TTest

    It floats, and the corner poles get used. One worked case from the drafts has the plate at roughly 180 g, where the pontoons came up short by about 10.7 cm³ of volume each. The printed plate was never weighed, so the case cannot be checked against the part that exists.

  4. LLearn

    The poles are the safety margin on a calculation we did not trust yet. They are also four parts, four washers and a decision per run. Every change to wall thickness or footprint moves the mass and the displacement at the same time, so the calculation has to be rerun on every design change, not once at the start.

The ballast poles came off, and the clear tank went inside a box that keeps the light out

The last change to the plate is a subtraction and the last change to the tank is an addition. Tuning the sealed volume closely enough makes the corner poles redundant, which removes four parts, four washer stacks and one judgment call per run; a black outer box gives back the darkness the clear tip box gave up in cycle 1.

Result
Sixteen printed parts, 42.27 cm³ of sealed air, 3.5 g of added load per millimeter of draft, and the tip box sitting inside a 14.7 × 11.5 × 6.2 cm light-blocking box.
Changed next
Weigh an assembled plate and read its freeboard off the load line, which is the one measurement that would close this track.

  1. DDesign

    Smaller footprint, clip-in handles so the plate lifts out of the reservoir without anyone touching the seedlings, and thirteen seed positions in rows of four, five and four. The buoyancy is computed from the STLs, not estimated: the two chambers contain 42.27 cm³ of sealed air, and down the pontoons’ straight sides the hull gains 3.51 cm³ per millimeter of draft.

  2. BBuild

    Sixteen parts in PLA, no supports, no adhesives and no fasteners. As drawn, the waterline sits 6.0 mm under the slab, a freeboard of 9.2 mm, where the assembled plate displaces 54.2 cm³.

    The tip box then goes inside a black light-blocking box of 14.7 × 11.5 × 6.2 cm with a notch cut in one side. Keeping the tank dark holds back algal growth, and the clear inner box still lifts out when we want to look at the root system.

  3. TTest

    The plate carried the salinity and heat assays on Plants: four boxes side by side on four identical plates, photographed from above through the run. No run on this plate has had its freeboard measured.

  4. LLearn

    Every 3.5 g added sinks it about 1 mm, so the sensitivity is known and the load line is drawn; what is missing is one weighing. Printed solid, an upper bound, the sixteen parts come to 92.6 g and would float with 1.9 mm of freeboard, which is the number that retires the 180 g worked case still in the drafts.
