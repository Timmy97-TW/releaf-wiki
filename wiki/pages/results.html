Results

Abstract

From engineered cells to precise plant protection.

ReLEAF brings together synthetic biology, plant experimentation, optogenetic control, continuous monitoring, and membrane-based bioreactor engineering. Rather than evaluating each component only in isolation, we aimed to test the major steps required for our proposed system to function: producing our protectants, evaluating their effects on plants, controlling biological activity with light, monitoring bacterial growth, releasing protectant through our bioreactor, and retaining engineered Bacillus subtilis within the system.

Key results

  • Protectant secretionACCD, LEA14 and BoPep4 all secreted by WB800; ACCD about three times the other two
  • Light controlGreen light switches ACCD on, and activity leads the protein by hours
  • The photometerR² 0.992 against silica, and 434 h of unattended monitoring
  • Plant rescueTiming decides the effect: pre- and post-treatment rescue, co-treatment does not
  • Containment and releaseActive ACCD in the cell-free shell for 72 h, no colonies on the shell side

Stress detection

Asks

Can sensor and weather data be turned into a decision about when to produce and when to apply?

Our planned interface puts a variety of inputs through AI, which assembles instructions for farmers on protectant application while actively monitoring conditions inside the machine. Our AIoT system is also what produces our protectant, activating the green light during optimal application windows.

Three models stand between the sensors and that decision, and each was scored on recorded data from our own runs: a fault detector on the instrument stream, a short-horizon forecast of culture density, and a growth prior carried from the light plate to the bioreactor. The architecture, the training and the interface are on Digital Twin; the four endpoints below are what the models returned.

Fault detection

An autoencoder trained on the healthy first 120 hours of run 3 scores two axes separately: a sensor axis on the reference beam, which never passes through the culture, and a process axis on the growth rate. They separate in both directions. At 120 to 160 h the process axis fired in 73% of windows and the sensor axis in none, and the density really was falling, 0.93 to 0.72. At 220 to 240 h the sensor axis fired in every window and the process axis in none, and the reference had stepped from 26.67 to 25.83 lux while the density trace still looked clean.

The instrument was therefore flagged at 216.3 h and the channel did not fail until 243.0 h: 26.7 h of lead, at a false-alarm rate of 6.1% on unseen quiet windows. The run itself is in Culture monitoring.

A rate fitted in a 150 µL plate well predicted the rate in a one-liter perfusion loop thirteen days later. Two things bound it. The fit runs over five hours in which a straight line describes the readings as well as an exponential does, so the rate comes from a stretch where the two shapes cannot be told apart; and the secondary endpoints stay unscored for the reason given when they were registered, that both depend on plate-reader quantities with no cross-calibration to the inline cuvette.

Field sensors

The crop-side input is a leaf sensor and an air sensor read together, and their difference is what says whether the canopy is wetter and cooler than the air around it. On 10 October both were held in air beside each other, with no leaf, at three sites of stable air temperature: three five-minute runs each, 540 paired readings. In air the two should agree.

They do not. The incubator holds near +1.8 °C and the stairwell near +1.3, while the room starts below zero and climbs past +1 across its three runs. An offset that changes with the site, and drifts within one, is an instrument characteristic and not a canopy signal, so no leaf-minus-air threshold can be set from these readings. What the test gives is the size of the correction the pair needs first: −0.2 to +1.8 °C.

No forecast has yet driven a light, and no light driven by a forecast has reached a culture, so this stage has not been joined to the next end to end.

Protectant production

Asks

Can our engineered Bacillus subtilis produce the intended protectants?

Following stress detection and optogenetic signal delivery, the next stage of ReLEAF is biological production. Our system uses engineered B. subtilis WB800 as the microbial chassis for producing plant-protective molecules in response to a controlled light signal. However, as the light inducible system is still being tested, we needed a separate circuit to obtain the protectants rapidly for subsequent functional assays.

Therefore, a fifth module named “Production Module” constitutively expresses the stress-mitigation proteins so that their maximal secretion and detection can be evaluated. It consists of the constitutive promoter Pveg, EEBD-RBS0, fusion ORF, and the B0015 terminator. The CDS options are AcdS, LEA, and BoPep4, each fused to a Csn or SamyQ secretion peptide and a 6x Histag, in wild-type, truncated and codon-optimized. These constructs were shown in Table 1.

Electroporation

To identify transformation under various electroporation conditions, two Bacillus subtilis strains, 168 and WB800, were evaluated under different electrical conditions to investigate their transformation performance.

Protein expression

To examine and quantify the secretion of our protectants, we performed three assays to evaluate protectant secretion, quantify protein concentration, and assess enzymatic activity. Each protectant contains a His-tag according to our construct design, allowing us to detect the target proteins by western blotting and assess their presence based on their expected molecular weights. Meanwhile, DNPH works alongside western blot to allow us to quantify the enzyme activity, determining how much active protectant is produced under the varying constructs of our engineered bacteria, with the different signal peptides. Specifically, one of our target protectants ACC deaminase is an enzyme that catalyzes the deamination of 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC), which we can then use the assay to detect the enzyme activity of the protein secreted by our engineered Bacillus subtilis.

Contributing to our project, ELISA quantifies the yield by reporting the protein concentration within the culture supernatants. Moreover, the quantified yield would allow us to account for the varying signal peptides' effect on secretion value based on preexisting articles. In retrospect, we used a varying signal peptide (SamyQ and Csn), affecting the secretion value, which would then impact the Western blot results, causing there to be two independent variables, which could contain error.

The construct we focus on most is for the protectant ACC deaminase, as the protectant highlights the results of our codon optimization. For the comparison, we have two constructs that vary in both the secretory signal peptide (Csn and SamyQ) fused with the ACC deaminase gene sequence (wild type vs codon-optimized v2t1). We quantify the ACC deaminase in culture supernatants by anti-His ELISA to compare the expression and secretion of the variant gene sequences. Though the variance in the signal peptide does influence the measured results, due to time constraints we were unable to account for the variance through new wet lab cloning. To isolate the visualization of the codon optimization, the signal peptide contribution is accounted for using published quantitative data. Though documented values are limited and come from different research: the native Csn signal peptide mediated 33.8% secretion of its cognate cargo within an E. coli system [9], while reaching near 72% of the best signal peptide within a 173-member B. subtilis library for cutinase [10]. Meanwhile, SamyQ has been reported to reach around 90% secretion of a heterologous α-amylase within B. subtilis [11]. Since the two signal peptides have not been directly compared in a single experiment, nor has either been documented with ACC deaminase, we present the codon optimization as a range calculated by dividing the raw concentration by the OD600 and then dividing the value by the signal peptide correction value of 90%/33.8% = 2.66.

All of our constructs were verified and successfully transformed into B. subtilis WB800, so we moved on to testing them. The results below follow the order in which we ran the experiments: first the activity and cofactor requirement of ACCD, then protectant production, then a comparison of wild-type and codon-optimized versions, and finally a bioreactor test.

DNPH assay: ACCD activity and the PLP requirement

We first used the DNPH assay to check the enzyme activity of ACCD and whether it needs pyridoxal phosphate (PLP), its coenzyme, to function. The samples supplied with PLP (csn+PLP and samyQ+PLP) showed higher activity than both csn without PLP and the WB800 control, so ACCD does need PLP to work well, and we kept this in mind for the experiments that followed (Figure 12).

ELISA: production of ACCD, LEA and BoPep4

Next, we used ELISA to test whether our strains produced the three protectants ACCD, LEA and BoPep4, and to measure how much of each was made. ACCD was produced at the highest level, about three times that of LEA or BoPep4 (Figure 13).

ELISA: wild-type and codon-optimized variants

We then ran more ELISA repeats to see how the codon-optimized variants compare with the wild-type versions. Collecting supernatants at 8 hr or 12 hr made little difference to the concentrations. For ACCD, AcdS 2.1 (about 9 µg/mL) was lower than wild-type AcdS (about 12 to 13 µg/mL). For BoPep4, both BoPep4 2.1 and BoPep4 2.2 were generally higher than wild-type BoPep4, most clearly at 12 hr (Figure 14).

Bioreactor test: ELISA and DNPH over time

Finally, we tested the strain in a bioreactor and ran ELISA and DNPH on the same time points. The amount of ACCD measured by ELISA was highest at 12 h (Figure 15), but the enzyme activity measured by DNPH was highest at 18 h (Figure 16). We therefore think ACCD may need a fixed period of time to become active after it is made. This does not need to be long, but it means the amount of protein and its activity do not peak together.

The chromoprotein reporter

In our project, AmilCP is a chromoprotein which serves as a reporter to visualize and quantify the successful secretion of the target protein from our engineered Bacillus subtilis in both the LPA and bioreactor. Since AmilCP displays a blue/purple hue, we are able to utilize absorbance as a quantifiable and more direct readout compared to biochemical assays. Considering feasibility, the enzymatic assay for ACCD requires DNPH that is hazardous to handle, as well as additional time and resources. Fluorescence-based reporters were not feasible, as we could not obtain a fluorescence microplate reader. Western blotting and ELISA are also time-consuming and costly. Therefore, a chromoprotein was convenient for us to verify the success of secretion.

Despite repeated experiments, the medium remained colorless (Figure 17). Based on an educated guess, the results could be due to folding failure or the protectant was never successfully created and secreted. Spectrophotometric measurement of the supernatant also showed no absorbance peak at the expected wavelength (588 nm): the A588 on the B. subtilis medium without AmilCP was 0.082 and with AmilCP was 0.081, and the A588 on B. subtilis without AmilCP expression suspended in PBS was 1.515, which bears no significant difference to the B. subtilis with AmilCP expression suspended in PBS, which was 1.628. The results showed that there is no chromogenic AmilCP performed in our liquid culture or bacteria.

A likely explanation for the lack of results is because of the folding of AmilCP, as the secretion of proteins of the originating Bacillus family has been well documented within literature to become severely damaged due to the bottlenecks in secretion pathways, often caused by slow or incompatible folding [13]. Taking this into consideration, AmilCP is a β-barrel chromoprotein that folds rapidly in the cytoplasm, which may explain the inefficient secretion, because the Sec pathway in B. subtilis requires proteins to remain unfolded during translocation [12]. We therefore reasoned that AmilCP and other chromoproteins cannot be secreted efficiently due to the incompatibility between the bacteria and proteins, and transferred our focus towards GanA.

Optogenetic control

Asks

Can the light plate deliver a controlled signal for optogenetic activation?

ReLEAF depends on light as the external control signal connecting environmental prediction to biological production. We therefore tested the light plate to determine whether it could provide reproducible illumination across the culture area.

We have successfully assembled our optogenetic circuit, which combines a red/green light-sensing system that converts light into a gene expression signal with a module producing our target protectant, either ACCD or ACCD 2.1 (Figures 5 and 6). The cells are therefore built to respond to light. Following our successful cloning of our Level 1 Green-Light Responsive Module, we then inserted this construct into a Level 2 vector alongside our PCB Production Module, Light Sensing Module and Transcriptional Module to form our complete optogenetic system. However, this does not show how strongly they respond to a given amount of light or if the light itself affects growth.

The Light Plate Apparatus (LPA) is an open-source hardware, a 24-well plate with an independently programmable LED under each well, so identical cultures can be held at different red and green light intensities in one experiment. We used it to check whether light can act as a reliable dial for protectant production; we checked whether 2%, 12% and 100% light gives clearly separated levels of expression, and whether any setting slows growth. Growth was read by OD600 and protein production by Western blot, and later by ELISA and DNPH assays.

Growth under light

We measured OD600 first because we did not know whether our bacteria could grow normally in the LPA under red and green light, especially since green light may place a heavy burden on the cells. The growth curves show that the cultures grew normally under both red and green light at 100% intensity, so light exposure in the LPA does not hinder growth (Figure 18).

Protein over time

Western blotting was used to identify and measure ACCD produced after Bacillus subtilis is exposed to different intensities of red and green light.

Since the L2-Csn-AcdS (ABDI) construct had been successfully assembled, we first used it to test whether light intensity and exposure time would affect the amount of protectant produced. The LPA was used to deliver light-on and light-off signals. Cultures were placed directly into the LPA and sampled every 2 hr from 0 to 8 hr (Figure 19). The data showed an increase in protectant expression with increasing green-light intensity, and expression also increased over time. Taken together, these results suggest that our optogenetic circuit is functional. However, the red light group also showed high expression, comparable to that of the highest green light intensity. This may be due to spectral leakage from the green channel into the red channel. In addition, the ABDI strain could not be recovered from glycerol stock, so every repeat required fresh electroporation. We therefore switched to the codon-optimized AcdS 2.1 (ABDQ), which does not have this problem.

Because we switched from AcdS to AcdS 2.1, we first tested whether ABDQ could be expressed and whether expression changed over time under green-light induction. A short term light response experiment was therefore performed (Figure 20). Supernatant samples were collected at 3, 6, and 8 hr after the onset of green light, following a 16 h dark culture. Western blot showed that the secreted protein was detected at all three time points. In addition, quantification of the western blot indicated that protectant expression decreased at 8 hr compared with 3 and 6 hr.

In contrast to the short term experiment, we next performed a long-term light response experiment to determine whether short-term induction was sufficient for protectant production (Figure 21). Cells were cultured in the LPA under green or red light for 13 to 16 h, and supernatant samples were collected at 13 and 16 h. Western blot showed clear, bright bands, indicating high protein accumulation after long term induction. Quantification showed greater accumulation in the green-light group than in the red-light group. These results indicate that protectant accumulation is higher under green light than red light, consistent with light dependent regulation.

ELISA and DNPH assay

Following the western blot results, ELISA and DNPH assays were introduced as more precise quantitative methods to measure ACCD accumulation and activity under short term and long term light induction. Competitive ELISA replaced the Western blot and estimated how much ACCD was in each sample, as a concentration. The DNPH assay measured ACCD activity by detecting α-ketobutyrate (α-KB), the product of the enzyme, and so showed whether the ACCD works. Running both on the same samples separated how much ACCD was present from how active it was. We ran them in two sets of experiments.

Short-term light response

The first set was a new experiment. Cultures were grown for 12 h in the dark, then placed under green or red light, and supernatants were collected 3, 6 and 8 h after illumination began.

The ELISA showed no change over time. ACCD was already present at the baseline (about 3.0 µg/mL before illumination) and measured 2.6 to 3.2 µg/mL at every time point under both colors, well above the plasmid-free control (about 1.3 µg/mL), indicating that the cultures produced ACCD but the amount did not rise with light (Figure 22). In contrast, the DNPH assay showed an increase in activity. Activity rose from the baseline (about 2.7 on the plotted axis) to about 18 under green light and 16 under red light by 8 h, and green was higher than red at every time point (about 8 vs 6 at 3 h, 12 vs 9 at 6 h and 18 vs 16 at 8 h; Figure 23). However, the gap was clearest at 6 h, and the error bars overlapped at 3 h and 8 h. Taken together, the amount of His-tagged ACCD stayed the same while its activity kept rising and leaned toward green. This may be because the protein takes time to become active: the ELISA counts the His-tagged protein whether or not it is working, whereas the DNPH assay only detects the active enzyme. We have not tested this directly.

Long-term light response

The second set repeated the design of Figure 21. Cultures were placed directly into the LPA under green or red light at 100% intensity, and supernatants were collected at 13 h and 16 h.

The ELISA showed about 12 to 13 µg/mL of ACCD in all four conditions, with no clear difference between green and red light or between 13 hr and 16 hr (Figure 24). The DNPH activity did not follow this pattern (Figure 25). At 13 hr, green light gave the highest activity (about 10 nmol α-KB mL−1 h−1, with a large error bar) compared with about 0.7 under red light, which suggests responsiveness to green light. However, by 16 hr the activity under green light had fallen to about 1, while red light was about 3.7. Because the amount of ACCD barely changed while its activity varied, the two assays gave different information, and the 13 hr green result was not repeated at 16 hr.

What it settles

The light plate delivered red and green light to the Bacillus subtilis cultures without slowing growth: at 100% intensity, both colors grew alike through 8 h and reached an OD600 of about 1.0 at 24 h (1.026 red, 1.017 green). On Western blots, the original ABDI construct gave an unclear result because the red 100% reference also showed its strongest band at 8 h, so we switched to the codon optimized ABDQ construct. With ABDQ, green light alone switched ACCD expression on, and green lanes were somewhat stronger than red at 13 h and 16 h, although the difference was small and not yet quantified. ELISA and the DNPH assay then gave more precise readings. In the short-term set, the amount of His-tagged ACCD stayed flat while its activity rose over time and leaned toward green, which may be because the protein takes time to become active. In the long-term set, ACCD amounts were again similar under both colors, and the high green-light activity at 13 h was not maintained at 16 h. The similar ELISA values across conditions may be due to too many samples in the LPA at once, so that light from neighboring wells affected each other. In addition, CcaSR needs red light at about 672 nm to switch off, but our red LEDs emit at 640 nm, which may be why red light did not turn expression off efficiently. We have not tested either explanation directly. Taken together, these results suggest that the plate can serve as our light-control platform and that ABDQ is expressed under green light, but they do not yet show a clean, graded response to light intensity. Before relying on light as a control signal, we will quantify the blots against a loading control, add a WB800 lane without the plasmid, and repeat the ELISA and DNPH assays with biological replicates.

Culture monitoring

Asks

Can the inline photometer continuously monitor changes in culture density?

To monitor culture growth without repeatedly removing samples from the bioreactor, we developed an inline dual-beam photometer and evaluated its performance through long-term monitoring and independent turbidity calibration.

Six runs

We conducted six bioreactor runs between June and October to evaluate the photometer's performance. Measurements from the first two runs were collected manually, while the remaining four used continuous inline monitoring without manual sampling. Run 3 served as our endurance test, demonstrating the photometer's ability to monitor a culture continuously for over 18 days without manual sampling. Over 434.2 hours, the system recorded measurements every ten minutes, producing 2671 valid readings out of 2720. However, the reference beam began showing irregular fluctuations around 220 hours, and the optical density measurements became unreliable at 243.0 hours, with variability increasing five- to tenfold. This failure provided valuable data for developing the fault detection model described in Stress detection, which identified the issue at 216.3 hours, before the measurement channel became unusable.

Run 5 experienced a different type of failure. Although OD600 initially increased from 0.10 to 0.30 over nine hours, the readings subsequently declined despite continued culture growth. Approximately one-sixth of the measurement windows contained over 20% bubbles, while the reference signal varied by a factor of 4.8. As a result, measurements after 10.6 hours could no longer reliably represent culture growth.

To address these issues, we made two improvements for run 6: reducing the permeate flow rate from 24 to 12 mL/min and introducing an acceptance filter that retained the last valid reading when measurements failed quality checks. Of the 1083 measurement windows, 31 were rejected, none exceeded 20% bubbles, and reference signal variation remained within 17%. These improvements produced a stable OD600 curve, increasing from 0.14 to 2.71 over 37 hours. Beyond approximately 2.7, the sample signal dropped to one-tenth of the reference, triggering a low-signal warning and indicating the instrument's upper measurement limit under these conditions.

However, two limitations remain. First, no simultaneous manual samples were collected to independently validate the inline measurements of these runs; rather we only performed calibrations via TiO2 and SiO2 suspension turbidity measurements where we collected manual samples to compare with our benchtop spectrophotometer, the BioDrop. Second, the photometer measures turbidity rather than cell concentration directly, meaning the recorded curves cannot be interpreted as exact cell counts without further calibration.

Calibration

To determine whether the photometer responded predictably to increasing turbidity, we calibrated it against two different particulate standards. Each provided a nonliving turbidity reference, allowing us to characterize the optical response independently of changes in microbial growth, and testing a second material allowed us to evaluate whether the relationship observed with the first was reproducible.

We first calibrated the photometer using titanium dioxide (TiO₂) in August, followed by silicon dioxide (SiO₂) to align with the iGEM interlaboratory protocol [5]. Both experiments involved gradually increasing particle concentrations in a continuously stirred loop and comparing the photometer readings with a BioDrop spectrophotometer at each concentration.

The inline photometer showed strong agreement with the BioDrop spectrophotometer in both calibration experiments. With titanium dioxide (TiO₂), it achieved a slope of 1.018 and an R² of 0.995 up to an OD600 of 1.2, with more stable readings than the BioDrop above approximately 30 mg. The silicon dioxide (SiO₂) experiment also showed a strong linear relationship (R² = 0.992), with only one-tenth of the reference instrument's residual variation. These results support the photometer's ability to produce consistent measurements across different materials, including with the updated prototype.

The SiO₂ experiment provided further evidence of measurement consistency through a dilution and recovery test at 137.6 minutes. The OD600 decreased from 2.3610 to 0.7943 after dilution, then returned to 2.3665 when the original concentration was restored. The dilution ratios were 2.97 and 2.98, and the final reading differed from the initial value by less than 0.2%. These results demonstrate that the photometer responded proportionally to changes in particle concentration and recovered its original reading when the concentration was restored, supporting its use for comparing measurements over time.

However, neither calibration directly measures cell concentration, as both experiments used nonliving particles. While the results confirm a linear relationship between particle concentration and turbidity, converting optical density measurements into cell counts would require additional calibration against plate counts, which we have not yet conducted.

Protectant function

Asks

Do our protectants actually improve plant stress response?

ELISA

To evaluate the production of our engineered bioprotectants, we used competitive ELISA to estimate the concentrations of His-tagged ACC deaminase (ACCD), LEA14, and BoPep4 in bacterial culture samples. The assay provided a quantitative measure of detectable recombinant protein, complementing Western blot analysis and enzymatic activity assays. By comparing the estimated protein concentrations across different constructs and experimental conditions, we aimed to assess bioprotectant production and identify potential differences in expression and secretion.

Six His-tagged protein standards ranging from 0.093 to 3 µg/mL were measured in duplicate. A logarithmic regression was fitted to the relationship between protein concentration and percentage of maximum binding (%B/B₀), producing an R² value of 0.9967. The decreasing %B/B₀ values with increasing protein concentration are consistent with the competitive ELISA principle.

Notably, BoPep4 v2t1 increased from 5.230 to 6.323 µg/mL, whereas the original BoPep4 construct decreased from 6.090 to 4.744 µg/mL. The WB800 control also produced detectable assay signals, indicating that the estimated concentrations should not be interpreted as recombinant protein-specific yields without additional validation. Furthermore, several calculated concentrations exceeded the standard-curve range, limiting the reliability of absolute quantification. Together, these measurements provide a preliminary comparison of apparent protein abundance across the engineered constructs.

DNPH assay

Plant phenotype provides whole-organism evidence, but we also wanted to directly evaluate the biological activity of our protectant.

Quantifying ACCD protectant activity in our engineered Bacillus subtilis (WB800) relies on measuring the enzymatic cleavage of ACC into ammonia and alpha-ketobutyrate. The 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) assay provides a highly sensitive method for quantifying acid accumulation by forming hydrazone complexes, which can absorb strongly at 540 nm during alkaline derivatization.

Without exogenous PLP supplementation, the csn strain exhibits product output nearly identical to the WB800 baseline, suggesting that its endogenous ACCD activity is negligible relative to the host background. Adding PLP significantly enhances csn performance, increasing total product output by 13.7 nmol (+0.119 mM alpha-ketobutyrate) above the WB800 baseline, demonstrating the importance of PLP supplementation for optimal ACCD activity. Notably, SamyQ with PLP achieves even higher catalytic activity, producing 89.7 nmol of total product, an increase of 30.0 nmol (+0.209 mM alpha-ketobutyrate) over the baseline. This represents a 2.19× increase in net product synthesis compared with Csn+PLP, highlighting the superior catalytic performance of SamyQ under PLP-supplemented conditions.

Culture duration, 16 hr against 13 hr under red and green light

This trial evaluated the effect of maturation time on optogenetic efficiency of switching under continuous red light (630 nm) versus green light (525 nm).

At 13 hours, green light (525 nm) induced strong gene expression (0.1249 mM vs 0.0089 mM for red light), which demonstrated a 14.03 times larger induction ratio. At 16 hours, a total inversion occurred, showing red light (630 nm) yields higher product accumulation (0.0923 mM vs 0.0130 for green light). This indicates phase-dependent optogenetic state switching and a potential metabolic turnover of alpha-ketobutyrate during extended incubation time.

Bioreactor v2.1 accumulation time-course

In order to map our product's extracellular stability, we ran a continuous 72 hour bioreactor run with periodic detecting and sampling.

Across the 36 hour run, the extracellular alpha-ketobutyrate accumulation shows a phase-dependent kinetics. Starting from a baseline concentration of −0.0399 mM at 1 hour (OD 540 nm = −0.015), the active enzyme secretion initiated by 6 hours (0.0333 mM, OD 540 nm = 0.003) and maintained steady accumulation through 12 hours (0.0293 mM, OD 540 nm = 0.002). The accumulation reaches its peak at 18 hours, which yields a maximum alpha-ketobutyrate concentration of 0.3709 (OD 450 nm = 0.086). After this peak, the culture enters a decay phase where product concentration declines to 0.2245 mM at 24 hours and reaches a minimum clearance level of 0.1309 mM at 30 hours, before showing a slight secondary rise to 0.1757 mM at 36 hours.

Pre-cultured 12 hour switchable light exposure

Cells were pre-cultured for 12 hr before exposure to controlled green or red light for up to 8 hours.

When pre-cultured Bacillus subtilis cells (12 h) were subjected to targeted exposure of light over an 8 hour period, both green and red light treatment show progressive and time-dependent increase in alpha-ketobutyrate accumulation compared to the pre-illumination baseline (0 h, 0.0333 mM, OD 540 nm = 0.003). At 3 hours of illumination, the red light shows faster initial induction, accumulating 0.1160 mM (3.48× increase over baseline, OD 540 nm = 0.023), compared to 0.0564 mM concentration under the green light (1.69× increase over baseline, OD 540 nm = 0.009). By 6 hours, the green light induction rose to 0.0916 mM (2.75× increase over baseline, OD 540 nm = 0.017), while red light temporarily dropped to 0.0537 mM (1.61× increase over baseline, OD 540 nm = 0.008). The peak expression for both channels occurred at 8 hours of continuous illumination, where green light achieved 0.2123 mM (6.38× increase over baseline, OD 540 nm = 0.037) and red light reached a maximum accumulation of 0.2475 mM (7.43× increase over baseline, OD 540 nm = 0.046).

Biological assay

To determine whether the protectants actually benefit plants, we used Arabidopsis thaliana as our plant model. We compared plants under normal conditions, stress conditions, and stress conditions supplemented with protectant. Our plant-validation experiments were designed to move from highly controlled agar-based testing toward a hydroponic system that more closely reflects our intended application pathway.

Agar-based salinity stress

To evaluate the efficacy of our engineered plant protectants in a controlled environment, we established an agar-based screening pipeline utilizing Arabidopsis thaliana as our standard model organism. The primary goal of these agar assays was to determine whether exogenous application of our protectants, such as ACC deaminase, LEA14, and BoPep4, could mitigate the adverse phenotypic and biochemical effects of salt stress. To maximize data accuracy and reproducibility, while monitoring and ranging protectants' application timing, we deployed the horizontal agar system as our platform. Initial gradient testing identified 100 mM NaCl as the optimal baseline stress concentration, severe enough to induce inhibition of plant growth without causing immediate systemic lethality.

Following the successful production and secretion of ACC deaminase (ACCD) from our engineered biological systems, we conducted targeted biological assays to evaluate whether the purified enzyme retains measurable enzymatic and protective function on plants under abiotic stress. Initial plate screenings evaluating filter-sterilized ACCD across different treatment timings (0, 75, 100 mM NaCl) revealed moderate rescue effectiveness. Although overall phenotypic improvements were modest due to baseline plate contamination during early filtering trials, post-treatment groups subjected to salt stress exhibited visible recovery comparable to baseline controls.

Under non-stressed conditions, 75 mM and 100 mM NaCl control groups show relevant chlorosis characterized by white and yellow leaves (Figure 42e, j), consistent with the applied salt stress. Despite contamination, all pre-treatment ACC deaminase groups showed the greater rosette area, dense foliage, and more extensive petioles and root growth (Figure 42b, f, j). Under the pre-treatment ACCD 100 mM NaCl group, leaves turned slightly yellow or white, and showed anthocyanin accumulation (Figure 42j), while pre-treatment ACCD with 75 mM NaCl plants also show slight chlorosis, overall still retaining the majority of chlorophyll pigments. Under groups treated with ACC deaminase at co-treatment timing, as salt stress concentration progressively increases from 0 to 100 mM, seedlings showed severe inhibition of plant growth under salinity stress (Figure 42g, k). This result indicates that pre-treatment or post-treatment of ACC deaminase showed rescue effect rather than co-treatment.

As shown in Figure 43a, fresh weight measurements were generally consistent with the visual phenotypes in the ACC deaminase pre-treatment groups (Figure 42b, f, j). In the pre-treatment group, fresh weight was highest under 100 mM NaCl, followed by 75 mM and 0 mM NaCl, consistent with the denser foliage observed at 100 mM NaCl. This result indicated that ACC deaminase pretreatment improves plant fresh weight under salinity stress.

As shown in Figure 44 the plants exhibited less severe damage under 75 mM NaCl but more stress symptoms under 100 mM NaCl compared with the untreated controls (Figure 44a, d, and g). Growth declined visibly as the NaCl concentration increased, with progressively smaller leaf blades and a shrinking rosette area. The untreated, non-stressed control (Figure 44a) shows lush growth and dense foliage, which serves as the baseline for comparing the treatment groups with normally grown plants.

Phenotypic observation of the BoPep4 co-treated plates (Figure 44b, e, h) shows progressive salt damage as the NaCl concentration increases from 0 to 100 mM. At 100 mM NaCl, this damage is characterized by extensive chlorosis, with yellow-to-green leaf margins, and partial leaf bleaching. In addition, every BoPep4 co-treated plate shows a green-to-yellow gradient from leaf to leaf, with round, spatulate leaves. This chlorosis across all co-treatment groups suggests that BoPep4 applied simultaneously with the stress has an inherent inhibitory effect.

Under 75 mM NaCl, the co-treated plates contain seedlings of varying sizes, mostly green rosettes with many chlorotic leaves, some whitish patches near the edges and little browning. Under 100 mM NaCl (Figure 44h), the plate shows the most severe phenotype: chlorosis, whitish stems or hypocotyl bases, bleaching and early necrosis on some leaves.

Even without salt stress, the BoPep4 co-treated plates (Figure 44b) show more inhibited growth than the mock control, indicating that co-treatment with BoPep4 is inadequate, or even harmful under salinity stress. As shown in Figure 44, plants with post-treatment BoPep4 show green and denser foliage compared to the corresponding untreated group at 75 mM and 100 mM NaCl. Only slight chlorosis under the 75 mM NaCl stress is shown, yet with higher 100 mM NaCl stress, post-treated plants even produce lush growth similar to the untreated non-stressed control.

Based on the qualitative images shown in Figure 46, all of the samples from this trial demonstrated positive growth conditions compared to previous experimental trials, yet the differences between stressed, unstressed, treated, and untreated were especially difficult to determine. Comparing the Arabidopsis plates under pre-treatment of LEA, the 75 mM NaCl groups demonstrated fuller foliage growth compared to the 100 mM and the control group (Figure 46b, e, h). A similar situation occurred with the post-treatment group, where the 75 mM NaCl group showed both fuller leaf area and darker leaf color (Figure 46f, i), further elaborating on our idea that LEA works best under medium salinity conditions.

Solely looking at chlorophyll data, there is a consistent trend between the plant groups with LEA14 treatment, no matter pre- or post-treatment. As shown in Figure 47b, both treatment times of LEA14 have a better performance, also acknowledged as greatest chlorophyll retention, on the 75 mM NaCl groups, followed by 100 mM then the 0 mM NaCl groups. This further supports the idea that our protectants perform the best under medium stress conditions and instead bring harm when released without the highest level of demand. Looking at the statistics in Figure 47b, the significance is shown through 3 asterisks, marking its ability to reject the null hypothesis. This significance helps in the conclusion that LEA14 works the best at 75 mM of NaCl.

Agar-based heat stress

We next evaluated plant response under controlled heat-stress conditions. Plants were divided into control and heat-stress groups, allowing us to test different treatment times of ACCD and viewing its benefits through a fixed treatment schedule.

Based on the data from the ACCD agar heat stress trial 2, ACCD demonstrated a better rescue effectiveness than the previous heat stress experiment. Through the qualitative phenotypic images in Figure 48, there is an obvious difference between the growth conditions of the control heat stress group and the post-treatment heat stress group. Especially in the leaves, it is demonstrated that the control heat stress group showed severe chlorosis while the post-treatment group showed higher biomass and chlorophyll content. Connecting to the chlorophyll content data in Figure 49, the standardized chlorophyll content for the post-treatment heat stress group was 14% higher than that of the heat-stress control, further emphasizing how the treatment of ACCD compares to no treatment. While the co-treatment and pre-treatment groups did not demonstrate as beneficial a rescue effectiveness as the post-treatment group, they managed to retain a stable amount of chlorophyll throughout 9 test subjects.

Based on the heat stress data, the application timing of ACCD makes a significant difference in determining its effectiveness. Looking at the chlorophyll content in Figure 49c, it is demonstrated that under 37 °C heat stress, adding ACCD after the stress and after the recovery period helps the Arabidopsis retain the most chlorophyll, followed by co-treatment then pre-treatment. Based on our preliminary research, increased ethylene production in plants occurs at two time points: when the heat is administered and when the heat is removed. After we take the plants out of the 37 °C incubator, they enter their recovery stage, where ethylene is produced to help manage cellular damage and adjust metabolism (Huang et al., 2023; Djanaguiraman & Prasad, 2010). The experiment allowed us to compare plant performance under heat stress and determine whether protectant treatment improved survival, growth, or recovery.

Hydroponic salinity stress

To move beyond agar-based proof of concept, we tested protectant performance in our self-made standardized hydroponic platform. The hydroponic system allowed plant roots to interact directly with the treatment solution and provided a closer approximation of how protectants could eventually be delivered through irrigation or nutrient circulation. To test protectant effectiveness on rescuing Arabidopsis under salinity stress, we treated 11-day-old Arabidopsis with different concentrations of BoPep4 and applied 100 mM salinity stress.

Arabidopsis showed anthocyanin accumulation from BoPep4 dilutions of 1/40 to 1/100 when no salinity stress was applied. On the other hand, the 1/40 WB800 group and 0.1× MS medium group did not show anthocyanin accumulation. This shows that BoPep4 inhibits plant growth when stress isn't applied. When comparing different dilutions of BoPep4 under 100 mM, the group treated with BoPep4 at 1/40 dilution remained green after salinity stress, and had the highest fresh weight and highest chlorophyll contents per plant. Therefore, BoPep4 concentrations greater than or equal to 1/40 improve Arabidopsis' salinity tolerance, while concentrations lower than 1/40 do not affect plant growth under salinity stress.

Hydroponic heat stress

In the hydroponic system, heat stress was also tested to further evaluate plant response under a more application-relevant setup. In order to evaluate the effectiveness of our protectants on rescuing plants under heat stress, we tested the effects of different dilution factors of ACCD and BoPep4 on Arabidopsis under 37 °C heat stress conditions.

After 4 days of 37 °C heat stress, Arabidopsis showed lower fresh weight and chlorophyll contents. ACCD at 1/40, 1/60, and 1/80 dilutions demonstrated higher chlorophyll contents when compared to the control group. However, the 1/100 ACCD dilution group showed lower chlorophyll contents than other groups treated with ACCD, and the data is close to the control group. Therefore, ACCD improves Arabidopsis' heat resistance when concentrations are greater than or equal to 1/80.

37 °C heat stress caused significant damage to Arabidopsis, significantly decreasing its fresh weight values and chlorophyll contents. BoPep4 slightly rescued Arabidopsis at 1/40 dilution, as shown by higher chlorophyll contents than both the 1/40 WB800 group and the 0.1× MS medium group. BoPep4 did not show rescue effectiveness at concentrations of 1/60, 1/80, and 1/100, which had low chlorophyll contents closer to the control group. In conclusion, BoPep4 shows slight rescue for Arabidopsis under heat stress when its concentration is greater than or equal to 1/40. This provides an additional layer of validation between protein expression and plant phenotype.

Soil salinity stress

Salt behaves differently in soil platforms compared to the agar trials; therefore, the salinity concentrations and protectant doses are required to be evaluated and fine tuned to allow for maximum observation differences in rescue effectiveness. We evaluated two established soil salinization protocols on 2-week-old Arabidopsis thaliana in growth chambers, including a top-down wash method using 100, 200, and 300 mM NaCl solutions and a sub-irrigation accumulation method. While initial trials with gradual ramps or subirrigation resulted in insufficient stress due to freshwater flushing and ion leaching, we resolved these hydrological challenges by implementing a sequential top-drenching protocol. We apply equal volumes from 30 to 50 mL per pot of 100 mM, 200 mM, and 300 mM NaCl consecutively without intermediate freshwater irrigation. Bottom tray reservoirs were maintained at a depth of 1 to 2 cm around 200 to 400 mL to keep the root zone in continuous equilibrium, establishing an optimal soil stress model to evaluate the protective efficacy of engineered microbial protectants across pre-treatment, co-treatment, and post-treatment windows.

A direct comparison between the sub-irrigation accumulation method (Kim et al., 2025) and the top-down wash or drench method (Cheng et al., 2013) was evaluated on 2-week-old Arabidopsis thaliana.

ACCD pretreatment and cotreatment did not visibly slow the bleaching process, while post-treatment showed a limited apparent effect on chlorosis. Under our experimental conditions, 100 mM NaCl was insufficient to induce clear visible stress, whereas stress symptoms became apparent at 300 mM NaCl. Establishing 300 mM NaCl as a suitable stress baseline allowed us to refine our experimental conditions and further investigate treatment timing. Since ACCD was applied one day after the initial 300 mM NaCl exposure, this post-treatment approach can be considered a delayed form of cotreatment initiated after salinity stress has developed, allowing us to investigate the potential effects of ACCD application under established stress conditions.

Soil heat stress

Transitioning heat stress assays into soil platforms requires re-evaluating incubation parameters and application regimens, as thermal responses in soil differ significantly from controlled agar trials. We adapted established thermo-priming frameworks to 2-week-old Arabidopsis thaliana grown in soil by exposing plants to 37 °C for 5 days in an incubator, followed by a 5-day recovery period at 22 to 25 °C in a growth chamber, to establish optimal parameters for testing engineered microbial protectants such as ACC deaminase. We systematically evaluate three application timings relative to heat exposure: pre-treatment to initiate cellular priming, co-treatment applied during heat exposure, and post-treatment during recovery, measuring post-stress survival, vegetative growth, chlorophyll retention, and anthocyanin accumulation.

Membrane bioreactor

Asks

Can a perfusion hollow-fiber membrane bioreactor produce protectant and keep the engineered bacteria inside?

Containment and release

The hollow-fiber membrane (HFM) module divides the system into a cell-containing lumen loop and a cell-free shell compartment, allowing secreted ACC deaminase (ACCD) to be recovered from the shell permeate without discharging the engineered Bacillus subtilis.

The lumen loop draws culture from a magnetically stirred medium reservoir and carries it through a peristaltic pump, an inline flow meter, an inline photometer, and the HFM module before returning it to the reservoir through a vent filter. Three pressure transducers report feed (lumen inlet), retentate (lumen outlet), and permeate (shell) pressure (Figure 56). The shell compartment is closed except for a single outlet that discharges into a dedicated collection bottle, so permeate leaves the system only when that valve is opened. In the final configuration, the valve is servo-actuated, so material is harvested on demand rather than as a continuous bleed.

Western blots of samples taken during the first integrated run confirmed selective passage of the product across the membrane (Figure 57). Proteins from cell lysate, lumen supernatant, and shell permeate were probed with an anti-His antibody directed against the C-terminal 6×His tag of recombinant ACCD. By approximately 40 h, bands migrating between 35 and 48 kDa were detected in all three fractions, consistent with the theoretical mass of the recombinant enzyme (~41 kDa). The product therefore reaches the shell side while the cell-associated fraction remains in the lumen.

To verify that bacteria on the lumen side would not escape the bioreactor, a simple containment test was performed. Medium was collected from both the lumen and shell sides while the bioreactor was running, and both samples were plated. After 24 h at 37 °C, the plates were compared. No detectable B. subtilis colonies were observed in the shell-side sample under the tested conditions. This provides preliminary evidence that the hollow-fiber membrane retained bacterial cells during the tested run.

Our containment evidence comes from two plating experiments, neither of which yielded bacterial colonies in samples collected from the shell side of the bioreactor. In the first, conducted on June 6, 2 mL of shell-side liquid was plated; the second compared bacterial growth in lumen and shell-side samples. Each experiment involved only a single plating without replicates; however, the incubation conditions for the June sample were not documented.

The containment mechanism relies on the difference between bacterial cell size and membrane pore size. Rod-shaped B. subtilis cells typically measure 0.8 to 2.0 µm in width and up to 3.0 µm in length, making them substantially larger than the membrane's 0.2 µm pores. Smaller particles, however, including cellular debris and vesicles measuring 20 to 200 nm, may still pass through the membrane alongside secreted proteins.

Together, these results are consistent with bacterial retention by the hollow-fiber membrane, but they do not establish a quantitative containment efficiency. Because the detection limit was not recorded and replicate experiments were not performed, further testing is needed to confirm the reliability of the containment system.

Growth in the reactor

Bacillus subtilis grows to high density in the HFM bioreactor under inline OD600 monitoring. The integrated system sustained B. subtilis for 116 h in a single continuous run, with the inline photometer resolving the full growth curve from inoculation to a final OD600 of approximately 3.7 (Figure 59).

The first integrated cycle was run at 37 °C in 500 mL of medium, using B. subtilis 168 carrying pSTK-EXP1-Pveg-Csn:Acds, a vector that constitutively expresses ACCD, with erythromycin at 5 µg/mL. Growth was continuous rather than batch-limited: OD600 rose steadily to a first plateau at approximately 40 h, the point at which ACCD accumulation was greatest. A gap of roughly 10 h appears early in the record because the host computer running the microcontroller applied an update and shut down; since the data were held only in the web UI, those readings were lost. The gap is shaded in Figure 59 and does not reflect a culture event.

Growth has two phases. After the plateau at 40 h, a second rise began at approximately 47 h and continued until the run was terminated at 116 h. We attribute this to loss of selection: erythromycin degrades over a run of this length, after which cells that have shed the plasmid carry a lower metabolic burden and resume growth. The fourth cycle supports this interpretation, as the antibiotic was resupplied and the second exponential phase did not appear.

Strain B. subtilis 168 secreted very little ACCD despite robust growth, consistent with its production of wild-type extracellular proteases that degrade the product. Subsequent cycles therefore used B. subtilis WB800, which is deficient in eight secretory proteases, carrying the codon-optimized construct pSTK-EXP1-Pveg-EEBD-RBS0-SamyQ:AcdS 2.1-Histag-B0015 (Figure 60), again with 5 µg/mL erythromycin.

Reliable inline optical density required two mechanical corrections. In the second cycle the OD600 trace was unstable throughout the run, and the cycle was terminated early (Figure 61). We attributed this to the short tubing connecting the inline photometer, which transmitted pump vibration directly to the optical head and displaced the optical axis. In the third cycle, the tubing separating the photometer from both the HFM module and the flow meter was extended to 30 cm, and the medium volume was reduced from 1 L to 500 mL to raise the surface-area-to-biomass ratio for oxygen diffusion. The OD600 and pH records from that cycle were stable and internally consistent up to the point of mechanical failure at 38 h (Figure 62), establishing the inline photometer as a usable continuous growth sensor.

A second failure mode of the optical reading emerged from the leakage events. When medium was lost and the pump ran partly dry, OD600 climbed rather than fell, because too little liquid passed through the flow cuvette to sweep settled cells out of the optical path (Figure 65). A rising OD600 without a corresponding pattern in flow and pressure should therefore be read as a possible hydraulic fault rather than as growth.

pH and antibiotic resupply

pH regulation and antibiotic resupply are both required for sustained, reproducible culture. Growth slowdown in the HFM bioreactor coincided with the fall of lumen pH to 6.5, and restoring pH together with antibiotic eliminated both the slowdown and the spurious second exponential phase, yielding a classical growth curve over 80 h (Figure 63).

The direction of the pH drift was initially unclear. In the second cycle, pH rose over the run, against expectation for continuous culture; few bubbles were visible in the reservoir at the time, so insufficient oxygen supply was suspected and the working volume was reduced from 1 L to 500 mL for the next cycle. With all sensors functioning in the third cycle, pH instead fell monotonically from about 6.9 to 6.4 (Figure 62). Superimposing the growth and pH profiles showed that the inflection in the OD600 curve occurred at the point where pH crossed 6.5. Since Bacillus subtilis growth and the structural stability of the protectant enzyme are both impaired below pH 6.5, and pH above 7.5 is also unfavorable, lumen pH needs to be within this window rather than just monitored passively. Because the crossing can occur outside working hours, automated correction was made a design requirement for the following cycle.

Automated pH control was implemented in the fourth cycle as a feedback loop on the feeding valve. A peristaltic pump with a maximum flow rate of approximately 60 mL/min was installed upstream of the feeding Luer valve. When lumen pH fell to 6.8, the controller dosed 5 mL of 1 M KOH per minute until pH returned to 7.0. The loop operated as designed for the first night of the run, after which 1 M KOH was found to be corroding the feeding valve. Automated dosing was therefore withdrawn, and pH was corrected manually once per day by adding 20 mL KOH mixed with 25 mL TB medium and supplemented with 100 µL of 25 mg/mL erythromycin.

The resulting profile is the most complete growth record obtained from the system (Figure 63). Exponential growth occupied 2 to 9 h, after which OD600 rose slowly and monotonically to approximately 3.3 by 80 h. pH traced a sawtooth between roughly 6.4 and 7.9, each sharp rise corresponding to a base addition and each decay to subsequent acidification by the culture. Critically, the second exponential phase seen in earlier cycles did not reappear. Because the daily feed supplied erythromycin alongside base, this supports the interpretation that the earlier second rise reflected loss of the selection marker rather than a genuine metabolic transition, and it establishes antibiotic resupply, not only pH correction, as a requirement for long-term operation.

The corrosion finding constrains the chemistry of the feed line. Two candidates could have attacked the feeding valve: 35% hydrogen peroxide from the CIP procedure and 1 M KOH from pH adjustment. Both circulate through the same fitting, so the cycles reported here cannot tell them apart. Before we can bring automated dosing back, we need either a milder base or a valve body that resists it.

Pressure and sampling

Valve-controlled transmembrane pressure drives permeate across the membrane and enables timed shell-side sampling. Coordinated actuation of the shell valve and the pinch valve raised mean transmembrane pressure (TMP) from approximately 0.03 bar to 0.16 bar for 60 s, yielding 20 to 30 mL of shell permeate per sampling event without interrupting the culture (Figure 64).

Under normal operation the feed (lumen inlet), retentate (lumen outlet) and permeate (shell) pressures sat at approximately 0.16, 0.025 and 0.065 bar g respectively, giving a mean TMP near 0.03 bar. The peristaltic pump imposed a periodic pulsation on TMP whose troughs reached negative values (Figure 64B). This briefly pulled fluid from the shell side back into the lumen, then pushed it out again. The back-and-forth flow kept the shell space from sitting as dead volume.

Sampling followed a set sequence. We shortened the sensor logging interval from 300 s to 5 s to catch the full pressure transient. Opening the shell-side valve to the collection bottle vented the shell space to atmosphere and dropped the permeate reading to near 0 bar. The pinch valve then closed off the tubing between the HFM outlet and the reservoir inlet for 60 s. Feed pressure rose to about 0.245 bar and retentate pressure to about 0.10 bar, which brought mean TMP to about 0.16 bar. Lumen flow dropped only slightly during occlusion, from about 305 to 285 mL/min (Figure 64C). We then released the pinch valve and closed the shell valve, and all three pressures returned to their pre-sampling values. The pressure record shows directly that the pinch valve supplies the driving force for filtration, not the pump's residual pressure gradient.

Pressure monitoring alone was insufficient to detect loss of containment. Leakage occurred at the feeding Luer valve, located at the serum bottle inlet, the lowest-pressure point in the loop. Contrary to expectation, lumen pressure rose rather than fell when the valve failed: feed pressure climbed from approximately 0.19 to 0.37 bar, while TMP showed no significant change (Figure 65). Pressure signatures are therefore diagnostic of this fault only in combination with other channels, and a dedicated leak-detection device is required for unattended operation.

Product in the permeate

Secreted ACC deaminase accumulates in the shell permeate and becomes catalytically active after 18 h. Shell permeate contained secreted ACCD above 2 µg/mL throughout a 72 h run, and the enzyme was catalytically active from 18 h, with specific activity rising to 6 to 8 U/mg and remaining at that level through the end of the run (Figure 67).

Product distribution across the membrane was established first. In the second integrated cycle, ELISA gave 4 to 8 µg/mL of ACCD in both lumen medium and shell permeate, and the DNPH assay detected activity on both sides, with lumen and shell values closely matched (Figure 66). Because the two compartments were comparable, lumen sampling was discontinued in later cycles.

In the fourth cycle, shell permeate was collected at 1, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36, 48, and 72 h and stored at −20 °C until assay. ELISA showed ACCD above 2 µg/mL at every time point, peaking at about 6.5 µg/mL at 12 h and settling to 2.5 to 3 µg/mL after 36 h (Figure 67A). The 1 h signal is most likely carry-over from the overnight primary culture used as inoculum rather than protein produced in the reactor.

Our data shows that activity lagged behind protein accumulation. The DNPH assay we conducted measures α-ketobutyrate (α-KB) released from ACC; it gave only marginal activity at 6 and 12 h, rose sharply at 18 h, and remained stable thereafter (Figure 67B). One unit (U) is the amount of enzyme releasing 1 µmol α-KB per hour under the assay conditions used here (30 °C, 12 h incubation), and 1 mU = 0.001 U. Normalizing to ELISA concentration gives specific activity below 1 U/mg at 6 to 12 h, rising to 6.3 U/mg at 18 h and holding between 5 and 8.4 U/mg through 72 h (Figure 67C). Therefore, immunoreactive protein appears before functional enzyme.

We think the lag comes from post-secretory maturation. ACCD only works as a multimer and needs pyridoxal 5′-phosphate as a cofactor. Neither oligomer assembly nor cofactor loading is likely to happen right away once the protein crosses into the extracellular space. Specific activity then plateaus, which suggests maturation keeps up with secretion for the rest of the run.

These results indicate that the system performs as designed, specifically that protein crosses the membrane into a cell-free compartment, remains active there, and can be harvested repeatedly over at least 72 h without terminating the culture.
