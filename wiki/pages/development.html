Development

Abstract

What does success look like for ReLEAF? Development presents the journeys of the various components of our project in parallel–where we started, and where we are now.

We define ReLEAF’s success as one connected chain: Detect, Forecast & Activate; Produce; Contain; Monitor; and Protect. Each stage has its own success criteria and evidence, allowing us to see clearly what has been demonstrated and what still needs validation. Alongside these metrics, our prototypes document the decisions, failures, and redesign that shaped the system—from early hydroponics setups to bioreactor and hardware iterations. Together, they show how ReLEAF progressed and where development continues next.

Success definition

What does success for the entire system look like?

ReLEAF is designed to protect plants on demand by producing protectant directly where it is needed. Rather than relying on a single component, ReLEAF connects multiple biological, hardware, and computational systems into one continuous process.

This led us to define ReLEAF’s success as a complete chain with five components:

  1. 1. Detect, Forecast & Activate

    Identify when the plant is under stress, forecast stress that is coming, and convert that into the light response

    Our starting point

    A basic sensor setup and a bare LED holder, with no quantitative threshold

    AIoT Sensor System + LPA

  2. 2. Produce

    Produce the intended type and amount of protectant using engineered Bacillus subtilis

    Our starting point

    A circuit design on paper, untested

    Genetic circuit

  3. 3. Contain

    Keep the engineered bacteria inside the bioreactor and out of the output stream

    Our starting point

    An initial reactor design, untested

    Hollow-fiber membrane + bioreactor

  4. 4. Monitor

    Track the conditions the culture is held at while a run is in progress

    Our starting point

    Hand sampling, read offline on a cuvette spectrophotometer

    Photometer + reactor sensors

  5. 5. Protect

    Produce a measurable improvement in plant performance under stress

    Our starting point

    A stress response observed by eye, with no measured rescue

    Agar + hydroponics + soil

Each stage began with a defined goal and measurable success criteria, allowing us to evaluate our progress and determine what we have achieved.

Journey toward success

LPABioreactor and hardware
The LPA page→
Start

Two LEDs per column were connected directly to a breadboard, with green light intended to activate protein production and red light to suppress it. While this setup demonstrated the basic switching concept, it had significant limitations: individual LEDs produced inconsistent light intensities, and the cultures remained exposed to ambient room light.

Turning points

A 4 × 6 LED array prototype supported six light conditions with four replicates each. After measuring the output of all 40 LEDs, we found that grouping them by brightness could not reliably produce distinct intensity levels. We therefore implemented pulse-width modulation (PWM) to control light intensity through software and added an enclosure to block ambient light. However, during an overnight shaker test, a support post broke, allowing light to leak between neighboring wells. To address these issues, we secured the bottom plate with four screws, reinforced the solder joints, and redesigned the black tube holder with thicker walls to improve stability and prevent light leakage.

Current prototype

The final LPA supports six fixed light conditions, each with four replicate cultures, and has been successfully used in a full culture experiment. Bacillus subtilis WB800 carrying the CcaS/CcaR circuit was exposed to green and red light at 100% intensity, with anti-His ELISA measurements showing protein concentrations of 12.9 and 12.5 µg/mL respectively at 13 h. However, light intensity was measured in lux, a unit based on human visual sensitivity that does not allow direct comparison of photon exposure between green and red light. Future improvements will therefore focus on measuring photon flux and verifying the actual wavelengths of the LEDs to better characterize the illumination conditions.

HydroponicsPlant system
The Plants page→
Start

Prototype 1 was designed based on Tocquin et al. (2003), using an opaque black box, a floating Styrofoam board, black tape, and microcentrifuge tube lids to support the seedlings. However, contamination quickly became a major challenge, prompting further design improvements. Prototype 2 used a commercially available container and successfully germinated three seedlings, while Prototype 3 introduced a transparent container and supported the germination of one seedling. These early trials helped us identify limitations in the growing environment and guided the development of subsequent prototypes.

Turning points

By the fifth prototype, we had replaced the hand-cut Styrofoam board with a 3D-printed board featuring uniformly spaced holes, increased the frequency of medium replacement, and introduced aeration to improve oxygen availability around the roots. These changes helped stabilize plant growth, allowing us to shift our focus from maintaining plant survival to conducting experiments. Before testing our engineered protectants, however, we first needed to determine whether the B. subtilis TB medium itself would affect plant health. We tested six dilutions of the medium and found that all concentrations, including the lowest tested dilution of 1/16, caused visible plant stress, with anthocyanin accumulation observed even in the least concentrated treatment groups. These findings highlighted the need to address the effects of the medium before evaluating the protectants themselves.

Current prototype

With a more stable hydroponic system, we were able to conduct experiments comparing different treatment timings under salt stress. Under 100 mM NaCl, plants receiving CH peptide during salt exposure (co-treatment) achieved the highest fresh weight of 0.2029 g, compared with 0.0862 g in the control group. Most of their leaves also remained green after seven days of salt stress, suggesting improved tolerance under these conditions. Beyond our own experiments, the redesigned hydroponic system was adopted by Professor Cheng, who first introduced us to hydroponic cultivation in March, for use by her graduate students.

AgarPlant system
The Plants page→
Start

We used Arabidopsis thaliana grown on agar as the most controlled of our three experimental platforms. After two days of stratification, four-day-old seedlings were transferred onto stress plates. Early trials faced inconsistent germination and frequent contamination, which we addressed through seed sterilization and performing transfers inside a biosafety cabinet.

Turning points

Counting seedling growth alone was not sensitive enough, so we switched to vertically positioned agar plates and used primary root length as our main measurement. Testing 75, 100, and 150 mM NaCl showed that mean root length decreased from 74.95 mm without added salt to 34.77, 11.00, and 1.36 mm, respectively, establishing 75–100 mM as a suitable stress range. We also improved our treatment method, replacing pipetting and spraying, which displaced seedlings and left droplets on leaves, with transferring seedlings directly onto treated plates. This allowed us to control treatment concentrations through the agar medium.

Current prototype

Our agar-based screening system now includes a calibrated salt stress range, an effective treatment concentration, and optimized treatment timing for each stress condition. Under 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM trehalose increased chlorophyll content 8.7-fold and primary root length by approximately 3 cm, with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.0001). Two additional experimental sets, BoPEP4 and AtLEA14, have been planted, photographed, and analyzed, but their results are still being documented, so no conclusions are drawn from them here.

SoilPlant system
The Plants page→
Start

We grew Arabidopsis thaliana in soil trays alongside our agar and hydroponic experiments to better represent the conditions ReLEAF would encounter in the field. However, inconsistent watering and mold growth made the results from the first four trays difficult to interpret. Some earlier plants had also begun flowering, making them unsuitable for experiments focused on vegetative growth.

Turning points

Because the amount of salt added to soil does not directly reflect the stress experienced by plants, we treated soil cultivation as a separate stress model rather than applying the same concentrations used in agar experiments. On September 6, we compared two published methods for inducing soil salinity. The top-down washing method caused visible growth inhibition, reducing fresh weight to 0.15 g, approximately 64% below the control. In contrast, sub-irrigation produced little noticeable stress, likely because watering between salt treatments flushed ions away from the root zone. Based on these results, we selected the top-down washing method for subsequent trials.

Current prototype

We tested ACC deaminase at three treatment timings under salt stress reaching 300 mM NaCl on September 12, followed by heat stress at 37 °C on September 22, with five days of stress and five days of recovery. Post-treatment plants retained the most green rosettes, although this observation remains preliminary because the pots have not yet been formally scored.

BioreactorBioreactor and hardware
The bioreactor page→
Start

A hollow-fiber membrane cartridge keeps bacterial cells contained in the lumen while allowing smaller molecules to pass into a separate collection stream. Our first cycle tested whether the culture could be continuously perfused while maintaining containment, but encountered two problems: a pump-head leak ended the experiment early, and inconsistent OD600 readings between the tubing and reservoir indicated insufficient mixing.

Turning points

The second cycle introduced tighter connections, magnetic stirring, and a sampling point at the reservoir outlet, allowing us to complete a full growth curve without leakage. Plating shell-side medium on LB agar showed no bacterial growth, providing initial evidence that the membrane successfully contained the cells. We then added flow and pressure sensors, followed by an in-line photometer and pH probe. However, dissolved oxygen measurements remained unsuccessful because the probe could not be calibrated.

Current prototype

We completed six bioreactor runs, with the longest achieving 102.4 hours of uninterrupted monitoring. The system now supports continuous pumping, stirring, and monitoring of OD600, pH, flow, and pressure. Across two October runs, anti-His ELISA measured ACC deaminase concentrations of 2.5–8.5 µg/mL, with one run sampling both sides of the membrane for 40 hours and the other sampling the shell side for 72 hours. Both runs showed peak concentrations at 12–16 hours, followed by a decline, indicating that protein levels did not increase continuously. Improving production and maintaining higher concentrations remain our next priorities.

PhotometerBioreactor and hardware
The photometer page→
Start

Measuring OD600 by manually collecting samples interrupts continuous culture, so we developed a photometer to take readings directly from the flowing loop. Our first prototype used a horizontal optical path mounted on an adjustable rail, but bubbles accumulated in the flow cuvette and interfered with the light beam. The adjustable components also shifted over time, causing misalignment and reducing measurement reliability.

Turning points

Switching to a vertical optical path allowed bubbles to rise away from the light beam. Tilting the channel by 10° cleared nearly all bubbles within 0.25 seconds and reduced the difference from BioDrop reference measurements from approximately 20% to 8%. By the fourth iteration, we had fixed all components in place and enclosed the optical path to improve stability. We also introduced a ratiometric design, using a beamsplitter to direct part of the light to a reference sensor, reducing the influence of LED fluctuations on measurements.

Current prototype

The photometer now operates directly within the flow loop, with a live dashboard, a median absolute deviation (MAD) filter, and bubble detection that identified 97% of disturbed readings without rejecting clean measurements. It has recorded over 400 hours of continuous data without manual sampling and detected a recirculation pump failure that scheduled checks might have missed. Compared with the reference instrument, measurements remained nearly identical up to OD600 2.5, with a slope of 0.995. Although a fiber-optic prototype was two-thirds smaller, it underestimated readings by approximately 5.6-fold, making the larger photometer our preferred design for the bioreactor.

ACCD productionGenetic circuit and production
The Results page→
Start

We divided protein production into two levels to validate the system step by step: Level 1 focused on constitutive ACC deaminase production under the Pveg promoter, while Level 2 introduced green-light regulation through the CcaS/CcaR two-component system. Both constructs were verified through PCR and sequencing before being transformed into B. subtilis. However, successful assembly and transformation alone do not confirm protein production.

Turning points

Our initial Western blot did not detect ACCD in the tested samples, prompting us to improve protein expression and recovery. A later blot confirmed the presence of a His-tagged protein band, allowing us to shift our focus from detection to quantification. We used anti-His ELISA with a fitted standard curve to estimate protein concentrations and the DNPH assay to measure α-ketobutyrate produced by ACCD activity. The Berthelot assay also required an ACC-adjusted calibration to reliably measure ammonium in our culture medium.

Current prototype

Level 1 demonstrated constitutive ACC deaminase production and activity across two October bioreactor runs. Anti-His ELISA measured 2.5–8.5 µg/mL, peaking at 16 h in the lumen (8.5 µg/mL) and shell (8.0 µg/mL) in one run. In the second, shell-side concentrations peaked at 6.5 µg/mL at 12 h before declining to 2.6 µg/mL by 72 h. DNPH activity peaked later, reaching 30.9 nmol α-ketobutyrate mL⁻¹ h⁻¹ at 18 h. Level 2 was assembled and verified in three clones, with ELISA measuring 12.9 versus 11.8 µg/mL under green and red light at 13 h. In a separate light-switching experiment, protein concentrations remained at 2.6–3.2 µg/mL, compared with 1.3 µg/mL in the plasmid-free control, while activity rose from 2.8 to 18.1 nmol α-ketobutyrate mL⁻¹ h⁻¹ after eight hours of green light. However, overlapping measurements and single-well limitations mean reliable light-dependent regulation remains unconfirmed.

In March, our focus was on deciding what to test. We explored several stress conditions, Medicago, hydroponics, and different methods of evaluating plant growth. Dr. Lin encouraged us to consider real-world applications and carefully select our target species, while Prof. Cheng suggested a more controlled approach: using young Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings grown on agar, which became the foundation of our plant experiments.

Turning points

Dr. Verslues encouraged us to shift from applying protectants after stress to using bacterial products to strengthen plants' own stress responses earlier. Prof. Chen introduced the idea of treating the hardware as a printer and the biofertilizer as its ink, while Dr. Brophy emphasized physical containment over a genetic kill switch. Together, these insights shaped our membrane-based bioreactor design. Dr. Sattely also stressed the importance of validating protectant effectiveness before integrating it into the system.

Current prototype

Our recent consultations focused on improving the scientific rigor of our experiments. Prof. Huang emphasized that protein concentration alone is insufficient without confirming identity, solubility, and activity, while Prof. Cheng highlighted the need to standardize experimental conditions to avoid introducing unintended stress. During our September meeting, Dr. Sattely and Prof. Endy helped establish our next priorities: distinguishing the effects of engineered bacteria from those of the protectants, verifying protein production and secretion, strengthening experimental controls, and clearly defining what each result demonstrates.
