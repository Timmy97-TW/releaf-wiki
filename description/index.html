Abstract

Climate stress costs Taiwanese farms yield before the damage shows, and the biological protectants that could answer it are still hard to make, store and deliver cheaply.

ReLEAF moves that production to the farm. A leaf sensor and the weather forecast feed a stress index fitted to 25 years of county harvests, and the index sets the brightness of a green lamp over engineered B. subtilis WB800. Green light switches on the CcaSR system and the cells secrete ACC deaminase inside a hollow-fiber bioreactor, which lets the protein through and keeps the bacteria behind. This page is the whole system in one place: where it matters, what each block does, and how far each one has actually been built.

On this page

The challenge

Climate stress

Rising temperatures, soil salinization, drought and disease all cut crop productivity. To see where in Taiwan they bite hardest, we mapped environmental and agricultural data across the island.

Warming, the size of a farm and the age of the person farming it all vary widely from region to region. A farmer does not experience a national average; they experience the conditions of their own field. Taiwan is exposed on top of that: more than 23 million people depend on around 769,000 hectares of cultivable land, and typhoons, drought and other climate-related disasters keep threatening what it produces.

Climate stress is local, so the answer to it has to work locally.

Gaps in biological solutions

Biofertilizers, biostimulants and bioprotectants are alternatives to conventional agrochemicals, but they are hard to deliver cheaply and reliably. They are made at central facilities, purified and formulated for stability, shipped to farms and kept in storage. Every step adds time and cost, and the farmer is still left to judge when to apply what arrives.

Where it matters

A large commercial farm can usually cover the money, the equipment and the labor this takes. A small-scale farmer has less access to monitoring, to storage and to precise application. So the farms most exposed to local climate stress are also the ones furthest from biological protection.

Taiwan’s holdings are fragmented, its technology is expensive and its workforce is aging: by 2021 the average agricultural manager was 63.8 years old. Precision agriculture is hard for a small farm to take up under those conditions, and a new system has to be simple enough to run without help.

For a small-scale farmer the question is not whether better technology exists. It is whether it is affordable and easy enough to use in an ordinary working day.

Project inspiration

Taiwan’s markets connect the food people buy with the farmers who grow it. Behind the stalls, though, lettuce leaves are getting smaller, rice yields are falling and harvests are harder to protect. Growing conditions are becoming more extreme, and farmers are left defending crops against stresses they cannot see coming.

That is where ReLEAF began, with one question: how can we give farmers a way to answer plant stress before the damage is permanent?

Our first design released engineered bacteria into the soil to sense stress and make protectants where the stress was. A DesK–DesR system would read temperature and switch on a stress-response pathway. Professors and bioprocess experts told us two things were wrong with it: the bacteria could not be contained, and biological sensing was neither precise nor controllable enough. So we moved the sensing out of the cell, into electronic sensors and an AIoT (artificial intelligence of things) model that predicts plant stress, and built a hollow-fiber perfusion bioreactor that holds the bacteria in while letting the protectants out.

Our solution

At the center of ReLEAF is a compact bioreactor built to make protectants on the farm that uses them.

First, a leaf sensor continuously monitors local crop conditions, including temperature and moisture. Our AIoT model combines these real-time measurements with weather forecasts and historical trends to predict plant stress and activate a green-light signal when conditions approach a damaging threshold. The data are displayed through an app interface, where farmers can view field conditions, receive stress alerts, and get maintenance reminders.

Then, green light activates the CcaSR optogenetic system in engineered Bacillus subtilis WB800, triggering the production of ACC deaminase. LEA14 and BoPEP4 were also constructed as parts for future integration into the same optogenetic system. Each one is aimed at a different mechanism: ACC deaminase at the ethylene a stressed plant makes, LEA14 at the damage drought and salt do to proteins, BoPEP4 at the plant’s own defenses. So far, our plant testing has focused on heat and salinity stress.

The complete unit measures about 40 × 24 × 62 cm, small enough to stand beside the field it supplies. Inside it, the membrane lets the protectants through to the delivery pathway and keeps the bacterial cells behind. The hollow-fiber module is a sealed, replaceable cartridge, like an ink cartridge, so it can be swapped without anyone handling the culture.

The protectants leave the machine in the permeate. In our plant trials so far, that permeate was collected and applied to the plants by hand; the bioreactor is not plumbed into a crop’s water supply.

Delivery through hydroponic circulation or soil irrigation is part of the design, but it has not been built or tested; connecting the bioreactor directly to growing crops remains a future step.

What leaves the system is the protein, not the cells that made it. ReLEAF can therefore protect a field where and when stress is predicted without releasing engineered bacteria into it.

Integrated Human Practice

Our project was continuously shaped by input from farmers, researchers, industry experts, and community members.

We visited local farms to understand how farmers recognize plant stress, manage their crops, and make decisions about intervention. Their feedback emphasized the need for a system that is automated, accessible, and compatible with existing farming practices.

We visited a hydroponic farm and consulted a plant-protectant company to better understand existing delivery systems and the barriers biological products face outside the laboratory. Their input helped us refine how ReLEAF could connect to hydroponic circulation, irrigation systems, and real agricultural workflows.

We then hosted a public forum that brought together farmers, company representatives, and community members to exchange perspectives and examine our bioreactor. These interactions helped us refine ReLEAF’s accessibility, safety, and real-world application.

Between them these conversations set our agenda. Farmers pressed us on everyday operation and on cost; researchers and industry experts pressed us on biosafety validation, cartridge maintenance, regulation and scale-up.

To learn more about how stakeholder feedback shaped the development of ReLEAF, visit our Integrated Human Practices page.

Our vision & future impact

Our vision

We want a farm-scale biomanufacturing unit to be something any farmer can get hold of. Making plant-protective products on site and on demand would cut out the central factory, the long-distance shipping and the cold storage in between. What we are really after is a shorter path from a result in a laboratory to a treatment in a field.

Future impact

The design is modular, so the same unit could make other protein-based treatments and answer stresses we have not tested. The work in front of us is to measure protectant efficacy across crops and growth stages, to test cartridge integrity as a biosafety check, and to make the cartridge easier to maintain and replace.

After that it becomes a question of who runs the machine. We want to work with farmer collectives, research farms and agricultural organizations on how units would be distributed, serviced and shared, and equipment rental schemes or government subsidies could bring the cost of adoption down. Field data, continued stakeholder feedback and the regulatory route would then take ReLEAF to pilot-farm trials and beyond.

The point of all of it is to put precise biological protection within reach of the small-scale farmers existing agricultural technology tends to pass over.
