Abstract

Heat and salinity threaten crop productivity, but biological protectants remain hard to manufacture, store and deliver affordably.

The farmers most exposed to localized climate stress also face the greatest barriers to biological protection. Our first design released engineered bacteria into the soil to sense plant stress and make protectants where the stress was. Professors and bioprocess experts told us the engineered bacteria could not be contained that way, and their feedback moved the sensing out of the cells: electronic sensors and an AIoT model now predict plant stress. The bacteria make plant-protective molecules inside a hollow-fibre bioreactor, which releases the protectants and holds the cells back.

On this page

The challenge

Climate change is pressing on agriculture: rising temperatures, soil salinization, drought and disease. A farmer does not meet a national average, though. They meet the conditions of their own field, so a response has to work field by field.

Climate stress is local

We mapped environmental and agricultural data across Taiwan to find out how much the pressure varies from one county to the next.

  • Temperature, salinity and climate volatility differ considerably between regions, and a field sits in one of them.
  • More than 23 million people depend on roughly 1.9 million acres of cultivable land, which leaves Taiwan with little margin.
  • Typhoons, drought and other climate-related disasters keep threatening production.

Gaps in biological solutions

Biofertilizers, biostimulants and bioprotectants are alternatives to conventional agrochemicals. Delivering them affordably and reliably is the hard part, because four steps stand between a working molecule and a stressed plant.

  • Manufacture. Production sits at centralized facilities, away from the farm.
  • Formulation. The product is purified and formulated for stability.
  • Transport. It travels to the farm.
  • Storage. It waits under conditions the farm has to provide.

The cost of the chain

Each step adds time and cost, and at the end of it the farmer still has to judge when the protectant should be applied.

Where it matters

Large commercial farms have the finance, the specialized equipment and the labour to carry that chain. Small-scale farms have less access to monitoring, to storage and to precise application.

  • The farmers most exposed to localized climate stress face the greatest barriers to biological protection.
  • Taiwan’s sector also carries smallholder fragmentation, high technology costs and an aging workforce, all of which make precision agriculture harder to adopt on a small farm.
  • By 2021 the average age of Taiwan’s agricultural managers had reached 63.8 years, so a new system has to be simple to operate.

The question we took on

For a small-scale farmer the question is not whether better technology exists, but whether it is affordable, accessible and easy enough to use in everyday farming.

Project inspiration

Taiwan’s markets connect the food people buy with the farmers who grow it. Behind the stalls, something is going wrong: lettuce leaves are getting smaller, rice yields are falling, harvests are harder to protect. ReLEAF began with one question. How can a farmer answer plant stress before the damage is irreversible?

The pivot

Our first design put the sensing in the cells. A DesK–DesR system would read a temperature change, open a stress-response pathway, and let engineered bacteria make protectants in the soil where the stress was. Consultations with professors and bioprocess experts closed that route: the bacteria could not be contained in open soil, and biological sensing gave us too little precision and control. Their feedback moved the sensing into hardware and the bacteria into a vessel.

Our solution

Four steps run from a reading in the field to a protected plant: detect, activate, contain, deliver.

Detect

Electronic sensors watch temperature and salinity continuously. Our AIoT model reads those data, predicts plant stress, and turns on a green-light signal once conditions cross a damaging threshold.

Activate

Green light activates the CcaSR optogenetic system in engineered Bacillus subtilis, which then produces ACC deaminase, LEA14 or BoPep4. Each protectant works on a different mechanism of heat, salinity or biotic stress.

Contain

The engineered bacteria stay inside a hollow-fibre membrane bioreactor. The membrane passes the protectants into the delivery pathway and holds the cells back.

Deliver

Hydroponic circulation or soil irrigation carries the released protectants to the plants.

What the four steps add up to

Protection arrives locally, at the time stress is predicted, and no engineered bacteria leave for the environment.

Integrated Human Practice

Farmers, researchers, industry experts and community members shaped the project throughout. Three kinds of visit changed it most.

Farm visits

We went to local farms to learn how farmers recognize plant stress, manage their crops and decide when to step in. They asked for a system that is automated, accessible and compatible with the way they already work.

Hydroponics and industry

A hydroponic farm and a plant-protectant company showed us the delivery systems that exist and the barriers biological products meet outside the laboratory. Their input set how ReLEAF connects to hydroponic circulation and irrigation.

The public forum

We hosted a forum where farmers, company representatives and community members examined the bioreactor and traded views on it. What they said sharpened the accessibility, the safety and the field application of the design.

How stakeholder feedback shaped ReLEAF

Our vision and future impact

Our vision

Every farmer with a small on-site biomanufacturing system. Protectants generated locally and on demand would cut the dependence on centralized manufacturing, long-distance transport and specialized storage, and shorten the path from a new biological discovery to the field: the protection a crop needs, where and when it needs it.

Future impact

The design is modular. The same loop can carry other protein-based treatments and answer a wider range of agricultural stresses, which makes precise biological protection safer, faster to respond and easier to reach for the small-scale farms that existing agricultural technology underserves.

The next step

Leave the laboratory. We want to pilot the system with small-scale farmers and farm cooperatives and collect harder data on reliability, usability, plant response and operating cost. Those field results would tell us what to change before anything wider.

Further out

With field data and continued stakeholder feedback, we want to work with farmer collectives, research farms and agricultural organizations on practical models for distribution, maintenance and shared use, and to move toward commercialization without losing the farms the project was built for.
