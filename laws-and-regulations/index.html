Laws and Regulations

Summary

ReLEAF’s regulatory strategy separates the bioreactor, engineered Bacillus subtilis culture, and cell-free agricultural output into distinct regulatory assessments.

ReLEAF’s primary commercialization strategy investigates plant-biostimulant pathways for abiotic-stress resilience. Separately, after Jamboree, we plan to investigate whether BoPep4 can control gray mold (Botrytis cinerea) and what pesticide/fungicide classification would apply in Taiwan. These remain distinct regulatory pathways, and neither potential pathway constitutes approval. Taiwan will serve as our development market, followed by the Philippines and Thailand, while the EU remains a longer-term opportunity. Before commercialization, we must validate containment, test for viable production organisms and residual recombinant DNA, demonstrate product safety and efficacy, and consult relevant authorities.

Abstract

ReLEAF's regulatory review identifies three separate questions: how the agricultural output is classified, how the engineered production culture is contained, and which requirements apply to the bioreactor. The most important finding is that a potential regulatory pathway is not an approval. A fertilizer or plant-biostimulant route is being investigated for ReLEAF's cell-free output, while the classification of the engineered Bacillus subtilis culture and the final equipment requirements remain separate questions. For post-Jamboree development, BoPep4 will also be investigated as a potential gray-mold-control product in Taiwan. This use may trigger a microbial fertilizer/fungicide pathway distinct from the fertilizer or plant-biostimulant route.

Our analysis covers Taiwan, the European Union, the United States, and potential Southeast Asian markets. Taiwan is our development and validation environment. Our current commercialization plan prioritizes the Philippines as the first international expansion market, with the European Union considered for a later stage.

The review draws on primary legal sources and an industry briefing from Cheng Han Biotechnology (正瀚生技). It also identifies unresolved questions concerning product composition, residual cells and DNA, efficacy evidence, and applicable contained-use requirements. These gaps are treated as development tasks, not as evidence of regulatory compliance. Regulation is therefore not the final checkpoint of ReLEAF – it is one of the constraints that shape what we build, what we claim, and where we sell it.

Regulation as Product Design

ReLEAF is a three-part system: the bioreactor, the engineered production culture, and the cell-free agricultural output. We examined these components across Taiwan, the European Union, the United States, and potential Southeast Asian markets. The same biological output may be treated differently depending on its composition, intended function, claims, and jurisdiction. We therefore treated regulation as part of product design rather than a final approval step.

Organic Farming Compatibility

ReLEAF's organic-farming compatibility depends not only on whether viable engineered Bacillus subtilis cells enter the agricultural environment, but also on whether each country's organic standards permit agricultural inputs manufactured using genetically engineered organisms. Although ReLEAF's bioreactor is designed to retain the production bacteria behind a 0.2 µm hollow-fiber membrane and deliver cell-free protective proteins and peptides, this containment design does not automatically establish organic eligibility. The following assessment follows ReLEAF's planned market-entry sequence.

1. Taiwan: Initial regulatory assessment

Taiwan's Organic Agriculture Promotion Act explicitly defines organic agriculture as production that does not use genetically modified organisms or their products. Article 15 of the Act further emphasizes the restrictions prohibiting substances outside the officially permitted list. Consequently, ReLEAF's use of engineered B. subtilis creates a potential compatibility barrier even if the final formulation contains no viable production cells. Before targeting certified organic farms, ReLEAF should seek clarification from the Ministry of Agriculture and an accredited organic certification body on whether the protective proteins are considered products derived from or produced by a GMO and whether the specific formulation can qualify as an authorized input. Conventional agricultural registration and organic eligibility must be evaluated separately (Agriculture and Food Agency–Ministry of Agriculture, AFA–MOA).

2. Philippines: First international assessment

The Philippines has an established organic agriculture framework under Republic Act No. 10068, as amended by Republic Act No. 11511, with national standards covering organic crop production and agricultural inputs. ReLEAF should consult the Bureau of Agriculture and Fisheries Standards (BAFS) and a recognized organic certification body to determine whether the cell-free formulation is permitted under the applicable Philippine National Standards. The review should distinguish the intended use of the product for abiotic-stress tolerance from microbial biocontrol applications and examine whether the engineered manufacturing process affects eligibility. Until the relevant provisions and input requirements have been confirmed, ReLEAF should classify organic compatibility in the Philippines as unresolved rather than assume that cell-free delivery qualifies for organic use (Bureau of Agriculture and Fisheries Standards, BAFS).

3. Thailand: Regional expansion assessment

Thailand's agricultural organic standard, TAS 9000-2564, covers organic production, processing, labelling and marketing. ReLEAF should assess the relevant crop-production and input requirements with the National Bureau of Agricultural Commodity and Food Standards (NBACFS) and an appropriate certification body. The central question is whether the protective formulation is permitted when its active proteins are produced by engineered bacteria, even if those bacteria are retained in the bioreactor. ReLEAF should also confirm whether the relevant certification scheme applies additional restrictions to GMO-derived agricultural inputs. Organic certification requirements should be distinguished from the separate authorization needed to market the product as a conventional agricultural input (Thai Agricultural Standard, TAS).

4. United States: Organic-method restrictions

The United States Department of Agriculture's National Organic Program (NOP, USDA) prohibits genetic engineering and other excluded methods in organic production. Its regulations also restrict the use of ingredients produced through excluded methods, while crop-production inputs must satisfy the applicable substance requirements. Consequently, ReLEAF faces a substantial potential barrier to use on USDA-certified organic farms: removing viable engineered bacteria from the final formulation would not, by itself, resolve the question of whether the active proteins were produced using a prohibited method. ReLEAF should obtain an assessment from a USDA-accredited organic certifier before considering an organic-use claim. Conventional product authorization would not establish USDA organic eligibility (United States Department of Agriculture, USDA).

5. Vietnam and Indonesia: Parallel assessments

Vietnam and Indonesia should be evaluated as separate regulatory markets, even though ReLEAF plans to investigate them during the same expansion phase.

In Vietnam, ReLEAF should assess the national organic agriculture framework, including Decree No. 109/2018/ND-CP and the applicable technical standards, such as, but not limited to: Decree No. 69/2010/ND-CP, Decree No. 84/2019/ND-CP (amended), Law on Plant Protection and Quarantine (2013), and Law on Product and Goods Quality (or Law No. 05/2007/QH12), with the competent authorities and appropriate certification body. The review should establish whether an agricultural input manufactured using engineered B. subtilis can be used in certified organic crop production when the final formulation is cell-free. The precise treatment of GMO-produced proteins must be confirmed before ReLEAF can classify the product as compatible.

In Indonesia, the assessment should follow the applicable National organic standard, including SNI 6729:2025, which revised the previous organic agriculture standard and will determine whether or not organic farmers can use ReLEAF to relieve their crop stress yet sell their harvest with the “ORGANIK Indonesia” label from the accredited Organic Certification Body (Lembaga Sertifikasi Organik). ReLEAF should confirm the relevant agricultural inputs. Particular attention should be given to whether the manufacturing process itself affects eligibility, rather than relying solely on analytical evidence that viable bacteria are absent from the output. Both markets should remain under regulatory assessment until written clarification is obtained (Government of Vietnam).

6. European Union: Strict restrictions on GMO-derived production

The European Union presents a significant challenge for ReLEAF's organic-market ambitions. Regulation (EU) 2018/848 explicitly excludes GMOs, products produced from GMOs and products produced by GMOs from organic production, subject to the regulation's stated exceptions. Because ReLEAF's protective proteins are manufactured using engineered B. subtilis, the product may fall within these restrictions even if the final formulation contains no viable cells. A demonstration of membrane integrity or the absence of detectable recombinant DNA would support the product's technical characterization but would not independently establish organic eligibility, which ReLEAF has the evidence detailed in Biomanufacturing page’s Regulations and Biosafety. ReLEAF should obtain a legal and certification assessment of the precise production process before considering the EU organic market. Conventional fertilizer or plant-biostimulant authorization would remain a separate requirement (Parliament and Council).

7. Malaysia: Final market assessment

Malaysia's myOrganic certification guidelines explicitly prohibit GMOs and their products in organic farming. This creates a potential compatibility barrier similar to those identified in Taiwan, the United States and the European Union. ReLEAF should consult the Department of Agriculture Malaysia and an appropriate certification body to determine whether its cell-free protective proteins are considered prohibited products of genetic engineering or could qualify under the applicable input requirements. The final assessment should consider the engineered production organism, manufacturing process, final formulation and intended agricultural use. ReLEAF should not advertise the product as suitable for certified organic farms until its eligibility has been confirmed (Department of Agriculture, DOA).

Overall strategy

ReLEAF should initially prioritize conventional agricultural applications while conducting organic-input assessments in parallel with its market expansion. Taiwan and the Philippines provide the first opportunities to obtain jurisdiction-specific guidance; Thailand, Vietnam and Indonesia can be assessed as the Southeast Asian strategy develops. The United States and European Union warrant early legal review because their organic standards explicitly restrict genetic engineering or products produced using GMOs. Malaysia should be assessed before regional commercialization is expanded there.

Across all markets, ReLEAF should maintain documentation of viable-cell retention, membrane integrity, residual genetic material, product composition and manufacturing controls. These data support biosafety and product-quality assessments, but they do not override organic standards that restrict the production method itself. Any claim of organic compatibility should therefore be made only after the relevant authority or certification body confirms that the specific formulation and manufacturing process satisfy the applicable requirements.

Three Components, Three Regulatory Questions

ReLEAF will not be entering the agricultural environment as an undifferentiated product; to our best knowledge, ReLEAF is the first team to create a system that delivers protectants to plants-in-stress on-site with a bioreactor, a strain of bacteria, and some proteins. The project is so nuanced that it’s great as a whole, but commencing it is a bit tangled and abstruse. Therefore, we decided to break down the project into three parts to register for commercialization instead of selling it as a bundle deal.

The hollow-fiber membrane (0.2 µm) is designed to retain the production culture and let the soluble products through. Under Regulation (EU) 2019/1009, PFC 6(B) is the category for non-microbial plant biostimulants, and PFC 6 covers functions including improving plant tolerance to abiotic stress. However, our review has not identified a general numerical residual-DNA limit in the Regulation that can be stated as a PFC 6(B) requirement. ReLEAF should therefore not claim a legal DNA threshold unless a specific provision, applicable delegated act, harmonised standard, conformity-assessment requirement, or competent authority interpretation is identified. Independently of the legal threshold, ReLEAF will validate its cell-free output by testing for viable production organisms and residual recombinant DNA, report the methods and detection limits, and describe the hollow-fiber membrane as “designed to retain cells” until retention has been experimentally validated. In essence, containment is not only an engineering requirement; it determines the exposure pathway that regulators must consider, and separating the engineered production organism from the cell-free output lets regulators assess the two as distinct questions.

EU Reference Point: Abiotic-Stress Biostimulants vs. Plant-Protection Products

The European Union (EU) provides a useful reference point for evaluating ReLEAF's regulatory strategy because it has an established framework for plant biostimulants and harmonized product-safety requirements. Rather than regulating every agricultural technology under a single category, the EU distinguishes products according to their intended function, composition, and method of use. This is particularly relevant to ReLEAF, which combines an engineered bacterial culture, a controlled bioreactor, and plant-protective outputs.

Under Regulation (EU) 2019/1009, plant biostimulants fall under Product Function Category (PFC) 6. These products must stimulate plant nutrition processes independently of their nutrient content, with the aim of improving characteristics such as tolerance to abiotic stress, nutrient-use efficiency, or plant quality. The regulation distinguishes microbial plant biostimulants (PFC 6(A)) from non-microbial plant biostimulants (PFC 6(B)). However, its Component Material Category 7 (CMC 7) restricts eligible microorganisms to specified groups, including Azotobacter, mycorrhizal fungi, Rhizobium, and Azospirillum. Therefore, ReLEAF cannot assume that its engineered Bacillus subtilis culture qualifies as an EU microbial plant biostimulant under this framework. A separately collected, cell-free protectant output may warrant assessment under the non-microbial category, but its eligibility and conformity requirements would depend on its composition, function, and applicable legal provisions (Regulation EU 2019/1009 Annexes I & II).

The bioreactor itself presents a separate regulatory consideration. Machinery placed on the EU market must satisfy the applicable safety and conformity requirements, including the relevant CE-marking obligations. Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 replaces the former Machinery Directive 2006/42/EC and applies from 20 January 2027. ReLEAF would therefore need to assess the requirements applicable to its intended market-entry date, alongside any other legislation relevant to the device's electrical, electromagnetic, or pressure-related components (Regulation EU 2023/1230).

For ReLEAF, the EU framework illustrates an important distinction: the regulatory pathway for the engineered culture may differ from that for the cell-free protectant and the bioreactor. This makes the EU a useful benchmark for identifying product-classification challenges, evidence requirements, and equipment-compliance obligations before examining Taiwan's domestic regulatory system. The following section therefore evaluates Taiwan's applicable laws and regulatory authorities in relation to ReLEAF's proposed commercialization model.

Three Target Markets

Taiwan

Taiwan: Development and Validation

Taiwan is where we build and test ReLEAF. Our lab work, plant trials and farmer meetings are all here, and it is where we would register first. The main regulatory question for the cell-free output is whether its composition, function, and claims fit an existing fertilizer-related category.

Cell-Free Output

Taiwan's fertilizer specifications recognize 植物生長輔助劑類 (Plant Growth Auxiliary Materials / “Biostimulants”) among its fertilizer categories. However, this does not establish that ReLEAF's protein- or peptide-based formulation automatically belongs in that category. Taiwan's Ministry of Agriculture oversees both routes. The Agriculture and Food Agency registers fertilizers under the Fertilizer Management Act, and the Animal and Plant Health Inspection Agency (APHIA) handles pesticides under the Pesticide Management Act. We would confirm our formulation and claims with the Agriculture and Food Agency.

If registered as a fertilizer, ReLEAF would need a defined and reproducible composition, safety and quality data, efficacy data, labeling and registration information. APHIA told us the review itself takes about 30 days once the file is complete.

Separating Abiotic-Stress and Fungicide Claims: Avoiding Pesticide Positioning

ReLEAF is investigating two distinct application pathways. The first focuses on abiotic-stress resilience, including potential applications of BoPep4 for salinity tolerance, AtLEA14 for salinity, drought, and osmotic stress, and ACCD for heat-stress mitigation. These applications may warrant investigation under Taiwan’s fertilizer or plant-growth-related regulatory framework, subject to confirmation of the product category and supporting efficacy data.

The second pathway emerged after the Jamboree: investigating whether BoPep4 can control gray mold (Botrytis cinerea), a fungal plant pathogen. This is a separate research direction and may require assessment under Taiwan’s Agro-pesticides Management Act. We will not assume that the product automatically qualifies as a microbial pesticide or fungicide. Its classification depends on the active substance, final formulation, intended use, and the competent authority’s interpretation.

This distinction matters because ReLEAF’s engineered Bacillus subtilis is intended to remain contained in the bioreactor. The agricultural product under investigation is the cell-free output, not the intentional release of engineered bacteria into the field. We therefore need to clarify whether a cell-free product produced by engineered microorganisms falls within an applicable pesticide category and what evidence would be required.

Until efficacy and classification are confirmed, gray-mold control remains a proposed research application rather than an established product claim or approved use.

Engineered Production Strain

The engineered B. subtilis is assessed separately from the cell-free output. Our current review did not identify a general Taiwan contained-use GMO framework that clearly resolves this specific engineered bacterial production scenario.

We therefore treat the applicable pathway as unresolved. Before commercial deployment, we would seek clarification from the relevant authority concerning manufacture, contained use, storage, transport, and waste handling. We will not describe the system as certified for contained use until the applicable framework and evidence requirements have been confirmed.

Bioreactor

The bioreactor is a piece of equipment rather than the agricultural product itself. Its requirements depend on the final hardware configuration, including electrical systems, pumps, pressure-bearing components, sensors, and fluid-contact materials. The final commercial design would therefore require a separate equipment-safety and conformity assessment.

European Union

European Union: A Market With High-Risk High-Reward

The EU provides a clear framework to investigate ReLEAF's intended function. Under Regulation (EU) 2019/1009, PFC 6 covers plant biostimulants, including non-microbial PFC 6(B). The regulation explicitly includes improvement of tolerance to abiotic stress within the plant-biostimulant function.

ReLEAF’s Potential Pathway

For now, ReLEAF’s experiment data may support applications for microbial fertilizers, biofertilizers, or plant biostimulants where applicable throughout jurisdictions. In the near future, where ReLEAF is given the opportunity to expand its data on proving efficacy across multiple stresses (salinity, drought, osmotic, heat, and B. cinerea), ReLEAF would apply for two different registrations for farmers to choose between: fertilizer or fungicide; the bacteria vials would be sold separately depending on the purchase between our fertilizer product or fungicide product.

The EU framework therefore creates a direct feedback loop between our wetlab work and regulatory strategy. The intended claim determines the evidence we need to generate: salinity-tolerance evidence for BoPep4, drought or osmotic-stress protection evidence for AtLEA14, and heat-stress mitigation evidence for ACCD. These experimental results would support the biological rationale for the corresponding claims, but would not by themselves determine the final regulatory classification.

For ReLEAF’s potential abiotic-stress applications, PFC 6(B) under Regulation (EU) 2019/1009 is a pathway worth investigating because it covers non-microbial plant biostimulants. However, this category does not automatically establish eligibility for ReLEAF’s cell-free output. The product’s composition, function, applicable component-material requirements, conformity assessment, and supporting evidence must be evaluated before determining whether this route is viable.

The proposed BoPep4 application against gray mold (Botrytis cinerea) is a separate regulatory question. A product intended to control a fungal plant pathogen may fall under the EU plant-protection-product framework, including Regulation (EC) No 1107/2009, rather than the plant-biostimulant pathway. Its classification would depend on the product and intended claims.

Accordingly, ReLEAF should assess the abiotic-stress and gray-mold applications separately. We should not assume that a potential PFC 6(B) route for abiotic-stress claims would authorize a fungicidal claim. Any applicable requirements for the active substance, final formulation, safety, efficacy, and market authorization must be confirmed for the relevant route.

Bioreactor and Engineered Culture

The bioreactor and engineered production culture remain separate from the cell-free fertilizing product. Their applicable equipment and contained-biotechnology requirements must be assessed according to the final design and production system.

United States

United States: A Decentralized Pathway

The United States differs from the EU because agricultural-input requirements can involve both federal and state authorities. For ReLEAF, the main questions remain:

  1. How is the cell-free product classified?
  2. Do its claims trigger pesticide/plant-regulator regulations?
  3. Which state-level fertilizer or beneficial-substance requirements apply?
  4. How is the engineered production microorganism regulated?

⇒ ReLEAF therefore cannot transfer an EU classification directly to the United States.

Cell-Free Output

ReLEAF’s potential US pathway must be evaluated according to the identity of the marketed product, the active substance, the intended use, and whether viable engineered microorganisms are present in the product.

For the proposed abiotic-stress applications, the team must first identify the appropriate federal and state regulatory frameworks for the final cell-free formulation and its intended agricultural claims. A fertilizer or plant-biostimulant positioning should not be treated as confirmed until the relevant requirements have been assessed.

For the proposed BoPep4 gray-mold application, the team must separately investigate the US Environmental Protection Agency’s pesticide framework under Federal Insecticide Fungicide Rodenticide Act (FIFRA). The distinction between a microbial pesticide and a biochemical pesticide may be relevant: the fact that a peptide is produced by bacteria does not, by itself, establish that the marketed cell-free product is a microbial pesticide. The applicable category and authorization requirements must be determined from the final formulation and intended use. Toxic Substance Control Act (TSCA) biotechnology requirements and United States Department of Agriculture Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service (USDA-APHIS) biotechnology rules should likewise be assessed according to their applicability to the specific organism, activity, and product. None of these pathways should be presented as a confirmed clearance route before the relevant classification questions have been resolved.

Engineered B. subtilis

For the contained engineered microorganism, the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)’s Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA) biotechnology framework under ‘Title 40 of the Code of Federal Regulations Part 725’ (40 C.F.R. Part 725) is a principal federal framework to investigate. Whether the final ReLEAF strain qualifies for an exemption or requires notification/review must be determined from the final strain and containment conditions.

Equipment

The bioreactor requires a separate equipment-safety assessment based on its final electrical, mechanical, pressure, and installation characteristics.

Rationale by Market

24+ pts

Tier 1

United States

32 pts

The United States of America, the largest base by far of 356 Mha of farmland, an average farm of 187 ha, and GM crops on 71.5 Mha (about 20% of farmland), so both market size and technology acceptance has a score of 5.

Regulatory-wise, the Environmental Protection Agency’s Toxic Substance Control Act biotechnology process names engineered microbes: a Microbial Commercial Activity Notice of 90 days before commercialization and a field-test application of 60 days before trials, with exemptions for some recipient organisms. Biostimulant categories exist at state level (AAPFCO definition; California permits plant biostimulant label claims from 1 January 2025).

As to why it is not our initial market, the United States has an entry ease score of 1 (about 12,650 km from Taipei with no home presence). Additionally, two overlapping regimes (EPA and state) add cost, and there are no federal biostimulant definition (the Plant Biostimulant Act of 2025 is pending), plus a risk the ACCD activity is treated as a plant growth regulator under Federal Insecticide Fungicide Rodenticide Act (FIFRA).

Philippines

25 pts

The Philippines is the only Asian market where engineered organisms are named inside the fertilizer system (Fertilizer [and] Pesticide Authority; FPA Memorandum Circular 2018-20). It passes 4 of the 5 culture checks with 3 of 3 evidence checks for the permeate, including a provisional registration after one season of efficacy data collection.

One of its weaknesses is that the amount of farmland in the Philippines is only 12.7 Mha and the average farm size fell to 0.79 ha in 2022 (88% of farms were under 3 ha in 2012), so both market-potential scores (smallholder and farmland) are low. Entry ease is 4 (1150 km away, but a licensed local importer and an FPA-accredited researcher are needed). On the other hand, its technology-acceptance score is a four because GM maize covers about 0.9 Mha, roughly 7% of the farmland.

18~23 pts

Tier 2

Thailand

22 pts

The Kingdom of Thailand, a long-established fertilizer regime run by the Department of Agriculture, dictates that biological fertilizers are already recognized as a category. It has the best data-supported third market in Southeast Asia by far with 22.4 Mha of farmland and the largest average farm of the Southeast Asian region (3.2 ha, 2003 census), which gives it the top deployment-fit score.

Documenting registration takes about 8 to 12 months of process; additionally, biofertilizer is a defined fertilizer type. However, Thailand falls short because no commercial GM crop cultivation has been approved (technology acceptance of 1), a draft GMO regulation in 2019, and entry ease is 2 (about 2500 km, no dossier reuse established).

Taiwan

21 pts

Taiwan, GEMS-Taiwan’s country-of-origin, has arguably the highest entry ease (5/5), largely because it is closest to the team, existing domestic infrastructure, and the easiest place to validate the product and consult regulators. It scores below the Philippines on regulation instead of practicality. On the contrary, its score is held down by market data: 0.56 Mha of farmland for over 80% of farm households cultivating under 1 ha, and no commercial GM crops.

The Fertilizer Management Act requires a registration certificate before any fertilizer is manufactured, imported, or sold. Microbial fertilizers need crop-toxicity, bio-toxicity, and ecological test reports. In 2011, the Ministry of Agriculture published a list of 16 pre-recognized safe strains (8 bacteria, 4 fungi, 4 yeasts), which mostly occur in Taiwan.

An engineered B. subtilis WB800 strain is unlikely to qualify for the simplified list, so it would need the full dossier. However, the Act states it does not apply to organic fertilizer that farmers make for their own use without selling. The on-site model may fit that carve-out, but whether it covers an engineered, purchased culture needs a regulator ruling. Note also that the Act bars repackaging, mixing, or diluting registered fertilizer for sale.

Lastly, domestic market potential is limited by farm size and area (2/5), so Taiwan works best as a validation and reference market for the dossier.

European Union

20 pts

The European Union, the unified union of 27 member states in the region of Europe, holds 156.4 Mha of agricultural farmland with an average farm size of 17 ha and a clear non-microbial biostimulant category that scores well for the permeate. The culture laws hit a knockout. Regulation (EU) 2019/1009, applied since July 16th, 2022, limits microbial biostimulants to four groups (Azotobacter, Azospirillum, Rhizobium, and mycorrhizal fungi). B. subtilis is not on the list, so the culture fails outside the harmonized route and national rules apply; an EU study to expand the list is under way but not complete.

Additionally, GMO contained-use and deliberate-release rules would likely apply to the engineered strain and are why the culture scores 1/5. There are no central EU registries, products are CE-marked after conformity assessment. However, the cell-free permeate could go through the non-microbial biostimulant route, so the EU stays a long-term target for the permeate, with an EU dossier that could support entry elsewhere.

≤17 pts

Tier 3

Vietnam

16 pts

Socialist Republic of Vietnam has an average farm size of 0.4 ha with 89% of smallholders and 12.3 Mha of farmland, so both market-potential scores (farmland and smallholder) scores are 1.

Decree 84/2019/ND-CP (amended by Decree 130/2022; consolidated in 04/VBHN-BNNPTNT, 2023) requires a Decision on Recognition of Fertilizer for Trade before a product is sold. The application needs a test report from a recognized testing organization; imports need an import permit and a certificate of free sale from the exporting country. Foreign firms must apply through a representative office or branch. However, the Decree sets quality indices for conventional fertilizer classes, so a cell-free biostimulant needs a classification decision, and the GMM status of the production host is a separate question. For example, GM maize is grown on about 0.1 Mha (technology acceptance of 3) and 1650 km distance is short.

Indonesia

16 pts

The Republic of Indonesia has farmlands spanning 55.1 Mha in area in total (largest in Southeast Asia), but 60.8% of land-using farm households work under 0.5 ha (2023 census) and GM crop areas are under 0.1 Mha. Regulatory-wise, the Ministry of Agriculture Regulation 1/2019 covers organic fertilizer, biofertilizer, and soil amendments, all fileable through the Online Single Submission (OSS), with extra documents for foreign products and a local legal entity needed. Additionally, no engineered-microbe or biostimulant provision was found.

Malaysia

13 pts

Malaysia has 8.6 Mha of farmland, mainly plantations; no average farm size or smallholder figure was found, so that score was flagged provisional 3.

Regulatory-wise, the Biosafety Act 2007 (act 678) requires the National Biosafety Board (NBB) approval for the release or import of living modified organisms and their products; contained use needs only institutional biosafety committee oversight. Hence, this makes a sealed-cartridge, contained design attractive. No fertilizer-registration source was reviewed, so the permeate score reflects an evidence gap.

Case Studies (Kelpak / Sagarika) & the Cheng Han Biotechnology Briefing

Kelpak

Kelpak provides a useful case study for understanding why ReLEAF cannot assume that one regulatory classification will apply globally. Kelpak is a seaweed-derived agricultural biostimulant whose regulatory treatment differs across markets, even though the underlying product remains substantially the same. This makes it particularly relevant to ReLEAF, where the same cell-free protectant output may encounter different legal definitions, registration requirements, and evidence standards depending on the destination market.

Table 15. The 3 Big Markets and Regulatory Treatment of Kelpak
MarketRegulatory Treatment of KelpakWhat This Demonstrates
European UnionCE-marked EU fertilizing productHarmonized plant-biostimulant framework
TaiwanRegistered fertilizer productNational fertilizer classification
United StatesAgricultural input with organic-input approval; requirements can also depend on stateDecentralized regulatory system

The table shows the pattern: Kelpak does not change when it crosses a border, but its pathway does. We therefore treat each target market as a separate regulatory assessment. In the European Union, Kelpak is positioned within the EU fertilising-products framework, demonstrating how a harmonized regional system can provide a defined pathway for plant-biostimulant products. In Taiwan, its registration as a fertilizer illustrates how the same type of agricultural input can instead be evaluated through a national fertilizer classification system. In the United States, Kelpak's market position demonstrates the more decentralized nature of agricultural-input regulation, where federal considerations, organic-input requirements, and individual state requirements can all affect market access.

The contrast is important for ReLEAF because regulatory classification depends not only on the biological material itself, but also on its formulation, intended function, claims, and jurisdiction. Kelpak therefore serves as the main regulatory case study for our project: the product does not change simply because it crosses a border, but the regulatory pathway governing that product can change substantially. ReLEAF consequently treats each target market as a separate regulatory assessment rather than assuming that approval or classification in one jurisdiction automatically transfers to another.

Sagarika

Sagarika is a useful example of a seaweed-derived plant biostimulant, rather than a confirmed microbial biostimulant. IFFCO describes Sagarika Liquid as a concentrated extract of red and brown marine algae (28% w/w), containing naturally occurring plant-growth substances and nutrients. Its official product information therefore supports describing it as a seaweed-extract product; the sources reviewed do not establish that the marketed product is itself a microbial inoculant. Sagarika is relevant to ReLEAF as a comparison for how an agricultural biostimulant is formulated, positioned, and regulated, but it is not a direct biological analogue for ReLEAF's engineered production culture or purified/cell-free protein output. India's regulatory framework for biostimulants was amended through Notification S.O. 882(E) of 23 February 2021, which added biostimulants to the Fertiliser Control Order, 1985, including Clause 20C and Schedule VI.

For ReLEAF, this reinforces the importance of establishing product identity, composition, efficacy, safety, traceability, and containment early.

  • Claims influence commercialization strategy
  • Regulatory requirements should be considered alongside product development rather than after it.

We used this discussion as an industrial perspective rather than as a substitute for official regulatory requirements.

A Dual-Track Regulatory Strategy

ReLEAF’s regulatory strategy separates its abiotic-stress applications from its proposed gray-mold application.

Track 1) Abiotic-stress applications. The team will investigate appropriate plant-biostimulant or fertilizer pathways for the cell-free output intended to support plant tolerance to abiotic stress. Potential claims include BoPep4 for salinity tolerance, AtLEA14 for salinity, drought, and osmotic stress, and ACCD for heat-stress mitigation. Each claim requires relevant efficacy evidence, a defined product specification, and confirmation of the applicable regulatory category.

Track 2) BoPep4 for gray mold. Following the Jamboree, the team will investigate whether BoPep4 can control Botrytis cinerea and whether the resulting cell-free formulation falls within Taiwan’s agro-pesticide/fungicide framework. This track requires separate efficacy studies and regulatory consultation. We will not assume that the output is legally a microbial pesticide simply because engineered bacteria produce it.

The bioreactor, engineered production organism, and cell-free agricultural output must be evaluated as distinct components. Containment of the production organism is an important design feature, but it does not independently establish the legal classification or market authorization of the output. ReLEAF’s regulatory decisions will therefore follow the sequence of intended function, product claim, classification, applicable requirements, development timeline, and market access.
