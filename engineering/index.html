Engineering

Abstract

This page holds twenty-three design, build, test and learn cycles across five subsystems: cloning the light circuit, the bioreactor, the in-line photometer, the light plate apparatus and the hydroponics box.

A cycle here is one question a subsystem could not answer about itself, and the work it took to answer it. The five tracks run in the order the project runs. Cloning builds the strain. The bioreactor holds it behind a membrane and lets the protectant across. The photometer watches the culture without opening the loop. The light plate apparatus is the bench instrument that would drive the switch, and the hydroponics box is what the protectant eventually reaches. Each track is read one cycle at a time.

Each cloning cycle redesigned on what the last one learned: a screen full of background added a sequence-verified entry step, a shared ampicillin marker then moved the build onto the kit’s own entry vector, and a recombination worry rebuilt module 1. Nine correct junctions across three clones say the four modules are joined in the right order; the joined plasmid is not sequenced end to end. No record shows PcpcG2 output changing with green light. Six cloning cycles put four modules into one plasmid and none of them tested whether the circuit does what it was designed to do, so every sentence here about the switch is a statement about the design, and it is written as one.

The four hardware tracks are where the numbers are, and every one of them is constitutive expression in B. subtilis WB800; none of them is the light switch doing anything. The reactor ran every subsystem at once on cells for fifty-three hours and put ACC deaminase on the far side of the membrane, weakly; how much arrives there has never been measured. The photometer held a culture in line past four hundred hours and then matched a benchtop instrument to within 3.8 % with no calibration transform at all, which is the one thing on this page that came out better than it was designed to. The light plate apparatus lights twenty-four cultures under six defined conditions, and the overnight run that proved it snapped a printed post and leaked light between the wells. The named failures stay where they happened.

Cloning the light circuit

Six cycles took the four modules of the optogenetic circuit from a kit that would not assemble to one plasmid with every junction checked. The write-up below is the team’s own, cycle by cycle.

  1. DDesign

    Based on the characterization of the CcaS/CcaR optogenetic system reported in Castillo-Hair 2019 [1], we redesigned and generated the coding sequences of the green/red light-responsive two-component system (CcaSm3 and CcaR), together with the PCB production enzyme (ho1-pcyA fusion), a version that can increase the PCB level, and our target output and reporter coding sequences (LEA, ACCD, BoPep4) by the modular cloning (MoClo) system [3].

    To assemble these functional transcription units into the MoClo 2.0 system provided from the distribution kit, we designated standard regulatory elements for each module. We designed custom PCR primers incorporating type II restriction enzyme recognition sites (BsaI for Level 1 assembly) flanked by specific standard overhangs following every Level 1 construct. This was designed to allow sequential assembly of individual promoters, RBSs, CDSs and terminators into single transcriptional units (Level 1), which could subsequently be directionally combined into multi-gene circuits at Level 2.

  2. BBuild

    We performed PCR amplification on template DNA to add the designed restriction sites and overhangs to each individual part. The resulting raw PCR products were purified and subjected directly to Golden Gate digestion-ligation reactions with our Level 1 destination vectors (pJUMP-29) to construct the complete Level 1 single gene modules. The assembly mixtures were transformed into E. coli DH5α competent cells and plated on selective media.

  3. TTest

    Transformant colonies were screened by colony PCR to check for successful Level 1 Golden Gate assembly, which yielded inconsistent and low-efficiency cloning. Amplified fragments were analyzed via agarose gel electrophoresis to assess insert size. A subset of colonies yielding bands near the expected sizes were supposed to be selected for plasmid isolation and subsequent restriction digest verification. All trials were unsuccessful and as a result, data is not shown.

  4. LLearn

    Colony PCR screening and restriction digests revealed high background, including potentially empty vectors and off-target genes, making it difficult to distinguish from assembly failures and PCR artifacts. We learned that attempting Level 1 assembly straight from unverified PCR products introduced too much ambiguity, highlighting the need for an intermediate cloning step to generate clean, sequence-verified Level 0 entry parts.

The coding sequences for the green and red light components, ccaR, ccaSm3 and the ho1:pcyA operon, were taken from the CcaS/CcaR characterisation of Castillo-Hair and colleagues [1] and paired with the cargo sequences the project needed, LEA, ACCD and BoPep4. To get those into MoClo 2.0 as transcription units, each module was given a standard set of regulatory parts, and every part got a custom primer pair carrying type IIS sites: BbsI and BpiI for Level 0 entry, BsaI for Level 1, each flanked by the standard overhangs. That hierarchy is the one the build kept, even after the kit under it was swapped.

The mixtures were transformed into E. coli DH5α and plated on selective media, screened by colony PCR and read on agarose for insert size. Colonies with a band near an expected size were marked for miniprep and a confirming digest. Colony PCR and restriction digest both came back with high background: empty vectors and off-target bands that could not be told apart from an assembly failure or a PCR artefact.

The other half of the fix came from outside the team: a professor connected to the original paper supplied the SubtiToolKit [4]. That was luck rather than planning, and it is recorded here as luck.

A pGEM-T Easy intermediate costs a day and removes the ambiguity

  1. DDesign

    Based on the results from DBTL 2, we shifted our focus toward optimizing the SubtiToolKit cloning workflow itself, rather than redesigning the genetic architecture of each module. Since the BsaI-only Level 1 entry step had introduced ambiguity in our earlier assembly, with colonies producing inconsistent or unconfirmed results, we revisited the cloning strategy at the PCR and vector-entry stage to improve reliability before moving into Level 1 Golden Gate assembly. The overall part composition for each module remained unchanged from DBTL 1 and 2; the redesign targeted the intermediate cloning steps used to generate clean, verified Level 0 parts.

  2. BBuild

    To improve the reliability of our Level 0 entry step, we amplified each module’s coding sequence by PCR and cloned the amplicon into a pGEM-T Easy vector [5]. pGEM-T Easy provides a direct, rapid method for cloning PCR products through direct TA cloning: the vector is supplied pre-linearized with single 3′-thymidine (T) overhangs, which are complementary to the single 3′-adenosine (A) overhangs left on PCR products by Taq and other non-proofreading polymerases. This complementarity allows the amplicon to ligate directly into the vector without any additional end-polishing, restriction digestion, or blunting step, making it a fast and efficient intermediate cloning strategy compared to attempting Golden Gate entry directly from a raw PCR product.

    A plasmid backbone is also generally easier to work with and verify than a linear PCR product: pGEM-T Easy clones can be more reliably purified, screened by colony PCR, and sequence-confirmed before being subjected to the BpiI/BsaI digestion-ligation reactions required for Level 1 assembly, reducing the risk of carrying an uncharacterized PCR error into downstream Golden Gate steps. Positive pGEM-T Easy clones carrying each construct’s coding sequence were identified by colony PCR and sent for sequencing to confirm the insert prior to use in subsequent Golden Gate reactions.

  3. TTest

    Colonies from the pGEM-T Easy transformations were screened by colony PCR using primers, and PCR products were run on an agarose gel alongside a DNA ladder to assess assembly success. A correct colony PCR band is one that runs at the expected size, calculated as the length between the T7 promoter and the SP6 promoter amplified by the screening primers; this confirms that a DNA fragment of the correct length was successfully ligated into the vector at the expected position. Colonies producing a single, clean band at this expected size were considered positive candidates for the correct insert. In contrast, colonies producing no band, a band at the size of the empty vector (i.e. no insert, indicating self-ligation of the pGEM-T Easy vector), or bands at incorrect or unexpected sizes were classified as negative and excluded from further analysis, since these patterns indicate either an empty vector or a truncated or mis-primed amplicon rather than the intended insert.

    Colonies showing the expected band size were selected and sent for sequencing to confirm that the cloned insert matched the intended coding sequence with no mutations, insertions, or deletions, prior to proceeding with Golden Gate digestion into the STK system.

  4. LLearn

    The pGEM-T Easy TA cloning step proved to be a fast and reliable way to generate verified Level 0 entry material before doing Golden Gate assembly. Because colony PCR alone can only confirm that an insert of approximately the correct size is present, and cannot rule out point mutations or small indels introduced during PCR amplification, we relied on sequencing rather than enzyme digestion to confirm the fidelity of each cloned insert; we did not perform additional restriction digest verification at this stage, since correct band size on colony PCR combined with full-length sequencing was sufficient to confirm both insert identity and sequence accuracy before proceeding to BsaI-based STK assembly.

    This step did add an extra day to our workflow compared to attempting direct entry from the PCR product, since colonies had to be picked, screened, and miniprepped before sequencing. However, it reduced the ambiguity we had encountered in DBTL 2, where using PCR products directly made it harder to distinguish true assembly failures from PCR artifacts.

  1. DDesign

    After DBTL 3, we further optimized our SubtiToolKit cloning strategy (v2) in response to a compatibility issue identified with the pGEM-T Easy intermediate cloning step: the pGEM-T Easy vector uses the same antibiotic resistance gene (ampicillin) as our Level 1 destination vectors (1A, 1B, 1C, 1D), which complicated downstream selection and increased the risk of background contamination from carryover vector during Golden Gate assembly. To resolve this, we redesigned our cloning approach to construct each module’s coding sequence directly using STK Level 0 primers, skipping the pGEM-T Easy intermediate altogether. This step was only relevant to the CDS not cloned into pGEM-T Easy, as the ones from DBTL 3 were still usable in Level 1 Golden Gate assembly.

  2. BBuild

    We amplified the coding sequence directly with primers carrying the STK Level 0 (BpiI) and Level 1 (BsaI) tail architecture, as described for Modules 1 to 4, and cloned the amplicon directly into the pSTK-0C CDS entry vector rather than through pGEM-T Easy. This removed the antibiotic resistance conflict, since pSTK-0 and our Level 1 destination vectors use distinguishable selection markers (chloramphenicol versus ampicillin), allowing a more obvious difference between correctly assembled Level 1 clones and the vector.

  3. TTest

    Three Level 0 screens on 14 August, all clean at their expected sizes, and three clones from each went to liquid culture. The students’ document leaves this step to the gels themselves.

  4. LLearn

    Although this approach resolved the antibiotic resistance conflict, it raised a new concern regarding potential recombination events in Bacillus subtilis. Because several of our modules share repeated regulatory sequences (e.g. Pveg, RBS0, B0015) across multiple constructs, we became concerned that homologous recombination between repeated elements, once all four modules were combined in B. subtilis, could destabilize parts of the assembled optogenetic circuit, potentially weakening the CcaS/CcaR signals. This concern motivated us to reconsider the repeated part usage across our modules going into the next design cycle.

Only the coding sequences that had not already been cloned in cycle 3 were affected; the ones already in pGEM-T Easy were still usable at Level 1.

Once all four modules sat in one cell, long stretches of identical sequence could give recombination something to act on, and the CcaS/CcaR signal path is exactly what would be damaged. The redesign written at the end of this cycle is one line: diversify the promoter, ribosome binding site and terminator across the modules so that no two of them share a long identical stretch. That is what cycle 6 was run on.

  1. DDesign

    To construct our circuit’s output module, we designed a library of candidate constructs capable of expressing and secreting functional biotic and abiotic plant protectants. Because the optimal protectant–secretion signal combination could not be determined prior, we adopted a parallel design strategy built on a unified expression format: the constitutive promoter Pveg, a composite ribosome binding site (EEBD-RBS0), a secretion signal peptide (SamyQ or Csn) fused upstream of the protectant coding sequence, a histag to view protein expression, and the B0015 terminator. All constructs were designed for assembly directly into the pSTK-EXP1-sfGFP destination shuttle vector, enabling immediate expression in E. coli as well as downstream transfer into Bacillus subtilis. To integrate parts that weren’t from the SubtiToolKit library (Csn, ACCD, Lea, BoPep4), we designed custom primers following standard STK architecture: a 5′ spacer, a BpiI site, a BsaI site, and a gene specific annealing sequence.

  2. BBuild

    We physically assembled all candidate Level 0 parts using BpiI-mediated Golden Gate cloning into pSTK-0-sfGFP, replacing the sfGFP dropout cassette. The resulting Level 0 entry plasmids were transformed into chemically competent E. coli DH5α, isolated, and sequence-verified. In the second assembly step, the verified Level 0 protectant cassettes were combined with pSTK parts (Pveg, EEBD-RBS0, and B0015) and the pSTK-EXP1-sfGFP destination shuttle vector in a BsaI Golden Gate reaction. Ligated expression vectors were transformed into E. coli DH5α, screen-confirmed by miniprep digestion, and prepared for downstream expression and functional testing.

  3. TTest

Positive clones producing single, clean bands at expected amplicon sizes were then electroporated into Bacillus subtilis. Transformant colonies were screened by colony PCR to confirm the presence of the full-length output expression cassettes. Electrophoresis of PCR products on agarose gels yielded sharp, distinct bands at the expected sizes, matching our prior sequence-verification data in B. subtilis 168. Verified single colonies were inoculated into liquid media, which were then scaled up for downstream functional testing in our bioreactor.

  4. LLearn

    Testing of our ACCD in our bioreactor provided critical insight into both expression levels and circuit stability. Using the Csn:ACCD construct allowed us to directly monitor protectant production and evaluate secretion efficiency; however, constitutive runs through our bioreactor and a western blot revealed that while ACCD expression was not clearly detectable, the overall protein yield was relatively low and did not reach optimal physiological levels. This highlighted the need for future optimization of expression elements (such as promoter strength or codon optimization) to boost output efficiency for demanding protectants.

    Furthermore, from an assembly perspective, although this cycle is mostly focused on the constitutive expression of ACCD, we were also working on our inducible system at the same time. Constructing multiple modules containing identical regulatory elements (Pveg, EEBD-RBS0, and B0015) highlighted an important concern. Reusing identical promoter and terminator sequences across all four modules of our full circuit created substantial sequence redundancy. When introduced into Bacillus subtilis, long stretches of sequence identity carry a high risk of triggering homologous recombination, which could destabilize or excise parts of our optogenetic circuit.

    Plotted against mass added, both instruments track the standard at R² > 0.999 (Releaf 0.9999, Bio-Drop 0.9998), and plotted against each other the raw points fall along the line of identity, agreeing to a median paired difference of 3.8%.

  4. LLearn

    We originally expected the Releaf readings would need a calibration transform; instead the two runs showed that no calibration is needed. Fitting our readings to the Bio-Drop would tie them to the reference instrument’s own measurement error. A calibration step, in other words, could make the readings worse rather than better.

Show details Two exclusions made on objective grounds, what the two runs each probed, and one accuracy claim that still has to be reconciled.

Two single-point exclusions were applied, each on objective grounds, and both are shown as open markers in the figures rather than deleted. In Test 1, the mg = 37 Bio-Drop reading is a handling error, off the trend of its neighbors. In Test 2, the mg = 5 Bio-Drop reading violates monotonicity, it exceeds the readings both above and below it, which is impossible for an increasing standard. In both cases, the paired Releaf reading is on trend.

The raw, uncalibrated Releaf output already sits near one-to-one with the reference and tracks the ground-truth mass standard with a residual scatter comparable to the Bio-Drop’s: in Test 1, across 2 to 40 mg, 0.030 OD600 for Releaf against 0.031 for the Bio-Drop. A line fit through the raw Test 2 points scatters by an RMSE of 0.011 OD600 about the line of identity, which measures how tight the raw agreement is and is not a correction applied to anything. The SiO2 calibration we deferred remains available as a future check, but on the strength of the TiO2 comparison it is a confirmation step, not a dependency.

Cycles 7 and 8 are named on the reactor record and neither has been run. Cycle 7 is the silicon dioxide calibration, deferred above. Cycle 8 is a simplified photometer, and the reasoning behind it is now written out: three parts carry most of the cost, the flow cuvette earns its price because it is what lets the instrument read the loop live and the beam splitter is what keeps a long run honest, which leaves the paralleling lens as the part to remove. Shaping the LED’s own output instead — a sanded flat cap, or a different LED — is expected to make the instrument two thirds smaller and $70 cheaper, against about $150 for the build the long run was made on and $15,000 for the Bio-Drop it was checked against. All of that is expectation. One draft reports the simplified build accurate, another reports it untested, no comparison numbers exist, and the older assembly is the one kept as final.

Light plate apparatus

DiOPAL is a testing apparatus, not part of the control loop. It exists to answer one question: how the wavelength and intensity of applied light affect protectant production in engineered B. subtilis. Three cycles took it from two LEDs on a breadboard to an enclosed instrument with software-set intensity tiers, and the overnight run that was meant to prove the third one broke it.

Cycle 1 · Dual-wavelength array Closed

Put a green and a red LED in every column and see whether the idea works at all

Result
The concept holds. Nothing about it is quantitative: output was uncontrolled and external light was not blocked.
Changed next
Measure every LED, sort them into matched groups, and shield the cultures.

  1. DDesign

    Put a green and a red LED in each column so we can switch between them quickly and exercise the on and off control of protectant production directly. Nothing is calibrated at this stage, because the question is whether the concept works at all before we spend effort making it precise.

  2. BBuild

    Dual LED per column, allowing for quick adjustment from Green to Red light output. Wire management prints, included from the start, since even at this size the harness gets unmanageable fast. The array was driven straight from a breadboard.

  3. LLearn

    Output consistency between LEDs was not controlled. External light was not blocked.

    Internal-resistance variance is a common problem in cheap single-bulb LEDs: nominally identical parts draw different currents and emit different intensities at the same drive level. As long as that is true, an intensity tier is not a real quantity, and the wavelength comparison is not fair either, since the green and red channels would be running at unknown relative brightness. Ambient light adds an unknown amount of illumination to every tube, and it lands hardest on the low-intensity condition, which is exactly where the separation has to be cleanest.

Cycle 2 · Matched output and light shielding Closed

Twenty LEDs sold as identical spread by a quarter, so the intensity tiers had to come from software

Result
Green ran from 2.01 to 2.54 kLux and red from 360 to 438 lux, spreads of 26 % and 22 %. Sorted into fours, each group holds to about 1 to 2 %.
Changed next
Set the tiers by pulse-width modulation, and enclose the electronics.

  1. DDesign

    The approach is to measure the light output of every LED individually and use that to identify units with similar internal resistance. From the matched units we form three groups of four per wavelength, one group per intensity tier, arranged as columns. Because the LEDs within a column are matched, the column can be recalibrated as a unit through straightforward mathematical adjustment, so that every column at a given tier matches every other: that is what turns low, medium and high from labels into reproducible quantities. Coverage was added to block external light, so that the intensity a culture receives is the intensity we set.

  2. BBuild

    Every LED was driven at 5 V and measured individually on a lux meter using a printed sensor fixture, so each reading is taken at the same distance and orientation. Each LED carries a tag made of its row and its column, rows A to D running downward and columns 1 to 5 across, so any physical LED can be traced back to its own measurement. The array was then rewired with the matched units grouped into columns.

  3. TTest

    All twenty green LEDs were read in kLux and all twenty red in lux, at 5 V, one at a time, and the grids are in the details below, laid out by measurement tag and not by the array’s own 4 × 6 grid. Sorting those into groups of four by output gives the matched sets the tiers are built from, and the six channels were read again after rewiring.

  4. LLearn

    Sorting by measured output tightens each group considerably: the green low group holds 2.33 to 2.34 and the green mid group 2.35 to 2.37, well inside 1%, and the red groups hold to about 2% each. But the separation between tiers is smaller than we need. Green low to green high is about 4% and red low to red high about 10%. Those gaps are the parts’ own spread after sorting; they are not three distinct light levels. The tiers therefore have to come from the PWM duty cycle rather than from LED selection: matching makes the four LEDs in a column agree with each other, and software sets how bright that column runs.

What remained after this cycle was also practical. The electronics were still exposed, intensity was still set by hand, and there was no dedicated way to hold the test tubes in a repeatable position over the array.

Cycle 3 · Enclosed and software-controlled Open

Twenty-four cultures under six defined conditions, and then a night on a shaker took the base apart

Result
An instrument that can be handed to someone else: enclosed electronics, code-set intensity, and a holder that fixes the distance between LED and culture. The overnight run on a shaker snapped a post off the bottom plate, pulled wires loose, and leaked light between the wells.
Changed next
Repeat that overnight run on the repaired base, measure photon flux rather than lux, check uniformity between wells, and run the circuit under it.

  1. DDesign

    An outer frame print houses the LED wiring and the perf board, so no electronics sit loose on the bench. Light intensity is varied in code, so a run is defined by a set of values rather than by a physical adjustment someone has to remember. Slider prints allow assembly and disassembly without tools. A tall print holds the test tubes at a fixed position over the array, so the distance between LED and culture is the same every run.

  2. BBuild

    Control runs from an Arduino Uno R4 WiFi. PWM signals from the Arduino drive three MOSFETs, one per intensity tier, and software sets the duty cycle for each tier. The selected intensities are held constant for the duration of a test, so each culture receives a fixed, well-defined dose; three calibrated tiers across two wavelengths give six defined light conditions per run.

    The switching stage moved with the build. In cycle 2 the three MOSFETs stood on a breadboard on the bench beside the LED holder; here they sit on the perf board underneath it, with the housing over them.

  3. TTest

    During our overnight test run, the connection point on the bottom plate broke off. The LPA was on a shaker for the run; the frequency was most likely too high for the printed post. Several wires came undone and had to be resoldered. The white tube holder print also allowed light to leak into neighbouring holes, which compromised our data, as the test tubes were receiving light from more than one source.

  4. LLearn

    All four screws on the bottom plate are now used, not two, which spreads the load across four points instead of concentrating it on a pair, and every soldered connection was reinforced. Neither change has been through a second overnight run on the shaker, so the repair is untested. The white tube holder was replaced with a black one, and its wall thickness increased from two loops to four.

Show details What the team now counts as success, the unit the readings are in, and what this apparatus cannot do by design.

This changed our definition of what it means for DiOPAL to be functional. Having 24 LEDs turn on and produce different readings is not enough. We need to establish the actual photon flux at each condition, verify illumination uniformity between wells, and determine whether the selected conditions actually produce the expected changes in protectant expression.

The last of those is the induction run the whole page waits on. The apparatus is ready for it and the Level 2 strain exists. Earlier faults in the same build are recorded and fixed: one green and red column stopped answering its MOSFET and two ran very weak until the MOSFET was changed, the assembly and disassembly is slow, and the jumper wire on the MOSFET spike sits loose.

Every reading on this track is in lux, which is weighted for human vision and peaks near 555 nm, so the weighting error is not the same size on the green channel as on the red. The dose that matters to the cells is photon flux, in µmol m−2 s−1. The team found this in week 16; until the readings are converted they compare our LEDs with each other and cannot be compared with a published dose.

Three things this apparatus cannot do

It applies a fixed dose and does not respond to the culture, so it cannot close a control loop, and nothing on this track transfers to the bioreactor until a feedback path is added. Its wavelengths, about 535 nm to induce and about 670 nm to halt, are taken from the published CcaS/CcaR values and have never been measured on our own parts. And matching corrects the spread between LEDs at build time; it does not correct drift as they age or warm through a long run.

Hydroponics box

A printed plate that floats on the medium, so thirteen Arabidopsis seedlings stay at the same depth no matter how much liquid is left. Three cycles, each one removing a thing somebody had to do by hand.

Cycle 1 · Fixed plate · April 2026 Named failure

A fixed plate holds the roots at a depth that the plants themselves destroy

Every hydroponic run in the project starts with the same question: where are the roots in the liquid? A fixed plate answers it once, on the day the run is set up, and then the answer drifts for a fortnight.

Result
Three plates cut, taped and drilled at 300 mL, 1.5 L and 2 L. The level had to be set by hand before every run, and topped up during it.
Changed next
Put the flotation inside the plate, so the plate falls with the level instead of being set against it.

  1. DDesign

    Labs that grow Arabidopsis in liquid medium improvise the setup: a piece of foam cut to size, a mesh taped over a container, or a fixed plate sitting above a reservoir. We did the same, and sized a plate for each of the three box volumes we had on the bench.

  2. BBuild

    Three plates, cut to the box, taped, and drilled on a grid of seed positions. Seed holders dropped into the holes; the medium sat underneath at a level set by eye before the run.

  3. TTest

    The level turned out to be a narrow window. It has to sit just under the growing platform and as high as it can be got: too low and the roots lose contact, too high and the platform floods. And it does not stay where it is put. The plants drink and the medium evaporates, so a level that was right at the start of a run is wrong by the end of it.

  4. LLearn

    Somebody has to watch the reservoir and top it up, and every top-up is a chance for conditions to drift between replicates. That is the variable the plate was supposed to remove, and the fixed plate reintroduces it once a day. The fix is not a better level: it is to stop setting a level at all.

Cycle 2 · First floating version · from 25 June 2026 Closed

Sealed air inside the frame, and four corner poles to trim what the arithmetic got wrong

If the plate floats, the roots stay at the same depth however much liquid is left, and nobody has to top anything up. The question that replaces the level is the freeboard: how high the plate rides.

Result
A printed plate that floats on its own frame. The freeboard came out adjustable by washers rather than right by calculation.
Changed next
Run the displacement calculation again on every design change until the poles are not doing anything.

  1. DDesign

    Move the flotation into the frame: sealed air chambers that are part of the structure, with nothing to glue on and nothing to come loose in the medium. Sizing them is a displacement problem. The plate has to displace its own mass, plus the seedlings, plus whatever water the printed part takes on, while sitting at the freeboard that puts the roots in the medium and the crowns above it.

  2. BBuild

    Printed in PLA on a standard FDM machine, pontoons hollow at 15 % gyroid infill with a teardrop cross-section that prints without support. A pole at each corner took washers, so the freeboard could be trimmed after printing rather than by reprinting the whole part.

  3. TTest

    It floats, and the corner poles get used. One worked case from the drafts has the plate at roughly 180 g, where the pontoons came up short by about 10.7 cm³ of volume each. The printed plate was never weighed, so the case cannot be checked against the part that exists.

  4. LLearn

    The poles are the safety margin on a calculation we did not trust yet. They are also four parts, four washers and a decision per run. Every change to wall thickness or footprint moves the mass and the displacement at the same time, so the calculation has to be rerun on every design change rather than once at the start.

Cycle 3 · Current version · July to August 2026 Open

The ballast poles came off, and thirteen seedlings ran four boxes for eleven days

The last change to the plate is a subtraction. Tuning the sealed volume closely enough makes the corner poles redundant, which removes four parts, four washer stacks and one judgement call per run.

Result
Sixteen printed parts, 42.3 cm³ of sealed air, 3.5 g of added load per millimetre of draft, and four boxes run side by side from 25 July to 4 August.
Changed next
Weigh an assembled plate and read its freeboard off the load line, which is the one measurement that would close this track.

  1. DDesign

    Smaller footprint, clip-in handles so the plate lifts out of the reservoir without anyone touching the seedlings, and thirteen seed positions in rows of four, five and four. The buoyancy is computed from the STLs rather than estimated: the two chambers hold 42.27 cm³ of sealed air, and down the pontoons’ straight sides the hull gains 3.51 cm³ per millimetre of draft.

  2. BBuild

    Sixteen parts in PLA, no supports, no adhesives and no fasteners. As drawn, the waterline sits 6.0 mm under the slab, a freeboard of 9.2 mm, where the assembled plate displaces 54.2 cm³.

  3. TTest

    The plate carried the hydroponic assay: four boxes, control, pre-treatment, co-treatment and post-treatment, photographed from above on 25 July, 28 July and 4 August. By the last date the co-treatment box carries the largest and greenest rosettes and the post-treatment box the palest. No run on this plate has had its freeboard measured.

  4. LLearn

    Every 3.5 g added sinks it about 1 mm, so the sensitivity is known and the load line is drawn; what is missing is one weighing. Printed solid, an upper bound, the sixteen parts come to 92.6 g and would float with 1.9 mm of freeboard, which is the number that retires the 180 g worked case still in the drafts.
