This page runs in four tracks. What we set out to build, and what was fixed before cycle one

ReLeaf grows engineered Bacillus subtilis inside a hollow-fibre membrane, feeds it green light when a plant is under stress, and lets the protectant the cells make cross the membrane into the root zone while the cells themselves stay behind. Two things had to exist for that to be more than a diagram. A light-responsive circuit in B. subtilis, and a vessel that keeps a culture alive and instrumented for long enough to be worth switching on.

The optogenetic system is four transcription units, not one construct

We built the light switch on the cyanobacterial CcaS–CcaR two-component system, which senses green light near 520 nm and drives the PcpcG2 promoter. CcaS is a photoreceptor histidine kinase and it does not work on its own: it needs phycocyanobilin as its chromophore, and B. subtilis does not make any. So the system is four parts, and they were designed, assembled and screened as four separate transcription units before anything was joined:

A · Chromophore supply

Ho1::pcyA — heme oxygenase and PCB ferredoxin oxidoreductase, run as one operon, so the chassis makes its own phycocyanobilin.

B · Light sensor

CcaSm3 — the photoreceptor kinase, built twice, under PliaG and under PlepA, because we did not know what expression level it wanted.

D · Response regulator

CcaR — the partner that CcaS phosphorylates, and the protein that actually binds PcpcG2.

I · Output

PcpcG2-veg driving a secretion-tagged cargo — csn::ACCD, csn::LEA, and a chromoprotein reporter, csn::AmilCP, for reading the switch by eye.

The letters are not decoration. They are the position labels used on every gel in the lab record and in every construct name below, so a clone called ACDI is unambiguous: chromophore module A, the PlepA sensor, CcaR, and the ACC-deaminase output. That vocabulary is used identically on Parts and Results.

Three constraints were settled before the first assembly, and they set the cycles

Marburg 2024 wrote its assumptions down before cycle one so a reader could judge the design against them instead of against whatever happened to work. Ours were these.

  • The chassis is B. subtilis, and we assumed it would recombine. B. subtilis is naturally competent and recombination-proficient, so four copies of one terminator in a single plasmid looked like a substrate. That assumption drove cycle 5 of the circuit track, and cycle 5 is also where an expert told us the assumption was wrong. It is listed here because it shaped the work, not because it survived.
  • The vessel had to contain the organism, not just hold it. The pivot on 15 April committed us to keeping engineered bacteria inside a sealed vessel and letting only the product out. That is a membrane specification, and it is why the reactor is hollow-fibre and not a stirred tank.
  • School budget, school lab. A commercial in-line OD sensor and a chlorophyll fluorometer were both priced and both dropped. The fluorometer went on 11 May on staffing, not feasibility; the OD sensor we built instead.
Not yet measured

No induction curve exists. Nothing on this page shows PcpcG2 output changing with green light, because that experiment has not been run. Every statement about the switch below is about whether the DNA is correct and whether the hardware can hold a culture, and that is a different claim. The first induction run is cycle twenty, and it is described at the bottom of this page.

A cycle changed a design; a repair changed a part

Nineteen is a number worth stating and a bad number to inflate, so here is the line we drew. Replacing a leaking fitting is a repair. Replacing it because the previous design's behaviour was measured and found unacceptable is a cycle. A cycle has to have a question we did not know the answer to, something built or changed to answer it, a measurement that produced a number or a scored gel, and a decision that altered what came next. If any of those four is missing, the work is in the notebooks and not on this page.

Applying that honestly removed several things we had been calling cycles. Swapping the pump tubing for BPT after the medium leak is a repair, and it appears inside cycle 1 of the reactor track, not as a cycle of its own. Re-running a colony PCR because the gel was overloaded is a repair. The Level 2 assembly failing three times in a row is one cycle, not three, because nothing about the design changed between attempts — which is itself the finding.

Every cycle below uses the literal headings Design, Build, Test and Learn, and adds Leiden 2023's fifth heading, Redesign, which forces us to write down in words what the next cycle will change and why. Where two steps were genuinely one step at the bench, they are merged and the heading says so.

Cloning the four modules5 cycles · all closed

Five cycles, and only the first one is about the circuit design. The other four are about the assembly method, because that is what actually stopped us. We changed cloning systems once, changed the part carrier twice, audited our own screening primers, and finished on a stoichiometry change. The DNA design barely moved.

We write these five together instead of module by module, because the four modules were never independent. They share a ribosome binding site, a terminator and, for a while, a promoter, and every problem below hit all four at once. Ownership was split — the chromophore and output modules to one member, the sensor and regulator to another — but the cycles were not.

One design decision worth its own paragraph

The secretion tag csn is toxic to E. coli, and every one of these plasmids has to be built in E. coli before it can go anywhere near B. subtilis. The output module therefore carries EEBD, an E. coli expression blocking device: two B. subtilis-only recombination sites flanking a double terminator, sitting between the promoter and the ribosome binding site. In E. coli the terminator stops transcription before it reaches the cargo. On transfer into B. subtilis, recombination excises the block and expression is restored. It is the reason the output constructs are clonable at all.

Circuit · iteration 1 of 5MoClo 2.0, and a shuttle vector we had to build before we could use it

Can a four-module optogenetic circuit be assembled in B. subtilis using the MoClo 2.0 standard?

DesignMoClo 2.0, and the vector it needed

MoClo 2.0 imposes two things on every part: no internal BsaI site, and a specific pair of 4 bp overhangs that fix the part's position in the transcription unit. Primer and fragment design was therefore mostly bookkeeping — domesticate every internal site, then attach the correct position-defining overhang to each end.

The harder problem was the destination. We had a shuttle vector, pNW33N, that moves between E. coli and B. subtilis, but in the team's own words it is specific in conventional restriction cloning but not Golden Gate assembly. So before assembling anything we had to build the vector: clone the sfGFP dropout cassette out of pJUMP49-2B and into pNW33N, making a MoClo 2.0 Level 2 plasmid we called pNW33N-L2(sfGFP).

BuildGibson, then digest checks

pNW33N recovered from BCRC and verified, then cut and joined by Gibson assembly to the pJUMP49-2B PCR product (3 µg/mL digest against 60 µg/mL insert, 50 °C for 30 min, into DH5α). Coding sequences were ordered on pTwist-Amp-High backbones. Golden Gate ran at a 2:1 insert-to-vector ratio, selected on kanamycin.

TestThe parts checked out; the assembly did not

Every part verified by digest. No colonies from the assembly.

pNW33N-L2(sfGFP) was confirmed by EcoRI/BtsI on 23 April and by BsaI on 30 April. The synthesised coding sequences were confirmed by EcoRI on 4 and 6 June — Csn::AmilCP linearising at 3,051 bp and Csn::AccD at 3,423 bp, both as designed. The Level 1 assembly is where it stopped:

We initially utilized the MoClo 2.0 standard, but we realized the resulting quality was quite poor as transformation didn't reveal any colonies, and the Golden Gate Assembly efficiency was low.

LearnThe method was the bottleneck, and the fix arrived by luck

Nothing was wrong with the parts. The failure was efficiency: with no colonies to screen, there was no gel to read and no way to tell a bad overhang from a bad ligation from too little DNA. A team member's independent write-up from the same weeks reaches the same wall — the DNA we assembled using Golden Gate Assembly did not yield the desired results… the most likely of which is that our DNA concentration was low to start with — which is a diagnosis you can only make when you have nothing else to go on.

We should say plainly that what unblocked this was not cleverness. A professor who had worked on the SubtiToolKit's original paper gave us the kit. Without that we would have spent the summer optimising a MoClo 2.0 reaction.

One thing from this cycle survived and turned out to be right. The kit's own shuttle vector, pSTK-EXP-1-sfGFP, is described in its library sheet as produced from pNW33N adapted for STK — exactly the vector we had spent April building for ourselves.

RedesignChange the kit, not the parts

Rebuild all four modules in the SubtiToolKit. Keep the part definitions; change only the assembly grammar and the vectors. The one document that records the transition is a June forecasting sheet that lists Module 4-1 (molecular cloning 2.0) and Module 4-2 (molecular cloning 2.0), then a horizontal rule, then Module 4-1 (STK) and Module 4-2 (STK).

Circuit · iteration 2 of 5The SubtiToolKit removes a design step, and four positions close in eight days

Does a cloning kit built for B. subtilis remove the step that was blocking us, or just move it?

DesignOne universal primer tail, two enzymes

The SubtiToolKit is an EcoFlex-derived Golden Gate kit for B. subtilis, with pre-built Level 0 part vectors and Level 1 destination vectors carrying sfGFP dropout cassettes. What it changed for us was primer design. Instead of a bespoke overhang table per construct, every primer carries the same 29 bp universal tail:

  1. a 5′ spacer, ATATAT, so a restriction enzyme has something to bind near the end of a linear fragment;
  2. a BpiI site, GAAGAC, for Level 0 entry — BpiI cuts outside its own recognition sequence, so digestion removes the tail entirely and leaves a 4 bp overhang that drops the fragment into pSTK-0 in place of the sfGFP dropout;
  3. a BsaI site, GGTCTC, for Level 1 — whose inner 4 bp overhang defines the part's position in the transcription unit.

The two enzymes are not interchangeable and each does exactly one job. The overhangs are chosen to do double duty where they can: in the chromophore module's forward primer the Level 1 overhang is TATG, which carries the start codon of ho1 and marks the 5′ boundary of the coding sequence in the same four bases.

Every entry in the team's primer table from this point on has the same value in its Purpose column: Convert MoClo to STK. Sixteen parts, one instruction.

BuildTwenty kit plasmids in, eight inserts amplified, four positions assembled

On 18 June the kit was minipreped and sized — twenty parts on one gel, including the four Level 1 destination vectors (pSTK-1A through 1D-sfGFP), seven Level 0 promoters, pSTK-0AB-PtagC+RBS, the 3P promoter, RBS0, and the terminators B0015 and L3S2P21. On 19 June eight inserts were amplified with the new tails. Golden Gate ran as 20 µL reactions with T4 ligase and BsaI-HFv2, thirty cycles of 37 °C for one minute against 16 °C for one minute, then 60 °C for five.

LearnThe kit removed a design step, not a bench step

What made the difference was not a better ligase or a better thermocycler. It was that the kit already owned the overhang grammar, the dropout cassettes and the shuttle vector, so the only thing left to get right was a primer tail we could write once and reuse. We then reused it literally: the light-sensing module's primers reuse the exact same 29 bp universal tail that we have already validated on our PCB production module primers.

Two positions did not come. The csn::LEA pair, under both the light-driven and the constitutive promoter, never produced a positive clone in this or any later round.

RedesignStop feeding PCR products into Golden Gate

Every insert going into these reactions was a raw, unsequenced PCR product. Make each one a verified plasmid first.

Circuit · iteration 3 of 5pGEM-T Easy makes clean donors, and carries the wrong resistance gene

Is a sequence-verified plasmid a better Golden Gate donor than a PCR product?

DesignVerify the part before you assemble with it

The team's stated reason, from their own outline: The vector is easier to assemble compared to the PCR product. Written out: the coding sequences were individually cloned into pGEM®-T vectors, rather than directly into the pSTK-0-sGFP vector, to generate the basic parts. Once the plasmids were confirmed by DNA sequencing, they were ready for Golden Gate assembly.

BuildTA cloning, seven coding sequences

pGEM-T Easy at 50 ng in a 3,015 bp backbone, 3:1 insert to vector, one hour at room temperature. The 12 June round took PcpcG2-veg (242 bp) and EEBD–MF001 (274 bp); the 24 June round, logged as Insert PCR products (MoClo → STK) to pGEM-Teasy, took Csn:Lea, Csn:AmilCP, Csn:Accd, CcaSm3 and CcaR.

TestAll seven cloned; downstream assembly still failed

The intermediate worked as an intermediate and failed as a donor.

Every one of the seven gave product at the expected size on 1 and 3 July, and the constructs were confirmed again by miniprep on 9 July. The Level 1 reactions fed from them did not improve. On 13 July the ligase was changed — Promega's, then a new MEBT4 — and sixteen colonies were screened from each. Both plates are recorded in the lab's own figure deck with one word: fail.

LearnThe donor and the destination share a marker

pGEM-T Easy carries ampicillin resistance. So does every Level 1 destination vector in the kit — pSTK-1A through 1D-sfGFP, and pSTK-EXP-1 — and Level 1 transformations were being plated on ampicillin. A plate selected that way cannot distinguish a correct assembly from carried-over donor plasmid or from a religated parent. The team recorded the problem in one line: Some issue happened because the pGEM-T vector have the same antibiotic resistant gene as Level1.

This is the only sentence in the archive that states it, and the failure mode above is our reconstruction from the kit's own selection map, not something we wrote down at the time. The kit's Level 0 vector, pSTK-0-sfGFP, is chloramphenicol-selected, which is precisely the property pGEM-T Easy lacked.

RedesignUse the kit's own Level 0, on a different antibiotic

Drop pGEM-T Easy as the part carrier. Design dedicated Level 0 primers and assemble the parts into pSTK-0-sfGFP, replacing the sfGFP dropout, so donor and destination never share a marker again.

Circuit · iteration 4 of 5Level 0 in the kit's own vector, and an audit of the primers we were screening with

With the marker clash removed, are the colony screens we have been reading actually reading insert?

DesignTrue Level 0, and a check on our own screens

Two changes at once, and they are related. Parts move into pSTK-0-sfGFP under chloramphenicol, which fixes the selection problem. And because the previous cycle had spent a fortnight reading gels that turned out to mean nothing, the screening primer pairs themselves were put on trial.

BuildLevel 0 parts, from 20 July

The first Level 0 Golden Gate is dated 20 July, into STK001_pSTK-0-sfGFP at 346 ng/µL and 3,090 bp, diluted twentyfold. By 26 July the four shared regulatory parts existed as Level 0 plasmids in their own right: Pveg at 2,361 bp, EEBD at 2,392, RBS0 at 2,113 and B0015 at 2,213.

TestThe old primer pairs gave product they should not have

Some of what we had been scoring as insert was primer artefact.

The 24 July audit ran each sequencing primer pair in three orientations, with and without template. Old pairs against new. Product in a lane with no template, or with two forward primers, cannot come from an insert. New pairs were designed and the affected screens re-run — the chromophore module was rescreened the same week with the BioBrick prefix against pSTK-seq-rv at 2,111 bp, and this time gave five positives that held up.

The dropout cassette earned its keep in the same weeks, as a screen rather than as a cloning step. Clone G2 was scored by digest and the band that came back was the dropout itself: our enzyme digestion results of clone G2 showed the band size of our dropout cassette, meaning that it was not successfully cloned and that it was empty.

LearnAudit the instrument before you trust the reading

This is the cycle that we would have skipped if we had been in a hurry, and it is the one that made the later gels meaningful. A colony PCR is a measuring instrument, and nobody had calibrated it. Two of the three cloning failures on this page were diagnosed by a control, not by a redesign.

We also came out of it with an explicit worry, written down at the time: We worried about the B sub. recombination issues that might influence our optogenetic system. The four modules share RBS0 and B0015, and modules 1 and 3 shared Pveg as well. Four copies of the same terminator in one plasmid, in a naturally competent host, looked like a substrate.

RedesignBreak the repeats

Give each module its own promoter, ribosome binding site and terminator.

Circuit · iteration 5 of 5The repeats came out, the recombination premise did not survive, and Level 2 closed on a ratio

Will four transcription units sharing one ribosome binding site and one terminator assemble and then survive in B. subtilis?

DesignTwo motives, and we wrote them as alternatives

The record is honest about this and we will be too. The team's own shorthand for the redesign reads try changing promoter, RBS, terminator → strength or recombination issue. Two different reasons for the same change, and at the time we did not know which one we were acting on.

The strength motive is the one written out in full: sequencing on the chromophore module showed that our request had no signal, and the conclusion drawn was about context, not repeats — A construct that is functional in its original design may not produce the same level of expression when placed in a different backbone or combined with different genetic elements. Since adequate PCB production is essential for CcaS-mediated light sensing, insufficient expression of the Ho1 module could prevent the system from responding effectively to light.

TestThree identical failures, then a stoichiometry change, then two verified clones

The four-module Level 2 assembly closed on a ratio change, not on a sequence change.

The individual modules were fine. Level 2, which joins them, was not. On 4 August all twenty-four colonies failed and the plates were re-transformed. On 9 August all twenty-four failed and the Golden Gate was repeated. On 13 August twenty-four ACDI and twenty-four ABDI colonies failed and the Golden Gate was repeated again. Nothing about the design changed between those three rounds, which is the finding.

On 28 August the insert-to-vector ratio was swept at 1:5 and 1:7. Both returned positives. On 1 September two ACDI clones were confirmed across three internal junctions at once — pcyA F against CcaSm3 R at 375 bp, CcaS F against CcaR R at 632 bp, and CcaR F against CcaSm3 R at 661 bp. Three junctions is what distinguishes a complete circuit from a plasmid that happens to contain one module.

LearnThe premise we redesigned on turned out to be wrong

The redesign was undertaken partly because we were afraid of homologous recombination in B. subtilis. We asked, and were told we were worrying about the wrong thing. The team's own note:

Module 4: Original terminator cold D (Failure) to B0015. Because Professor Huang thinks recombination in Bacillus is not the issue, we changed our terminator back to our original plan, B0015.

So one half of the motive for cycle 5 was retired by a conversation, and module 4's terminator went back to where it started. The bkdB cold double terminator survives on disk as the artefact of a redesign we undid. The other half — expression strength in a new genetic context — stands, and the rebuilt chromophore module is the part of the redesign we kept.

The Level 2 lesson is separate and blunter. We repeated an identical failing reaction three times before changing a parameter. A fourth repetition would have taught us nothing that the first three had not.

RedesignStop building and start illuminating

With a verified four-module Level 2 plasmid in hand, the next cycle is not a cloning cycle. It is the first green-light induction run, and it is described at the bottom of this page.

The protectant and the vector that carries it4 cycles · 1 open

The light switch decides when the cells make something. This track decides what. Three of its four cycles happened on a computer, and the most useful thing any of them produced was a list of five claims we had to retract — one of which had already gone out as a DNA order.

The cargo is BoPep4, a 23-residue plant elicitor peptide from Brassica, designed to act on the Arabidopsis receptor kinases PEPR1 and PEPR2. Alongside it the same vector carries ACC deaminase (ACCD), a LEA protein, and the chromoprotein AmilCP as a visible reporter. The peptide is where the design work went, so it is the thread these cycles follow.

Protectant · iteration 1 of 4Seventeen truncations, and the shortest fragment that still binds

Which of BoPep4's twenty-three residues does the receptor actually need, and how short can we make the peptide we ask B. subtilis to secrete?

DesignA complete ladder, not a guess

BoPep4's archetype is AtPep1, whose complex with PEPR1 is solved at 2.59 Å (PDB 5GR8). Blanked on water this time. Medium read zero, one millilitre of titanium dioxide read 0.3712, two millilitres read 0.5785 — a factor of 1.56 where a factor of 2 was expected. Either that is genuine at this path length, or our transform is wrong, and two points cannot tell us which.

Two things we could not explain at all. Absolute light dropped hard between builds, from 660 to 137 lux, and we did not write down what changed in the drive or the optics. And the reference channel moved between two readings taken minutes apart, which a reference should not do.

Against the benchtop reference on a single paired reading this build gave 0.431 to the reference's 0.467, a difference of 7.7 %. That is the 8 % figure quoted elsewhere on this wiki, and it belongs to this build and to one reading.

LearnTwo points is a demonstration; a ladder is a measurement

Nothing about linearity, range or repeatability can be claimed from two concentrations, and the unexplained lux drop means we could not even compare this build to the last one on absolutes. A full dilution series was the only way forward, and it took another two months to run.

RedesignLock every position, line the mounts, seal the light path

Photometer · iteration 4 of 6Four times high on the reactor, and the blank was to blame again

Does the finished instrument agree with a benchtop spectrophotometer on the real machine?

BuildLocked, lined and sealed

Component positions fixed at their aligned defaults instead of left sliding, mounts lined with TPU so the glass is not scratched, and the light path enclosed in snap-in covers to block room light. Structural support was added to the stand so the tilt angle would not change under the weight of the tubing.

TestOne whole cycle inside a single evening

An offset, not a drift — so the shared term was the blank.

At 18:41 the instrument gave its first sensible live reading on the loop. By 19:06 we had found ourselves that it was reading about four times the benchtop value. A loose rail drifts, and this was a constant factor, so the rail was not it. A developer mode written for exactly this purpose streams both raw channels; both read cleanly, which left the blank as the only term they share. At 19:07 that became the hypothesis. The flow cuvette cannot be cleaned while it is in the loop, so the blank had been taken through a dirty window.

At 20:35 the fix was proposed — let the operator type the blank in by hand — and at 20:54 it was implemented. The reading came back 0.100 against the benchtop's 0.149. No longer four times high. Now low, and past the 10 % agreement target we had set beforehand, in the other direction.

LearnThe same failure, twice, and the second time we recognised it in twenty-five minutes

Iteration 2 blanked on medium. Iteration 4 blanked through a cuvette that cannot be cleaned in line. Both are the same class of error and the instrument has no way to detect either, which is a design gap rather than an operator gap. What did improve is the diagnosis: the first blanking failure took a rebuild to find, and the second took twenty-five minutes, because by then there was a developer mode that could rule the optics out.

RedesignStop reading one point against one point

One paired reading says nothing about linearity, range or repeatability. The next cycle is a full ladder, and a run long enough to be worth calibrating.

Photometer · iteration 5 of 6Eighteen days unattended, a fifty-nine-point calibration, and a pump that died in front of it

Does the instrument hold up over weeks on its own, and does it agree with a commercial one across the range a culture actually occupies?

DesignTwo questions the earlier cycles could not answer

Unattended endurance, and linearity. They need different experiments: a long run on real culture for the first, and a scattering standard for the second. Titanium dioxide was chosen over cells deliberately — it does not grow, it settles slowly, and it is consistent between batches, so what it measures is the instrument and not the biology.

BuildA logger, a live dashboard, and a stirred 400 mL loop

One reading every ten minutes, written to CSV, served on a dashboard over the local network. There is no alerting of any kind; it only draws. For the calibration, titanium dioxide was added a milligram at a time from 2 to 60 mg into a stirred recirculating loop, with both instruments read at every point. The benchtop reference is specified only to about OD 1 and we kept adding past it deliberately, to see how each instrument fails outside its own specification.

LearnThe instrument caught the reactor failing, and caught itself failing too

Between roughly 240 and 300 hours the scatter on the trace grew by an order of magnitude — residual SD of 0.105 and 0.171 against 0.019–0.030 everywhere else — and over the same hours the reference channel fell from 25.8 lux to 9.3, bottoming at 1.4. The first hypothesis was contamination. That was wrong. The recirculation pump had failed, and swapping it knocked the optical axis. So part of that window is the reactor failing and part of it is us, and the reason we can tell the difference is that the instrument's own reference channel flagged the window as untrustworthy while it was happening.

We would rather put it that way than the way we first wrote it, which was that the photometer detected the pump dying. It did log the window, nobody was rostered to look at the trace daily, and the fault was noticed late.

One dip we still cannot explain

A separate, earlier feature — a fall from OD 0.93 to 0.72 between 120 and 160 hours — has no attribution at all. The optical channel was healthy through it, so it is not the instrument. The run was a fixed-volume batch with no feed. Contamination was the first hypothesis and no evidence was found: the culture still smelled like B. subtilis at the end and streaking gave 168 colony morphology. Lysis followed by cryptic growth, sporulation and germination, and biofilm sloughing all remain open and unseparated.

The measurement that would have separated fouling from biology is a flush and reblank at the end of the run. It was never done, and once the rig was torn down the chance was gone.

RedesignTwo experiments, and a cheaper instrument

Titanium dioxide gives linearity, not a cell count, so the calibration has to be repeated on cells: culture drawn at several mid-run points and read on both instruments, over OD 0.05 to 1.2, with three repeats and a stated limit of detection. Plate counts should run alongside optical density, because optical density cannot separate lysis from sporulation from settling. And then the instrument needs to get cheaper.

Photometer · iteration 6 of 6 · openHow much of the optical train is actually load-bearing

Can the instrument be built for a farmer's budget by removing optics rather than by buying cheaper ones?

DesignDelete the two most expensive components and see what breaks

The intended user farms around half a hectare and has little tolerance for unit cost, and the target unit cost for the packaged reactor is still an open item. The proposal is to remove the paralleling lens and the beamsplitter and rebuild around the flow cuvette and the two sensors alone, targeting roughly NT$5,000 for the instrument.

The reason this is a cycle and not a saving is that the beamsplitter is what makes the reference channel possible, and the reference channel is the whole basis of the ratiometric design — it is why source drift divides out, and it is what flagged the fault window in cycle 5. Removing it means finding another way to reject drift, or accepting that the instrument can no longer run for eighteen days unattended. That trade is the experiment.

TestNot run

Nothing has been built and no cost has been verified.

This cycle is on record only as a plan. The stopping condition, if it runs, is the one the calibration in cycle 5 established: a residual standard deviation against the benchtop reference no worse than 0.024 OD below OD 1.2, and a reference channel stable enough to survive a run of days. If a lens-free build cannot hold both, the saving is not available.

The tracks are coupled, and two couplings did real work

These are not four independent loops. The photometer only matters because the reactor runs continuously, and the reactor is only worth running continuously because the photometer can watch it — which is why the pump failure appears in both tracks, once as a reactor fault and once as the instrument that recorded it. In the other direction, the reactor's pressure trace decided where the photometer goes in the loop. On the molecular side, the circuit track and the protectant track share the output module: the same cargo, behind a light-driven promoter in one and a constitutive promoter in the other, precisely so that a negative result in the light-driven version can be attributed.

Decisions we have not made

  • Which chassis. Every number on this page is B. subtilis 168. The protease-deficient WB800N is named once in our archive, in the plan for cycle E4, with the result field blank.
  • How the probes get in sterile. Dissolved oxygen and pH both read on the bench and neither is on the reactor. This blocks unattended operation more than any electronics problem does.
  • Whether the membrane fouls. No fouling measurement exists. The criterion is settled — watch resistance instead of pressure, and clean at twice baseline — but the flush-and-reblank that would have produced the first datum was never run.
  • What the instrument costs. The target unit cost for the packaged reactor is an open item, and cycle P6 exists to attack it.

Cycle twenty-three is the first induction run, and here is what would end it

Everything above is about whether the DNA is correct and whether the hardware can hold a culture. Neither is the project's claim. The claim is that green light sets how much protectant a culture makes, and we have not measured that once.

The next cycle is therefore not a cloning cycle and not a hardware cycle. Its inputs now exist: a sequence-verified four-module Level 2 plasmid confirmed across three junctions on 1 September; a light array that holds twenty-four cultures under six defined conditions; and an in-line photometer with a calibration good to 0.024 OD against a commercial instrument below OD 1.2.

Design

A dose–response of PcpcG2 output against green photon flux, with the AmilCP chromoprotein as the readout so the result is visible without an assay, run against a red-light and a dark control.

Build

The verified ACDI plasmid in B. subtilis, on the LED array at its six calibrated intensity tiers, four replicates each.

Test

Absorbance per unit optical density at each light level, sampled over a time course, with the dark control as the floor.

The stopping condition

A monotonic, statistically separable increase in output across at least three light levels, with the dark and red-light arms at the floor. Anything less and the cycle is not finished.

If that comes back flat, the first thing to check is not the circuit. It is whether the chassis is making phycocyanobilin at all, because without the chromophore CcaS cannot respond to anything — and the chromophore module is the one whose first build sequenced blank, and the only module we rebuilt from scratch.
