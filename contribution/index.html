Contribution

Abstract

ReLEAF hands on a parts collection of thirty-two entries in five modules, a standardized plant stress protocol, four pieces of hardware, a digital twin, a GIS workflow, bilingual lesson materials, and a culture medium made from agricultural waste.

Each one carries the reasoning that produced it and the limit that still applies. The light circuit is split into four modules so one can be rebuilt without touching the other three, and a fifth constitutive expression module carries the cargo; that fifth one is behind every bench result on this wiki. The plant protocol fixes medium, vernalization, plate angle and imaging geometry across fifteen pages. The four devices ship as printable files, dimensions and a materials list on their own pages. Four of the limits are worth naming before anyone reuses this: no part is characterised for light response, containment has no log reduction value, the photometer has never been calibrated against a known cell density, and no printed hydroponics plate has been weighed. Those parts of the work are offered as a method, not as a result.

Parts

Thirty-two entries in five modules: a green-light switch in Bacillus subtilis with a secreted plant protectant behind it.

We organized the construct into separate functional modules instead of treating it as one fixed design, so that an individual component can be modified or replaced on its own. That makes the system easier to build, to troubleshoot and to adapt. Four modules carry the light circuit; a fifth expresses the cargo constitutively, and it is that fifth one every bench result on this wiki was produced with.

Eighteen of the thirty-two entries are new this year, eight come from the SubtiToolKit and six reuse one of five distinct Registry accessions.

Module 1: PCB production endogenous heme into the chromophore the photoreceptor needs

This module supplies phycocyanobilin (PCB), the chromophore the CcaS-m3 photoreceptor needs in order to sense light. It converts endogenous heme into PCB inside the cell.

It is built as a standardized composite part. The bacterial promoter PtagC and a ribosome binding site control transcription and translation. These are followed by the ho1–pcyA fusion CDS, which encodes heme oxygenase 1 fused to phycocyanobilin:ferredoxin oxidoreductase, the two enzymes that make PCB. The transcriptional terminator L3S2P21 closes the module and gives reliable termination.

Module 2: Light sensing the green-light photoreceptor, and the RBS chosen for B. subtilis

This module carries the engineered green-light photoreceptor system that detects external light.

The constitutive promoters PlepA and PliaG give baseline transcription of the elements downstream. For translation initiation the module uses RBS0, a strong ribosome binding site optimized for B. subtilis. At its center is the ccaS-m3 CDS, derived from the cyanobacterial CcaS/CcaR system: once loaded with the PCB that module 1 supplies, CcaS-m3 detects green light and autophosphorylates, which relays the signal downstream.

Module 3: Transduction the response regulator that takes the phosphoryl group

This module passes the signal from the sensor to the gene expression system downstream.

The constitutive promoter Pveg holds expression steady and initiates translation of the response regulator. The functional component is the ccaR CDS, which receives a phosphoryl group from the activated CcaS photoreceptor and then activates transcription.

Module 4: Green-light responsive expression the protectant cassette, and the blocking device that keeps it quiet during cloning

This module is the output: it turns the green-light signal into targeted expression and secretion of the protectants.

It is controlled by the synthetic light-responsive promoter PcpcG2-veg together with EEBD-RBS0, a composite ribosome binding site carrying an Escherichia coli expression blocking device — a B0015 terminator flanked by recombination sites — which suppresses toxic protein production during the intermediate cloning steps.

Several coding sequences were evaluated in this position, among them Csn-AcdS-Histag and SamyQ-AcdS-Histag v2t1, which fuse ACC deaminase to a secretion signal peptide and a C-terminal 6×His tag. Six of the collection’s thirty-two entries, the SamyQ fusions among them, are marked on Parts as designed: a sequence exists, in most cases a synthesis plasmid with it, and no bench step after that is recorded.

Module 5: Expression the constitutive cassette behind every bench result, and the shuttle vector

The largest module, fourteen entries, holds all three protectant cargoes behind a constitutive promoter, with the shuttle vector that carried them into B. subtilis. It exists because a light-gated cassette cannot be tested until the cargo itself expresses and secretes, so the constitutive series was built first and every protein, plant and reactor result on this wiki came out of it.

For a future team this is the honest entry point: build the expression module, confirm the cargo, and only then put the light circuit in front of it.

Together the standardized modules work as a flexible toolkit that turns complex optogenetic regulation into a system where a future team can substitute its own output module. Breaking the system into separate functional units means components can be mixed, upgraded or troubleshot without redesigning the whole circuit, which lowers the barrier to building light-driven circuits in B. subtilis.

  • Who can use it. Future iGEM teams working in plant synthetic biology, microbial engineering or agricultural biotechnology.
  • Resources. All thirty-two entries with their evidence state, plus the characterization data and protocols, on Parts and the Registry entries linked from it.
What the collection does not yet carry

No part is characterised for light response: no induction curve, no dose response, no dark control. The Level 2 join gave three clones with bands at all three junctions on 19 September, junction-checked and not sequenced end to end. So the light circuit is handed on as a design that assembles, and the expression module as the one with results behind it.

Two more things to know before reusing them. Every module was assembled with the SubtiToolKit Golden Gate system in Bacillus subtilis [1], so the parts fit that framework and not a standard E. coli chassis, and the light-driven cassette uses the CcaS/CcaR green light system [2]. Every hardware track ran on B. subtilis 168, wild type or secreting ACC deaminase constitutively, so none of those numbers is the light switch doing anything. Which modules assembled, which failed, and what the sequencing said are on Parts and Engineering.

Plant protocol

A standardized workflow for evaluating plant responses under controlled growth and stress conditions, so that a protectant result can be attributed to the protectant.

Arabidopsis thaliana is one of our laboratory plant models. The protocol fixes temperature, photoperiod, nutrient conditions, growth medium, plant age and developmental stage, and hydroponic conditions, and adds controlled stress procedures for salinity, long-term heat and short-term heat shock. Each experiment records stress intensity, duration, plant age and treatment conditions, so it can be run again.

The readouts are fixed too: root length, fresh weight, chlorophyll content, MDA, anthocyanin, total protein and relative gene expression. Root length is traced by hand in ImageJ from the baseline drawn on the vertical agar plates. Leaf area stays qualitative, because leaves overlap and obstruct the measurement.

  • Why it matters. Allows future teams to reproduce our plant experiments and compare biological treatments under consistent conditions.
  • What it carried. Eleven agar and two soil sets under salinity, and three agar and two soil sets under five days of 37 °C heat, across trehalose, the CH Bio peptide, ACC deaminase, LEA14 and BoPEP4.
  • Resources. The protocol as a PDF below; the runs made with it on Plants, and the numbers they returned on Results.

What the protocol leaves open

Three doses are not set. The ACCD concentration, the LEA14 dose and the BoPEP4 working concentration are optimization parameters in our records, not validated concentrations, so the protocol gives the delivery method and the control design and leaves the dose to be established on your own material.

4. Treatment timing pre, co and post, the comparison the protocol is built around

The same protocol can be used to investigate when protectants should be released.

5. Monitor plant response what to photograph, what to record, and the endpoint measurements

Observe plants for approximately 10 days. Take standardized photographs daily using the same black background, the same lighting, the same plate orientation, and the same camera distance with quality control.

Daily: root growth, leaf area or plant size, visible phenotype, survival.

Data collection: fresh weight, chlorophyll content, final root length.

For protectant testing groups: MDA, total protein, enzymatic activity testing.

Experimental logic. Salt only determines the effect of salinity stress. Protectant only determines whether the protectant itself affects plant growth. Salt plus protectant determines whether the protectant rescues plants from salinity stress. Pretreatment against cotreatment against post-treatment determines when protectant delivery is most effective.

Read the protocol beside Plants, which says what the runs could and could not settle: the salt ladder is calibrated, from 74.95 to 1.36 mm of root over 0 to 150 mM NaCl; the CH Bio peptide gave the clearest rescue at 100 mM; trehalose rescued in two sets and no dose held across all six; and our own three candidates have not shown an effect yet. Sets 1 to 3 have no chlorophyll data, because the protocol was not yet standardized when they ran. That is the gap this contribution exists to close.

Perfusion bioreactor

A perfusion reactor that keeps engineered B. subtilis in the loop and lets secreted protectant out through a hollow-fiber membrane.

Cultivation results are hard to compare because batch systems change throughout a run: nutrients deplete, waste accumulates, and cell density and product concentration move together, with no way to set one without moving the other.

Researching bioreactors elsewhere, we never found a group using a hollow-fiber membrane to separate bacteria from a produced protein, so we developed our own continuous-flow system to separate the protectant from the cells that make it. That search showed us a broader problem: a system that harvests a secreted product is only reproducible if the hydraulic conditions it runs at are characterized and reported.

Our first prototypes tested flow rate alone, or plain B. subtilis growth. We then iterated toward a hollow-fiber membrane system that retains cells in the loop while secreted protectant passes into the permeate, with an operating point established by hydraulic characterization and not by trial and error.

What it standardizes
  • cell retention and product separation
  • the flow operating point, written down instead of left implicit: 94 mL/min on the culture loop with the reservoir stirred at 200 rpm, and the module’s own pressure drop checked against theory at 263 mL/min
  • the shear environment the cells experience
  • permeate collection and sampling points
  • the hollow-fiber specification: PES, 0.2 µm pore rating, cells on the lumen side in crossflow
  • Why it matters. Reporting a characterized operating point, where most write-ups say only “continuous culture”, means a result can be attributed to the biology and not to an undocumented vessel. Runs become comparable within one team, and a future team starts from a defined configuration.
  • Who can use it. Teams working on plant and microbe systems, environmental biotechnology, bioreactors, or deployment of engineered microbes.
  • Resources. The ten specifications, the materials list and the culture medium on Hardware: Bioreactor; the hydraulic fit and the shear calculation on Biomanufacturing; the six runs and their traces on Digital Twin.
  • What it cost. NT$16,450 in parts, or NT$30,450 with the reference spectrophotometer that the cost sheet carries beside them.

One number to settle before you copy the module. Our own records give it as both 150 cm² with eight fibers, which is the vendor sheet, and 200 cm² with eleven, which is what the 16 August bench check agrees with; Biomanufacturing works through the arithmetic and stays with eleven. Measure your own cartridge; do not take either figure from us.

Membrane containment, and what it has not yet shown the same membrane used as a physical barrier

The membrane that separates product from cells is also the containment barrier between engineered B. subtilis and the plant environment outside. For reuse the specification is a PES hollow-fiber module rated at 0.2 µm, run in crossflow with the cells on the lumen side.

The hollow fiber membrane physically blocks bacteria from leaving the lumen side, since the pore size of the membrane (0.2µm) is much smaller than the cell size of B. subtilis (0.25 to 1.0µm in diameter). To validate this theory, a simple test was conducted. When the bioreactor was running, we collected medium from the lumen and shell sides and plated them. After 24 h at 37 °C, the samples were compared with each other.

What this contribution does not include

A log reduction value. Two platings are on record, the lumen-against-shell comparison above and 2 mL of shell-side medium on 6 June, and neither grew colonies; but neither states a replicate count, the June incubation conditions are not documented, and there is no detection limit. The barrier is tested at single time points and never against a deliberately induced leak. Reuse the geometry and the plating method, not a retention figure, and plate the shell side at every sampling point of your own run.

Fouling has one trend behind it: across 42 h on 14 to 16 October the pressure the module needed beyond clean water at the same flow rose from 2.0 to 3.8 kPa, nothing had to be cleaned, and no replacement interval follows from a single run. A droplet sensor and the transmembrane pressure line are both fitted to flag a leak, and neither has been tested.

What has not been measured is how much protectant crosses: there is no shell-side concentration and no transfer rate, and the two records read the faint Western blot band differently, so membrane passage is still unverified. The containment claim as a safety argument is on Safety; the plating evidence is on Results.

In-line optical density photometer

A low-cost in-line photometer that reads OD600 inside the loop, without pulling a sample.

Growth data is hard to compare because OD600 is conventionally measured by pulling a grab sample and reading it in a benchtop spectrophotometer: a manual, low-frequency, contamination-prone process that interrupts the run it is meant to describe. Existing tools require sampling and medium extraction, and we wanted to remove the human error that derails data accuracy. The broader problem is that continuous cultivation is a continuous process and the measurement of it is almost always discontinuous.

Our in-line photometer monitors OD600 without removing sample from the loop. The device integrates a 600 nm source, a paralleling lens for a parallel light path, a 90:10 beam splitter that sends a tenth of the beam to a reference channel, two GY-302 lux sensors on one I²C bus and a 2 mm flow cuvette in the bioreactor loop. It runs with no computer attached and reports OD600 from the sample-to-reference ratio about every ten minutes. Five build cycles took it from a bench light path to the instrument that ran for 400 hours, and a second, slimmer design followed it in October.

What it standardizes
  • measurement position: in-line, non-destructive, no sample removal
  • sampling cadence and averaging, a 16-reading burst with the first two discarded, then median and MAD
  • outlier handling, a MAD filter at median ± 3·MAD, with the rejection rate shown in red as an alarm above 50%
  • two acceptance gates added in October: readings whose two estimates differ by more than 0.03 OD are flagged, and a jump beyond 0.05 OD or 5% is held back until three consecutive readings agree
  • optical path geometry, fixed across runs, and the 2 mm cuvette scaled ×5 to a standard 10 mm path
  • automated logging at a fixed interval, every 10 minutes
  • Why it matters. Measuring in-line removes the handling step that introduces most of the variance in manual OD readings, and fixing the optical geometry makes readings comparable across runs and across time. Against a BioDrop the raw, uncorrected output agrees to a median of 3.8%, so the instrument can be rebuilt and used without fitting a calibration equation to a reference machine.
  • Problem addressed. Laboratory OD instruments such as the BioDrop cost around US$15,000 and generally require manual sampling. The parts for ours come to about US$360.
  • Who can use it. Teams working with bioreactors, continuous culture, fermentation, microbial growth monitoring and closed-loop biological systems.
  • Resources. Eight printable meshes, the materials list, the print settings, the optical drawings and the five build cycles on Hardware: Photometer; the raw readings from the 400-hour run and the October filter test are linked from the same page.
Validation, and the limits that came with it what the long run and the calibration actually established
  • A continuous run of more than 400 h on B. subtilis 168 at 22 °C, 2,132 points traced over the first 336 h, with no sample withdrawn.
  • Two turbidity series against a BioDrop reference: titanium dioxide on 23 August, and silica on 10 October with all three instruments.
  • A documented account of reference-channel drift, of the noise floor, and of the two readings the filter missed.

On the titanium dioxide series each instrument tracks the mass added at R² above 0.999, and the raw readings sit within a median 3.8% of the BioDrop with no correction applied. On the silica series the two regress against each other at a slope of 1.001 with a constant offset of about 0.12 OD, ours reading lower at every point. On that evidence we treat calibration as unnecessary rather than pending.

The limit that is still open

Both series are scattering standards, not cells. The working limit of OD600 2.5 and the scale factor between the two instrument bodies come from a single silica run, and the same series has never been run on B. subtilis at known densities, so no reading here is a cell count. Reuse the optical design, the filter and the drift log; establish the cell relationship on your own culture. Note also that the longest run’s optical channel became unusable at 243 h, when the recirculation pump failed and was replaced the same evening.

The dashboard, the fault model that flagged this instrument 26.7 h before it failed, and the gate that turned away 31 of 1,083 reading windows are on Digital Twin.

Light plate apparatus

A dual-wavelength LED array that lights twenty-four cultures at a known intensity, with every LED measured before it drove anything.

Optogenetic characterization is hard to compare because the light dose a culture receives depends on LED output, distance, vessel geometry and crosstalk between neighbouring wells — factors that vary between setups and are seldom reported in enough detail to reproduce. In our research we found existing light plate hardware costly enough to be a barrier for anyone trying to get trustworthy light intensity at every position on a plate.

We built the array to map protectant expression against green and red light intensity through the CcaS/CcaR system. It is a four-by-six holder of twenty-four cultures, lit at about 535 nm or about 670 nm, with an Arduino driving three MOSFET channels by PWM. Forty LEDs were measured one at a time and matched in fours, which brought each group within 0.4 to 1.8% of itself, from spreads of 26% in green and 22% in red before sorting.

Matching alone did not do the job we expected. Even after sorting, the groups sat too close together to form three separate intensity tiers, so the tiers are set by the PWM duty cycle instead and the matching serves to make the four cultures in a group comparable to each other. That is the finding worth passing on: sort the LEDs for replicate consistency, set the dose in firmware.

What it standardizes
  • light intensity per well, with LEDs matched in fours to within 0.4 to 1.8%
  • six conditions, two wavelengths by three intensity tiers, four replicate cultures each
  • illumination geometry and well-to-well spacing, a 4 × 6 grid on an 18 mm pitch
  • programmable dose and exposure duration by PWM duty cycle
  • fixed-dose delivery, for characterization and not feedback control
  • Why it matters. Measuring every LED instead of assuming nominal equivalence means the intensity axis of a dose–response curve reflects what the cultures actually received, and the matching data is published so a later team can see how far from nominal real LEDs are.
  • Who can use it. Teams running optogenetic characterization, light-induced expression systems or any plate assay where dose per well has to be known.
  • Resources. Six printable meshes in the instrument’s own coordinates, the per-LED measurements, the printed-parts table and the build cycles on Hardware: LPA.
What to check before you copy the layout

Twenty-four cultures across six conditions means one wavelength to a well: in the build as wired a well receives green or red, never both, and a protocol that needs both colours in one well needs a second LED per hole or a two-pass design. Readings are in lux, which is weighted for human vision, so green and red values are not equivalent biological doses; photon flux is what a dose–response axis needs. The overnight run on a shaker failed when a post snapped and the white tube holder leaked light between wells; the rebuild with four screws and a black holder has not been re-run. No end-to-end culture run under the array is on record yet, so there is no dose–response data to inherit — only a characterized light source to generate it with.

Laboratory hydroponics platform

A 3D-printable floating plate that holds thirteen Arabidopsis seedlings at one depth, assembled without adhesives or fasteners.

Plant experiments are hard to compare because small differences in spacing, nutrient exposure, root position or container geometry introduce variation on their own.

Visiting a laboratory at National Taiwan University, we saw older hydroponic setups stored away and no longer in use. A hydroponic system that exists is not necessarily one another lab can rebuild: without standardized dimensions and clear instructions it stays a one-off.

The first prototype was a plate held at a set height and corrected by hand. The second floated, on corner poles carrying trim washers that were later removed. The third is the one we publish: a smaller footprint, clip-in handles, no ballast, sixteen printed pieces, and thirteen bores in rows of four, five and four on a 21.59 mm pitch. Sealed air of 42.27 cm³ across its two pontoons puts the drawn freeboard at 9.2 mm, and every 3.5 g added sinks it about a millimetre.

The platform standardizes plant spacing, nutrient exposure, root positioning and experimental geometry. On the plant side it is now used inside a black light-blocking box of 14.7 by 11.5 by 6.2 cm, which is what the salinity runs on Plants were grown in.

  • Why it matters. Standardizing the physical growth environment removes variation that has nothing to do with the treatment. Experiments become easier to reproduce within one team, and other laboratories can compare results under consistent conditions.
  • Who can use it. Research laboratories, school laboratories, plant synthetic biology teams and future iGEM teams.
  • Resources. One mesh per printed part, the dimensioned geometry, the parts table and the print settings on Hardware: Hydroponics; the growth runs it carried on Plants.
The one measurement missing

The load line is computed, not weighed. No printed plate has been on a balance and no run has had its freeboard measured, so the 9.2 mm figure and the 3.5 g per millimetre sensitivity are geometry, not measurements. The print settings are recorded as 15% gyroid infill with no supports, and layer height, wall count and orientation are not; no cost is recorded. Weigh your first print.

Software and maps

Two digital contributions that work together: software that keeps a live picture of the reactor, and the spatial analysis that tells it where protection is needed.

Digital twin

The ReLEAF reactor is meant to stand near farmland, where nobody can constantly sample or check the culture. Our software therefore has to do what an operator at the bench would do: watch reactor conditions, judge whether sensor data can be trusted, predict how the culture will grow, and decide when to make protectant. A digital twin maintains a dynamic representation of the physical system, with information passing continuously in both directions, so the virtual copy reflects real-time changes and can be used to hold the physical system at the condition it should be in.

It holds what the field needs beside what the reactor can deliver. The reactor side supplies biomass, growth, pressure, flow, membrane state and sensor reliability; the regional side supplies crop-stress risk from the spatial analysis below. Directly measured, operator-commanded, calculated and planned values are kept separate, so every number on screen has a clear source. Those inputs are what the system uses to assess whether the culture is ready and whether protectant production should be started.

Six reactor runs are on record, from the first prototype in June to 41.8 continuous hours on 14 to 16 October. The strongest piece of the handover is a forecast we registered before that run: on 1 October we published a growth rate of 0.2986 h⁻¹, with a 90% interval of 0.2705 to 0.3300, fitted on light-plate data. The October run returned 0.286 ± 0.010 h⁻¹, inside the interval. A fault model trained on the first 120 h of the 434-hour run flagged the failing photometer 26.7 h early, at a 6.1% false-alarm rate, and the short-horizon filter beats persistence by 41% at two hours.

  • Why it matters. The bioreactor and the field are treated as one system, so a production decision rests both on when protection may be needed and on whether the biological system can respond in time. Registering the forecast before the run is what makes the model’s agreement with it worth anything.
  • Who can use it. Teams building remote or semi-automated bioprocesses, and anyone who has to decide from sensor data whether a culture is fit to run.
  • Resources. The interface, the six run records and the scored forecast are on Digital Twin; both programs are released under the MIT licence, with the source beside it in this wiki’s own repository under software/rig-app/.
How far the automation actually goes

The models have so far run on logged data after each run, and a person still makes every decision. Green light is tested but not integrated into the reactor, so the twin cannot switch it on; the field leaf sensor has only been read on the bench, never on a plant; and above an optical density of about 2.7 the reactor is blind until someone draws a sample by hand. Weather and blast-risk inputs belong to the next version of the twin, which is designed and not tested. Working toward automated monitoring is how ReLEAF would reduce the need for constant supervision and specialized knowledge; this version is the instrumentation and the judgement, not the autonomy.

Spatial analysis

Plant stress is not spread evenly. Temperature, salinity, rainfall and agricultural productivity vary across Taiwan, so each farming region carries its own combination of risk. We used GIS to connect environmental conditions with agricultural outcomes and to see where climate-related stress overlaps with important crop-producing regions.

For ReLEAF this matters because the system is designed to answer local conditions. Treating every farm alike would waste that, and spatial data instead shows where heat stress is more severe, where salinity risk is higher, which agricultural regions may be more vulnerable, and where localized deployment could have the greatest relevance.

In our analysis of Taiwan we compared climate variables with regional rice-yield data to explore how environmental conditions relate to agricultural productivity. The fertilizer and agrochemical footprint was built the same way, aggregating 2,792,536 individual land parcels by township into 346 demand nodes. It moved us from looking at individual farms to reading broader geographic patterns.

GIS can reveal spatial patterns, but interpreting large environmental datasets is still difficult for a non-specialist. We therefore wrote a broad, reusable prompt: a structural guideline an LLM follows to turn raw data with inconsistent coordinates, software and formatting into GeoJSON, CSV or shapefiles that QGIS can read. Its rules are what make it safe to hand on — use only the data provided, never geocode or invent a coordinate, ask when the reference system is unknown, flag empty, ambiguous or unusable values, show the calculation, and state the reference system in the output. The LLM does not do the analysis; it organizes the data the user already has, so the user is not left doing the checking and reorganization by hand.

  • Why it matters. This gives a future team a way to connect a synthetic biology or engineering project with the place where it may eventually be used. Besides asking whether a solution works, a team can ask where the problem is most relevant and how environmental conditions differ between regions. The workflow can be adapted by teams working in agriculture, environmental biotechnology, climate resilience, public health or any other location-dependent application.
  • Resources. A beginner’s QGIS guide covering raster against vector data and the three import formats, the full text of the prompt with its rules, the comparison against a plain-language prompt, sixteen maps with their data sources, and the farm calculator, all on Geospatial Analysis.
  • What is still placeholder. The soil classification behind the ownership model, which needs parcel-resolved survey data before the model’s township economics can be trusted.

Education

Three lesson plans, three bilingual slide decks, two card games, two online games and a plant stress simulator, free to download and reteach.

For our team, education is not a one-time lesson taught in an hour or two. It is a journey students carry forward and apply to other parts of their lives. Our program helps students understand plant stress, climate change, sustainability and synthetic biology through hands-on activities and interactive learning. One of our main goals throughout was to build an education that is itself sustainable.

We compiled an accessible website holding lesson plans, presentations, activities and learning materials from this year and from the past two years. The three slide decks carry both English and Traditional Chinese on the slide, so they suit different learning environments and reach a wider range of students. Because they work without our team being physically present, they can go on benefiting students, teachers and future iGEM teams.

Our lessons were also developed through interactive feedback loops and structured student debates. Post-activity survey forms guided how we adapted each lesson, and bioethics debates showed us the range of student perspectives on synthetic biology. The elementary lesson went through four trials on three dates before we published it, and the junior high and high school lessons have each been taught once. We are handing over both the teaching materials and the process that produced them, so a future team can adapt them to build environmental awareness in its own community.

  • Why it matters. Materials that can be taught again without us are what makes an education program outlast one season of workshops.
  • Who can use it. Teachers, school clubs, community educators and future iGEM teams.
  • What the surveys said. Every question scored at least 80% for accuracy except DNA base pairing, which 30% of students answered correctly from a 0% baseline; 75 of 99 elementary students named the card game as their favourite part; all three teachers surveyed said they would take part again.
  • Resources. The lesson plans, slide decks, card game and playable games on Education, and the standing site, the outreach map and the past three years of projects on our education website.

Two things to know before reteaching them. The junior high lesson plan is in Chinese only, while the three slide decks are bilingual, and of the two card games only the base-pairing one is published as a file. Worksheets were used in the room and are not among the downloads.

Waste media

Hay infusion, okara extract and sugarcane extract tested as culture media for B. subtilis, measured against LB.

To make ReLEAF practical on a real farm, we asked whether agricultural waste could replace the culture medium. In the laboratory B. subtilis is usually grown in chemical media such as LB [7], which works well on a bench but is not something a farmer can easily obtain. Without access to culture medium, cultivating the bacteria and producing protectant stops being feasible, and the technology stops being adoptable. Agricultural byproducts, by contrast, are already generated on site and may carry useful carbon and nitrogen.

We tested three agricultural waste sources: hay infusion, okara extract and sugarcane extract. Hay provides carbohydrates and structural polysaccharides, okara is rich in protein [4], and sugarcane bagasse contains cellulose and hemicellulose that can serve as carbon sources after processing [3]. We also tested mixtures of the three extracts, to see whether combining nutrient sources would support growth better than any one of them.

After 18 hours of incubation at 37 °C, bacterial density was measured at OD600. The 1:1:2 mixture of hay infusion, okara extract and sugarcane extract gave the highest average OD600 at 0.720, followed by the 1:2:1 mixture at 0.708. LB medium reached 0.573 and hay infusion alone 0.555, while okara extract alone was the weakest at 0.333. Combining different agricultural waste extracts appears to give a more balanced nutrient source than any single extract.

What this contribution does not yet include

The written preparation protocol is not published here yet, and the comparison rests on two cultures per medium at a single 18-hour endpoint, with no growth curve behind it.

At least 1,300 million tons of agricultural waste is produced every year, and the figure is likely to rise. Handling it through landfill and incineration contributes about 3% of global greenhouse gas emissions and creates various toxic pollutants [5]. For hay in particular, the humid weather in Taiwan damages its quality and encourages mold, wasting nearly 5,000 tonnes each season [6]. Repurposing these wastes as culture media turns a local byproduct into a resource, and supports SDG 12: Responsible Consumption and Production.

What it would take on a real farm who prepares the medium, and what it costs

This is an initial framework for using agricultural byproducts as media for B. subtilis. Preparation still takes processing, so deployment would more realistically run through farmer cooperatives or agricultural research stations than through individual farmers. That lifts the technical burden off the farmer but adds costs in transport, processing and distribution, so future work should cost the whole production chain and look at what subsidies or government programs would keep it affordable.

Further research will test additional agricultural wastes, improve pretreatment methods, and monitor OD600 over time so that growth curves can be compared instead of a single endpoint. That is what would show whether a waste-based medium can become a practical, lower-cost input for localized bioproduction.

  • Who can use it. Teams who need to culture a chassis outside a laboratory supply chain, and anyone working on low-cost or decentralized bioproduction.

Recommendations for future teams

The designs are only half of what we can hand over. The other half is what the engineering process taught us, including the parts that went wrong.

What worked

Habits that paid for themselves, and that cost almost nothing to start.

  • Stirring the reservoir. Adding a 200 rpm magnetic stir bar was one of the three changes (with the sampling point and the temperature) between a reactor where the culture did not grow and one that reached plateau faster than a shake flask. If you build a perfusion loop, stir the reservoir from the first run.
  • Splitting the construct into four modules, so that a failure could be located and rebuilt without touching the rest.
  • Fixing the imaging geometry before the first experiment: same background, same lighting, same plate orientation, same camera distance. It costs an afternoon and it makes every later photograph comparable.
  • Calibrating an optical instrument against a nonliving turbidity standard first. It separates instrument behaviour from biology, and it is cheap.
  • Registering a forecast before the run that tests it. The October growth rate landed inside the interval we had published three weeks earlier, which is worth more than the same number produced afterwards.
  • Taking the prototype to a farmers' market and a public forum while it was still ugly. The questions we got there changed the design.

What didn't

Six failures with a cause we can name. Each one cost a week or a dataset.

  • The first reactor build leaked at the peristaltic pump and the trial had to be stopped early. Use biocompatible tubing that seals well at the pump head, not standard tubing.
  • Running the loop without stirring: the culture in the fiber lumen did not grow at 22 °C while a control at the same temperature did.
  • Trehalose as a reference protectant. Six experiment sets, rescue in two of them, and no dose that held across all six; two trials of one set produced opposing trends on a single plate per condition. Pick a reference with a published dose in your assay, and more than one plate per condition, before you build experiments on it.
  • Spraying plants with a culture nobody had blotted. The Western blot on the sprayed batch reads negative, which left a week of plant work with nothing behind it.
  • Deciding how to normalise chlorophyll after the fact. Per fresh weight, a severely stressed plant can look richer in chlorophyll because it has less biomass, so the two normalisations rank the same samples differently.
  • Leaving the reagent field blank on the hydroponic box sheets. Two complete datasets exist and nobody can now say what was added.

What we would change

What we would do differently on day one of another project.

  • Measure the light before building experiments on it: an intensity map across every well position, the duty cycles written down, and one induction curve, green against dark, before anything downstream assumes the switch works.
  • One canonical spreadsheet per assay, with the formula written once and the raw readings never retyped.
  • Record the run conditions in the same file as the data: medium, inoculum, which compartment the optics sat on, incubation time and temperature.
  • Plate the shell side at every sampling point, not once per project, so containment stops resting on a single plate.
  • Log transmembrane pressure continuously across a whole run instead of taking spot readings at each pump setting, and check the sensor's own scaling before trusting a single trace.
  • Decide the normalisation for chlorophyll (per fresh weight or per plant) before the first extract, because the two rank the same samples in opposite orders.
