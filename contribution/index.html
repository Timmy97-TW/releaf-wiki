Contribution

Abstract

ReLEAF hands on a four-module genetic design, a standardized plant stress protocol, four pieces of hardware, a digital twin, a GIS workflow, bilingual lesson materials, and a culture medium made from agricultural waste.

Each one carries the reasoning that produced it and the limit that still applies. The genetic system is split into four modules so one can be rebuilt without touching the other three. The plant protocol fixes medium, vernalization, plate angle and imaging geometry across fifteen pages. The bioreactor, the photometer, the light plate and the hydroponics platform each ship as CAD files, a bill of materials and an assembly guide on their own pages. Two of them carry no number yet: containment has no log reduction value and the photometer has no biomass calibration, so both are offered as a method and not as a result.

Our contributions at a glance

ReLEAF was designed as a project for our own agricultural application and as a collection of tools, protocols, hardware designs and engineering lessons that future teams can reuse and improve.

They span synthetic biology, plant experimentation, hardware, measurement, data analysis and education. For each one we give both the finished resource and the reasoning behind how it was developed.

1. Parts

A four-module genetic system for protectant production in engineered B. subtilis, built so that one module can be swapped without redesigning the circuit.

2. Plant experiment protocol

A standardized workflow for evaluating plant responses under controlled growth and stress conditions, written out step by step and downloadable.

3. Hardware

Four devices with CAD files, dimensions and assembly guides: a continuous-flow bioreactor, an in-line photometer, a programmable light plate and a hydroponics platform.

4. Digital twin

Software that holds regional crop stress and local reactor measurements in one view, and decides from both whether protectant production should start.

5. Geographic information systems

A field-scale stress picture of Taiwanese farmland, and a prompt workflow that turns inconsistent environmental datasets into files QGIS can read.

6. Education

Lesson plans, slide decks, worksheets and games in English and Traditional Chinese, taught and revised across three age bands before publication.

7. Agricultural waste media

Hay infusion, okara and sugarcane extract tested as culture media for B. subtilis, with the preparation method and the growth comparison against LB.

Parts

A four-module genetic system supporting protectant production in engineered Bacillus subtilis.

We organized the construct into separate functional modules instead of treating it as one fixed design, so that an individual component can be modified or replaced on its own. That makes the system easier to build, to troubleshoot and to adapt.

Module 1: PCB production endogenous heme into the chromophore the photoreceptor needs

This module supplies phycocyanobilin (PCB), the chromophore the CcaS-m3 photoreceptor needs in order to sense light. It converts endogenous heme into PCB inside the cell.

It is built as a standardized composite part. The bacterial promoter PtagC and a ribosome binding site control transcription and translation. These are followed by the ho1–pcyA fusion CDS, which encodes heme oxygenase 1 fused to phycocyanobilin:ferredoxin oxidoreductase, the two enzymes that make PCB. The transcriptional terminator L3S2P21 closes the module and gives reliable termination.

Module 2: Light sensing the green and red photoreceptor, and the RBS chosen for B. subtilis

This module carries the engineered green-light and red-light photoreceptor system that detects external light.

The constitutive promoters PlepA and PliaG give baseline transcription of the elements downstream. For translation initiation the module uses RBS0, a strong ribosome binding site optimized for B. subtilis. At its center is the ccaS-m3 CDS, derived from the cyanobacterial CcaS/CcaR system: once loaded with the PCB that module 1 supplies, CcaS-m3 detects green light and autophosphorylates, which relays the signal downstream.

Module 3: Transcriptional relay the response regulator that takes the phosphoryl group

This module passes the signal from the sensor to the gene expression system downstream.

The constitutive promoter Pveg holds expression steady and initiates translation of the response regulator. The functional component is the ccaR CDS, which receives a phosphoryl group from the activated CcaS photoreceptor and then activates transcription.

Module 4: Green-light responsive output the protectant cassette, and the blocking device that keeps it quiet during cloning

This module is the output: it turns the green-light signal into targeted expression and secretion of the protectants.

It is controlled by the synthetic light-responsive promoter PcpcG2-veg together with EEBD-RBS0, a composite ribosome binding site carrying an Escherichia coli expression blocking device — a B0015 terminator flanked by recombination sites — which suppresses toxic protein production during the intermediate cloning steps.

Several coding sequences were evaluated in this position, among them Csn-AcdS-Histag and SamyQ-AcdS-Histag v2t1, which fuse ACC deaminase to a secretion signal peptide and a C-terminal 6×His tag.

Together the four standardized modules work as a flexible toolkit that turns complex optogenetic regulation into a system where a future team can substitute its own output module. Breaking the system into separate functional units means components can be mixed, upgraded or troubleshot without redesigning the whole circuit, which lowers the barrier to building light-driven circuits in B. subtilis.

  • Who can use it. Future iGEM teams working in plant synthetic biology, microbial engineering or agricultural biotechnology.
  • Resources. Part documentation, characterization data and protocols, on Parts and the Registry entries linked from it.

Three things to know before reusing them. Every module was assembled with the SubtiToolKit Golden Gate system in Bacillus subtilis [1], so the parts fit that framework and not a standard E. coli chassis. The light-driven cassette uses the CcaS/CcaR green light system [2], and we have no induction curve for it yet, so it is a design and not a characterised switch. Every bench result on this wiki was produced with the constitutive ACC deaminase cassette. Which modules assembled, which failed, and what the sequencing said are on Parts and Engineering.

Standardized plant experiment protocol

A standardized workflow for evaluating plant responses under controlled growth and stress conditions.

  • Why it matters. Allows future teams to reproduce our plant experiments and compare biological treatments under consistent conditions.

Arabidopsis thaliana is one of our laboratory plant models. The protocol fixes temperature, photoperiod, nutrient conditions, growth medium, plant age and developmental stage, and hydroponic conditions, and adds controlled stress procedures for salinity, long-term heat and short-term heat shock. Each experiment records stress intensity, duration, plant age and treatment conditions, so it can be run again.

The readouts are fixed too: root length, fresh weight, chlorophyll content, MDA and total protein. Root length is traced in ImageJ from the baseline drawn on the vertical agar plates.

5. Monitor plant response what to photograph, what to record, and the endpoint measurements

Observe plants for approximately 10 days. Take standardized photographs daily using the same black background, the same lighting, the same plate orientation, and the same camera distance with quality control.

Daily: root growth, leaf area or plant size, visible phenotype, survival.

Data collection: fresh weight, chlorophyll content, final root length.

For protectant testing groups: MDA, total protein, enzymatic activity testing.

Experimental logic. Salt only determines the effect of salinity stress. Protectant only determines whether the protectant itself affects plant growth. Salt plus protectant determines whether the protectant rescues plants from salinity stress. Pretreatment against cotreatment against post-treatment determines when protectant delivery is most effective.

Hardware

Four devices, each built because the equivalent was unavailable, unaffordable or undocumented. Every one of them ships as CAD files, dimensions and an assembly guide on its own hardware page, so a later team can rebuild it without redesigning it.

Continuous-flow bioreactor

A perfusion reactor that keeps engineered B. subtilis in the loop and lets secreted protectant out through a hollow-fiber membrane.

Cultivation results are hard to compare because batch systems change throughout a run: nutrients deplete, waste accumulates, and cell density and product concentration move together, with no way to set one without moving the other.

Researching bioreactors elsewhere, we never found a group using a hollow-fiber membrane to separate bacteria from a produced protein, so we developed our own continuous-flow system to separate the protectant from the cells that make it. That search showed us a broader problem: a system that harvests a secreted product is only reproducible if the hydraulic conditions it runs at are characterized and reported.

Our first prototypes tested flow rate alone, or plain B. subtilis growth. We then iterated toward a hollow-fiber membrane system that retains cells in the loop while secreted protectant passes into the permeate, with an operating point established by hydraulic characterization and not by trial and error.

The platform standardizes:

  • cell retention and product separation
  • the flow operating point, 263 mL/min, fixed by a hydraulic fit rather than chosen by hand
  • the shear environment the cells experience
  • permeate collection and sampling points
  • the cleaning and preparation procedure between runs
  • Why it matters. Reporting a characterized operating point, where most write-ups say only “continuous culture”, means a result can be attributed to the biology and not to an undocumented vessel. Runs become comparable within one team, and a future team starts from a defined configuration.
  • Who can use it. Teams working on plant and microbe systems, environmental biotechnology, bioreactors, or deployment of engineered microbes.
  • Resources. CAD files, bill of materials and assembly guide on Hardware: Bioreactor; the hydraulic fit and the shear calculation on Biomanufacturing.
Membrane containment, and what it has not yet shown the same membrane used as a physical barrier

The membrane that separates product from cells is also the containment barrier between engineered B. subtilis and the plant environment outside. For reuse the specification is a PES hollow-fiber module rated at 0.2 µm, run in crossflow with the cells on the lumen side.

The hollow fiber membrane physically blocks bacteria from leaving the lumen side, since the pore size of the membrane (0.2µm) is much smaller than the cell size of B. subtilis (0.25 to 1.0µm in diameter). To validate this theory, a simple test was conducted. When the bioreactor was running, we collected medium from the lumen and shell sides and plated them. After 24 h at 37 °C, the samples were compared with each other.

What this contribution does not include

A log reduction value. The plating above is a single test on a single run, with the incubation time and temperature recorded but no replicate and no detection limit. Reuse the geometry and the plating method, not a retention figure, and plate the shell side at every sampling point of your own run. Transmembrane pressure is likewise not quoted as an operating value: the logged pressures need rescaling before any number from them is published.

The containment claim as a safety argument is on Safety; the plating evidence is on Results.

In-line optical density photometer

A low-cost in-line photometer that reads OD600 inside the loop, without pulling a sample.

Growth data is hard to compare because OD600 is conventionally measured by pulling a grab sample and reading it in a benchtop spectrophotometer: a manual, low-frequency, contamination-prone process that interrupts the run it is meant to describe. Existing tools require sampling and medium extraction, and we wanted to remove the human error that derails data accuracy. The broader problem is that continuous cultivation is a continuous process and the measurement of it is almost always discontinuous.

Our in-line photometer monitors OD600 without removing sample from the loop. The device integrates a 600 nm source, a collimating lens for a parallel light path, a beam splitter for reference correction, and a flow cuvette in the bioreactor loop. Burst sampling and outlier rejection are handled in firmware.

The platform standardizes:

  • measurement position: in-line, non-destructive, no sample removal
  • sampling cadence and averaging, a 16-sample burst with 2 warm-up discards
  • outlier handling, a MAD filter at median ± 3·MAD, with a retry above 50% rejection
  • optical path geometry, fixed across runs
  • automated logging at a fixed interval, every 10 minutes
  • Why it matters. Measuring in-line removes the handling step that introduces most of the variance in manual OD readings, and fixing the optical geometry makes readings comparable across runs and across time. Validation against a BioDrop reference showed that the raw, uncalibrated output already agrees with the reference and is at least as linear against a mass-based ground truth, so the device can be rebuilt and used without an instrument-specific calibration step.
  • Problem addressed. Laboratory OD instruments such as the BioDrop can cost tens of thousands of dollars and generally require manual sampling.
  • Who can use it. Teams working with bioreactors, continuous culture, fermentation, microbial growth monitoring and closed-loop biological systems.
  • Resources. CAD files, optical drawings, firmware and the four build cycles on Hardware: Photometer.
Validation, and the limits that came with it what the long run and the calibration actually established
  • Continuous run over days, logged at fixed intervals, with the full growth curve captured through decline phase.
  • Initial comparison with BioDrop measurements.
  • Documented reference-channel drift and other remaining calibration limitations.

Against a BioDrop reading the same titanium dioxide suspension, our instrument gives ours = 1.018 × BioDrop − 0.031 with R² = 0.995, and above 30 mg of added TiO2 it is the steadier of the two instruments. This shows the reading is linear in turbidity. It is not a cell count: the silica calibration that would tie a reading to biomass is written up but not yet run. Our OD channel also failed partway through the longest run, and the log does not record what was in the loop. Reuse the optical design and the drift log; there is no biomass calibration yet.

The dashboard and the sensor alarm that flags readings not to trust are on Digital Twin.

Programmable light plate apparatus

DiOPAL, a dual-wavelength optogenetic programmable array of LEDs, with every LED measured and grouped by its output.

Optogenetic characterization is hard to compare because the light dose a culture receives depends on LED output, distance, vessel geometry and crosstalk between neighbouring wells — factors that vary between setups and are seldom reported in enough detail to reproduce. In our research we found existing light plate hardware costly enough to be a barrier for anyone trying to get trustworthy light intensity at every position on a plate.

We built DiOPAL to map protectant expression against green and red light intensity through the CcaS/CcaR system. The design drives the LEDs by PWM, and every LED is measured individually and assigned to a group by matched output, so wells within a group receive a comparable dose.

The platform standardizes:

  • light intensity per well, with LEDs grouped by measured output (green spread 0.01 to 0.04 KLux; red spread 6 to 7 Lux)
  • independent green and red channel control
  • illumination geometry and well-to-well spacing
  • programmable dose and exposure duration by PWM
  • fixed-dose delivery, for characterization and not feedback control
  • Why it matters. Grouping LEDs by measured output instead of assuming nominal equivalence means the intensity axis of a dose–response curve reflects what the cells actually received. Characterization becomes reproducible between plates and between runs, and a future team can reuse the dose–response data without regenerating it on uncharacterized hardware.
  • Who can use it. Teams running optogenetic characterization, light-induced expression systems or any plate assay where dose per well has to be known.
  • Resources. CAD files, LED grouping data and build notes on Hardware: DiOPAL.

Laboratory hydroponics platform

A 3D-printable hydroponics plate with fixed dimensions, for laboratory plant experiments.

Plant experiments are hard to compare because small differences in spacing, nutrient exposure, root position or container geometry introduce variation on their own.

Visiting a laboratory at National Taiwan University, we saw older hydroponic setups stored away and no longer in use. A hydroponic system that exists is not necessarily one another lab can rebuild: without standardized dimensions and clear instructions it stays a one-off.

Our first prototypes were plastic containers, enough to test plant spacing and nutrient exposure. Three printed generations later the hole pattern settled into a plate with defined dimensions and a reproducible layout that makes access, measurement and nutrient transfer repeatable.

The platform standardizes plant spacing, nutrient exposure, root positioning, solution volume and experimental geometry.

  • Why it matters. Standardizing the physical growth environment removes variation that has nothing to do with the treatment. Experiments become easier to reproduce within one team, and other laboratories can compare results under consistent conditions.
  • Who can use it. Research laboratories, school laboratories, plant synthetic biology teams and future iGEM teams.
  • Resources. 3D print guide, CAD and STL files, dimensions, bill of materials and assembly instructions on Hardware: Hydroponics.

Software: digital twin

Software that keeps a live data-based picture of the reactor, so a farm can run it without sampling the culture.

The ReLEAF reactor is meant to stand near farmland, where nobody can constantly sample or check the culture. Our software therefore has to do what an operator at the bench would do: watch reactor conditions, judge whether sensor data can be trusted, predict how the culture will grow, and decide when to make protectant. A digital twin maintains a dynamic representation of the physical system, with information passing continuously in both directions, so the virtual copy reflects real-time changes and can be used to hold the physical system at the condition it should be in.

It combines regional crop-stress data with local reactor measurements, comparing what the field needs against what the reactor can deliver. The regional inputs are weather forecasts, crop-stress risk and geospatial analysis; the reactor supplies biomass, growth, pressure, flow, membrane state and sensor reliability. Directly measured, operator-commanded, calculated and planned values are kept separate, so every number on screen has a clear source. Those inputs are what the system uses to assess whether the culture is ready and whether protectant production should be activated.

  • Why it matters. The bioreactor and the field are treated as one system, so a production decision rests both on when protection may be needed and on whether the biological system can respond in time.
  • Who can use it. Teams building remote or semi-automated bioprocesses, and anyone who has to decide from sensor data whether a culture is fit to run.
  • Resources. The interface, the run records and the forecast scoring are on Digital Twin; the code is in our software repository, linked from that page.

Working toward automated monitoring and decision-making is how ReLEAF reduces the need for constant manual supervision and specialized technical knowledge. That is what could make the reactor operable by a farmer without laboratory training, and what its deployment outside a research facility depends on.

Geographic information systems

Plant stress is not spread evenly. Temperature, salinity, rainfall and agricultural productivity vary across Taiwan, so each farming region carries its own combination of risk. We used GIS to connect environmental conditions with agricultural outcomes and to see where climate-related stress overlaps with important crop-producing regions.

For ReLEAF this matters because the system is designed to answer local conditions. Treating every farm alike would waste that, and spatial data instead shows:

  • where heat stress is more severe
  • where salinity risk is higher
  • which agricultural regions may be more vulnerable
  • where localized deployment could have the greatest relevance

In our analysis of Taiwan we compared climate variables with regional rice-yield data to explore how environmental conditions relate to agricultural productivity. It moved us from looking at individual farms to reading broader geographic patterns.

LLM-assisted application

GIS can reveal spatial patterns, but interpreting large environmental datasets is still difficult for a non-specialist. We therefore wrote an LLM-assisted integrative prompt: a structural guideline that an LLM follows to organize large data sets with inconsistent coordinates, software and formatting into files that go straight into a geographic information system.

The workflow accepts data sets in various formats and translates them into numerics that can be entered directly into QGIS, without the risk of taking in false data the model invented. The LLM does not do the GIS analysis; it organizes the data sets the user already has, so the user is not left doing the external checking, research and reorganization by hand.

  • Why it matters. This gives a future team a way to connect a synthetic biology or engineering project with the place where it may eventually be used. Besides asking whether a solution works, a team can ask where the problem is most relevant and how environmental conditions differ between regions. The workflow can be adapted by teams working in agriculture, environmental biotechnology, climate resilience, public health or any other location-dependent application.
  • Resources. QGIS workflow guide, data-processing instructions, sample GIS project, map-layer documentation, data sources and the LLM workflow, all on Geospatial Analysis.

Education

Lesson plans, slide decks, worksheets and games in English and Traditional Chinese, free to download and reteach.

For our team, education is not a one-time lesson taught in an hour or two. It is a journey students carry forward and apply to other parts of their lives. Our program helps students understand plant stress, climate change, sustainability and synthetic biology through hands-on activities and interactive learning. One of our main goals throughout was to build an education that is itself sustainable.

We compiled an accessible website holding lesson plans, presentations, worksheets, activities and learning materials from this year and from the past two years. They are available in both English and Traditional Chinese, so they suit different learning environments and reach a wider range of students. Because they work without our team being physically present, they can go on benefiting students, teachers and future iGEM teams.

Our lessons were also developed through interactive feedback loops and structured student debates. Post-activity survey forms guided how we adapted each lesson, and bioethics debates showed us the range of student perspectives on synthetic biology. We are handing over both the teaching materials and the process that produced them, so a future team can adapt them to build environmental awareness in its own community.

  • Why it matters. Materials that can be taught again without us are what makes an education program outlast one season of workshops.
  • Who can use it. Teachers, school clubs, community educators and future iGEM teams.
  • Resources. Lesson plans, slide decks, worksheets, card games and online games on our education website; the surveys and debates that changed them are on Education.

Agricultural waste as culture medium

Hay infusion, okara extract and sugarcane extract tested as culture media for B. subtilis, measured against LB.

To make ReLEAF practical on a real farm, we asked whether agricultural waste could replace the culture medium. In the laboratory B. subtilis is usually grown in chemical media such as LB [7], which works well on a bench but is not something a farmer can easily obtain. Without access to culture medium, cultivating the bacteria and producing protectant stops being feasible, and the technology stops being adoptable. Agricultural byproducts, by contrast, are already generated on site and may carry useful carbon and nitrogen.

How it works

We tested three agricultural waste sources: hay infusion, okara extract and sugarcane extract. Hay provides carbohydrates and structural polysaccharides, okara is rich in protein [4], and sugarcane bagasse contains cellulose and hemicellulose that can serve as carbon sources after processing [3]. We also tested mixtures of the three extracts, to see whether combining nutrient sources would support growth better than any one of them.

After 18 hours of incubation at 37 °C, bacterial density was measured at OD600. The 1:1:2 mixture of hay infusion, okara extract and sugarcane extract gave the highest average OD600 at 0.720, followed by the 1:2:1 mixture at 0.708. LB medium reached 0.573 and hay infusion alone 0.555, while okara extract alone was the weakest at 0.333. Combining different agricultural waste extracts appears to give a more balanced nutrient source than any single extract.

What this contribution does not yet include

The written preparation protocol is not published here yet, and the comparison rests on two cultures per medium at a single 18-hour endpoint, with no growth curve behind it.

Why it matters

At least 1,300 million tons of agricultural waste is produced every year, and the figure is likely to rise. Handling it through landfill and incineration contributes about 3% of global greenhouse gas emissions and creates various toxic pollutants [5]. For hay in particular, the humid weather in Taiwan damages its quality and encourages mold, wasting nearly 5,000 tonnes each season [6]. Repurposing these wastes as culture media turns a local byproduct into a resource, and supports SDG 12: Responsible Consumption and Production.

Real field deployment

This is an initial framework for using agricultural byproducts as media for B. subtilis. Preparation still takes processing, so deployment would more realistically run through farmer cooperatives or agricultural research stations than through individual farmers. That lifts the technical burden off the farmer but adds costs in transport, processing and distribution, so future work should cost the whole production chain and look at what subsidies or government programs would keep it affordable.

Further research will test additional agricultural wastes, improve pretreatment methods, and monitor OD600 over time so that growth curves can be compared instead of a single endpoint. That is what would show whether a waste-based medium can become a practical, lower-cost input for localized bioproduction.

  • Who can use it. Teams who need to culture a chassis outside a laboratory supply chain, and anyone working on low-cost or decentralized bioproduction.

Recommendations for future teams

The designs are only half of what we can hand over. The other half is what the engineering process taught us, including the parts that went wrong.

What worked

Five habits that paid for themselves, and that cost almost nothing to start.

  • Stirring the reservoir. Adding a 200 rpm magnetic stir bar was one of the three changes (with the sampling point and the temperature) between a reactor where the culture did not grow and one that reached plateau faster than a shake flask. If you build a perfusion loop, stir the reservoir from the first run.
  • Splitting the construct into four modules, so that a failure could be located and rebuilt without touching the rest.
  • Fixing the imaging geometry before the first experiment: same background, same lighting, same plate orientation, same camera distance. It costs an afternoon and it makes every later photograph comparable.
  • Calibrating an optical instrument against a nonliving turbidity standard first. It separates instrument behaviour from biology, and it is cheap.
  • Taking the prototype to a farmers' market and a public forum while it was still ugly. The questions we got there changed the design.

What didn't

Six failures with a cause we can name. Each one cost a week or a dataset.

  • The first reactor build leaked at the peristaltic pump and the trial had to be stopped early. Use biocompatible tubing that seals well at the pump head, not standard tubing.
  • Running the loop without stirring: the culture in the fiber lumen did not grow at 22 °C while a control at the same temperature did.
  • Trehalose as a positive control. Six experiment sets, and no rescue that survives its own controls. Pick a reference protectant with a published dose in your assay before you build experiments on it.
  • Spraying plants with a culture nobody had blotted. A Western blot later showed that the batch carried no detectable ACC deaminase, which voided the whole week of plant work.
  • Chlorophyll workbooks maintained in two copies. The same raw absorbances produced different answers in different files, and every number from four experiment sets had to be withdrawn.
  • Leaving the reagent field blank on the hydroponic box sheets. Two complete datasets exist and nobody can now say what was added.

What we would change

What we would do differently on day one of another project.

  • Measure the light before building experiments on it: an intensity map across every well position, the duty cycles written down, and one induction curve, green against dark, before anything downstream assumes the switch works.
  • One canonical spreadsheet per assay, with the formula written once and the raw readings never retyped.
  • Record the run conditions in the same file as the data: medium, inoculum, which compartment the optics sat on, incubation time and temperature.
  • Plate the shell side at every sampling point, not once per project, so containment stops resting on a single plate.
  • Log transmembrane pressure continuously across a whole run instead of taking spot readings at each pump setting.
  • Decide the normalisation for chlorophyll (per fresh weight or per plant) before the first extract, because the two rank the same samples in opposite orders.

Future development

The work a team picking this up would start with.

  • Increase experimental throughput
  • Improve system automation
  • Strengthen containment validation
  • Improve photometer calibration and ratiometric measurement
  • Adapt hydroponics to additional plant species
  • Scale modular bioreactor systems
  • Integrate sensing and biological control into a more automated closed-loop platform
