Overview

ReLEAF is an optogenetic bioreactor system that enables on-demand protection against combined biotic and abiotic crop stresses through cell-free delivery of protective proteins. Our mission is to strengthen agricultural resilience by reducing crop losses while decreasing dependence on chemical interventions.

What we plan to do

Deploy a sealed bioreactor system at the farm level that continuously monitors soil and environmental conditions, detects when stress exceeds thresholds (heat, salinity, pathogens), produces and applies protective proteins directly to the crop. The system operates with minimal farmer intervention and integrates seamlessly into existing irrigation systems.

What the product does

The bioreactor houses engineered Bacillus subtilis that synthesizes three complementary protective proteins: LEA 14 (abiotic stress protection), ACCD (ethylene reduction), and BoPEP4 (antifungal defense), triggered by green/red light signals when stress is detected. A hollow-fiber membrane ensures biological containment: bacteria stay inside, only protective metabolites exit into soil.

Mission statement: ReLEAF strengthens agricultural resilience by enabling on-demand protection against combined biotic and abiotic stresses through an optogenetic bioreactor, shifting from reactive chemical management to proactive, precision crop defense while reducing environmental contamination.

Vision: Every farmer is a biomanufacturer.

Our vision is to empower every farmer to have their small bacteria factory for producing biostimulants that help plants withstand environmental stresses. Through our bioreactor system, we wish to reduce long bioprocessing times and challenges, so that farmers can benefit from science at a faster rate than the present.

Climate stresses & vulnerability of agriculture

In a world of climate change, where seasons shift dramatically, temperatures fluctuate uncontrollably, soil salinity escalates, and fungal outbreaks intensify, humanity is forced to choose between immediate technological adaptation or multi-system crop failure in modern agriculture. Abiotic stresses, primarily heat and soil salinity, already cause an estimated 50% of worldwide crop yield losses annually, and climate change further exacerbates the severity and frequency of these issues (Bray et al., 2000; Mittler, 2002). For example, a 1 degree Celsius rise in average growing season temperatures reduces rice yield by 6.2%. Additionally, over 20% of the world’s irrigated farmland is already significantly affected by salinity, with an additional 3 hectares becoming unproductive every minute (Zhao et al., 2017). Small farmers produce approximately 70-80% of the global food supply, a proportion that rises to over 80% of all consumed food in many developing sub-regions (Ricciardi et al., 2018). The focus on small farmers is critical because they represent the backbone of local food sovereignty. In East Asia, including Taiwan, where over 90% of agricultural holdings are small-scale family operations averaging less than 1 hectare, these small farmers directly supply more than 80% of domestic fresh vegetables and fruits (Ministry of Agriculture, 2024). In Taiwan’s Dahu area, which produces about 90% of the country’s strawberries, rising temperatures and typhoon rainfall have been linked to lower first-period yields (Chang, 2020), while year-round tomato production in the subtropical lowlands is challenged by summer heat and a growing risk of salinity stress, with yield strongly suppressed even by short-term mild salinity in a highly heat-tolerant cultivar (Liu et al., 2014). For all the smallholder farmers that make up over 500 million smallholder farms, these staggering numbers mean that a single stress event can potentially wipe out an entire season’s income with no recovery mechanism or backup plan.

One of the key issues that make this crisis particularly acute is the combination of heat and salinity stress. Under field conditions, these stresses occur repeatedly, compounding cellular damage in ways that can’t be triggered by either stress independently. At the same time, plants that are weakened by stress can become vulnerable to pathogens, meaning that even a miniscule instance of salinity or heat can initiate a disease outbreak. Current agricultural systems are not equipped to handle such crises - either current solutions damage soil health and burden farmers with costs they can’t afford, or these solutions are reactive and too slow, only able to be applied after visible damage has occurred. Chemical solutions such as fungicides (ex. mancozeb, azoxystrobin, or copper based sprays) and synthetic fertilizers remain the most accessible and affordable remedies, but they only further destroy the conditions of the already stressed plants because they are only broadly applied after visible and often irreversible crop damage has already occurred.

Product description

Product usage and function

Our field-deployable bioreactor is a sealed system containing engineered Bacillus subtilis that uses a hollow-fiber membrane filtration system to deliver protectant molecules directly into the root zone of vegetable crops. By restricting all living bacteria inside the device while allowing only the secreted protectants to exit, it automates crop defense without the need for manual spraying or application. The device functions with minimal farmer intervention, requiring only occasional maintenance checks or a cartridge replacement when the protectant runs low. It is fully automated by an Arduino-based control unit, a circulation pump, a temperature sensor, a salinity/EC sensor, and a green and red LED optogenetic control system. This integrated tech stack continuously monitors environmental conditions and automatically activates specific stress-response modules the moment predefined thresholds are breached.

The leaf humidity sensor sits on the leaves of crops and tells us the humidity of the leaf surface. The sensor then sends its readings to our AIoT system, which uses the readings to forecast when the crop is going to need protectant. By using the sensor data, the AIoT system also predicts upcoming weather conditions, while the sensor tells the user how the crop is doing. If the leaves show signs of stress before the forecast predicted, the AIoT system can bring production forward accordingly. The AIoT translates this multi-source data into a guidance for farmers without technical background understanding of the bioreactor. Together, the sensor and AIoT link conditions in the field directly to production in the bioreactor, ensuring protectant is available exactly when the crop needs it.

The bacterial culture replenishment system supplies a sealed Bacillus subtilis inoculum in a single-use cartridge that fits the bioreactor’s filter and cartridge design. The cartridge drops into the reservoir port from above and opens automatically once it is seated, so the culture goes straight into the reservoir. There is no need to handle an open culture, and the bioreactor stays closed throughout. Each package includes two culture cartridges together with the main unit. Thus, a replacement is on hand from the first season. Since the culture arrives ready to use, farmers do not need a lab, special equipment, or any microbiology skills to prepare fresh culture themselves.

The app pairs up with the bioreactor and consolidates operational information into five modules: Overview, Field Setup, AI Forecast, Log, and Settings. The Overview page shows a prioritized list of required actions, a summary of the current AI forecast, real-time field and reactor measurements, a schematic of the red/green light-control system, and the status of consumables (medium and culture). The Field Setup module records the inputs from farmers regarding the farm location, the crops grown and the area allocated to each, and the numbers of soil sensors and bioreactors already installed. From this data, the system estimates the recommended equipment and its total cost. The AI Forecast module reports a stress risk level for each crop, the estimated time until stress onset based on the farmer’s location, the target quantity of protectant, and the quantity produced to date. Orders are collected in a cart accessible from the header. In the present version, all sensor readings are simulated for demonstration purposes.

Business model

What is a social enterprise business model?

A social enterprise, at its core, is a business model that aims to use commercial revenue to sustain its operations and advance a social mission, which makes it distinct from an non-governmental organization (NGO). A good example of this is USE-GH, or Ubuntu Social Enterprise-Ghana, which is actually one of the organizations that inspired the very first project pitch for this. USE-GH buys crops from farmers directly, at a much lower fee than middlemen charge. It also teaches farmers to process their raw materials into finished products, so they earn more from the same harvest. As can be seen, the aim is to be able to generate enough profit to continue running all while helping the community the social enterprise wants to support, which in our case is small farmers all over the world.

Why a social enterprise?

The team is aware that not a lot of money can be made out of it, but our vision is to have a social impact, and to empower farmers, which is why we chose this business model instead of a traditional one. We want to be able to preserve our vision in the best way possible while not becoming an NGO, as then operations would be difficult due to high costs.

SWOT analysis

In order to better understand where our advantages and weaknesses lie, we did a SWOT analysis, carefully considering our strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats.

Strengths

Our SWOT analysis highlights the strong potential of our project while weighing key challenges that must be addressed for successful implementation. The system’s primary strengths lie in its modular multi-chamber design, which allows for the targeted, light-triggered production of three specific protective proteins (LEA, ACCD, and BoPEP4). Additionally, its cell-free delivery approach reduces biosafety and regulatory concerns, while its integration of electronic sensors and AI forecasting allows it to deliver precise protectant doses to both soil-based and hydroponic systems. These features provide a sustainable alternative to conventional chemical pesticides or outdated methods while minimizing environmental contamination.

Opportunities

Externally, the project is in a beneficial position due to the rapidly growing biocontrol market, increasing climate-related crop stress, and global support for sustainable agriculture. Additionally, many countries strictly regulate or are in the process of phasing out toxic chemical pesticides, which creates a strong push toward biological alternatives. This widens our market opportunity across both traditional field agriculture and high-tech hydroponic systems, increasing adoption potential amongst growers seeking sustainable solutions. Many companies around the world charge the same amount for microbe fermentation and sensing separately while our product is a two in one for the same price. Furthermore, since the Taiwan government is willing to give subsidies to local farmers, it makes our product more accessible to our stakeholders. Also, our product can be tailor made for different farmers from different backgrounds, which expands our market.

Weaknesses

However, the project also has several weaknesses. There could be potential protectant loss from being washed away by water. Additionally, the bioreactor might not work fast enough when met with a sudden mass onslaught of stress. Imperfections in the bioreactor could lead to dilution of the protectants, while biofouling inside the chambers may reduce protein yield and shorten the system's lifespan. The product may also face limitations in countries with strict organic certification requirements because organic certification standards exclude engineered inputs, so we do not target organic farms. Furthermore, farmers would need to learn how to replace cartridges, which could increase long-term maintenance costs and create an additional barrier to adoption. With the bioreactor, the hollow fibre membrane might get blocked, which can slow down or potentially prevent excretion.

Threats

Finally, several external threats could affect the success of the product. Unclear regulations surrounding cell-free agricultural biologics may delay approval, especially if the product falls between the classifications of a biological pesticide and a synthetic biology-derived compound. Farmer adoption may also be limited by unfamiliarity with the technology, concerns about cost-effectiveness, and reliance on traditional chemical pesticides. In addition, some farmers may view synthetic biology as too risky, while restrictions on organic certification could limit access to high-value organic farming markets. Lastly, the bioreactor may get destroyed by natural disasters such as the frequent earthquakes or typhoons in Taiwan.

PESTEL analysis

Political factors create both challenges as well as opportunities. Government regulations on GMO crops represent a significant barrier to market entry in regions with strict policies. However, this also presents us with potential pathways for government support through green-tech initiatives and agricultural subsidies, which could accelerate adoption rates while reducing development costs.

Economic considerations directly influence the market viability of our product. High fertilizer and pesticide prices drive demand for sustainable alternatives, which creates favorable market conditions for our bioreactor. However, global supply chain disruption affecting traditional chemical fertilizer delivery could create temporary delays in our competitive timeline, while shifts in agricultural spending patterns may affect the purchasing power of customers.

Social factors are critical to the success of our product. Public perception and acceptance of GMOs are relatively mixed, which requires strong communication strategies and transparency about the safety and benefits of our product. The growing consumer demand for eco-friendly farming practices aligns well with our value proposition, although we must navigate cultural attitudes toward agricultural innovation in different markets.

Technological advancement is also a key component. Rapid developments in AI and forecasting tools allow us to create competitive advantages through better performance as well as monitoring fields in real-time. Standardized manufacturing processes for hardware and sensors are able to reduce scaling barriers.

Environmental pressures also pose benefits and risks. Severe climate threats including droughts, heat waves, and fungal disease create an immediate demand for crop resilience solutions. The global push to meet UN Sustainable Development Goals (particularly SDGs 2, 4, 10, 12, 15) reinforces the market need for our environmental solution while opening doors to funding focused on sustainability.

Legal and regulatory frameworks also require a lot of careful navigation. Environmental release and biosafety laws determine how our bioreactor can be deployed in the market, while regulatory limitations for engineered biological products in food are very complex across different institutions. However, at the same time, clear regulatory pathways could potentially accelerate approval timelines.

Current challenges and solutions

Current management methods often depend on repeated chemical spraying, manual monitoring, and frequent maintenance, leading to higher labor costs, chemical waste, environmental pollution, and inefficient use of resources. In addition, these traditional solutions are rarely tailored to the specific conditions of individual farms, making it difficult for farmers to identify the right time to intervene. By combining stress detection and treatment in a single platform, our technology reduces unnecessary chemical inputs, minimizes waste and runoff, lowers daily labor requirements, promotes faster plant recovery, and helps maintain more stable crop yields during challenging growing seasons.

Our solution

Unlike traditional agricultural inputs that rely on centralized chemical manufacturing and reactive spraying, our system introduces novel solutions. Instead of shipping chemical bottles to the field, we deploy an on-site, on-demand field factory directly to the farm. This bridges the gap left by standard solutions, which either rely on chemical inputs that cause runoff, basic biostimulants that lack automatic delivery, or passive alert sensors that identify stress but cannot treat it without human intervention. By utilizing our optimized CcaS-CcaR two-component optogenetic system linked to automated LED arrays, the bioreactor dynamically shifts production to synthesize specific protectants right at the crop site. Green and red light signals act as the trigger into the system to simultaneously regulate bacterial cell growth and control protectant production, protecting plants against both biotic and abiotic stresses without leaving chemical residues in the bioreactor culture. Furthermore, our solutions offer a cheaper solution and more competitive price compared to others to environmental stress when compared with other companies that offer similar options.

Our bioreactor uses engineered Bacillus subtilis to continuously evaluate crop conditions by pairing real-time field sensors with predictive stress forecasting. When environmental or biological threats pass a safety threshold, the device automatically synthesizes targeted protectants to address climate stress and plant disease precisely when and where intervention is required. Built with a flexible, modular design, the system can be adapted across different crop types and growing setups. It also takes into great consideration of biosafety, ensuring engineered bacteria remain safely contained inside the hardware to allow only active protectants to reach the crops.

Challenges

Despite all these opportunities, our product will undeniably face several challenges on the market. Our product is unlikely to be more effective than the current best chemical solutions, and because of all the components included within it, is unable to match the price of chemical solutions either without sacrificing revenue entirely. Due to our product including GMOs, it will face legal hurdles such as extensive testing, heavy usage restrictions, and close government surveillance, as GMOs are treated with much caution by several countries and key markets, such as the European Union, Australia, and our home country, Taiwan. This could also restrict how much of our product we can distribute throughout countries and in Taiwan, and make it so that the person buying our product needs a license to use it. Aside from existing legal challenges, there’s also existing stigma regarding synthetic organisms that may push farmers away from purchasing our product, as they fear it could negatively affect the environment or escape the containment of our bioreactor. The B. subtilis itself could also present a challenge in that its survival rate may be too low to truly be sustainable, causing a need for frequent switches and creating a high cost to using our product that will likely alienate our target demographic, who are smaller, sustainable farmers.

Phase 1.1: Concept Validation (Year 1)

Our mission is to enhance agricultural resilience against climate-intensified abiotic stressors such as heat waves, salinity and drought by developing an automated, sensor driven bioreactor. Financial projection

Because no two farms are alike, ReLEAF’s financial projection is not a fixed price. The financial projection is built from several factors that, put together, decide what each farm needs and how much it pays. In our interactive financial model, farmers input their farm size (0.25-5 ha), how many crop types they grow (1 to 3), and planting density of their crops (22,000 plants per hectare for small-fruit tomatoes or 33,000 for large-fruit tomatoes). From these variables, the model works out the number of plants on the farm and how many enzymes each spray needs. That tells us how many bioreactors the farm needs, each producing 1 L batch of culture every 16 hours. The model also gives one humidity sensor for each crop type and works out how many refill cartridges the farmer will need each season. The cartridge count is set by whichever has a larger value: the enzyme the farm uses or the cartridge’s six-month shelf life. All of this is then combined with our calculated enzyme activity, our hardware costs, and practical costs (including freight, setup visits, and warranty). The result is the Year 1 package price (including hardware and cartridges) and the lower yearly cost after that, when the farmer only needs to pay for cartridges. This lets each farmer see a cost that fits their own farm when deciding whether the ReLEAF product is worth it.

Regulations

Taiwan presents a unique regulatory opportunity forming the foundation of our Phase 2 strategy. Taiwan has stringent restrictions against Living Modified Organisms (LMOs) and GMO field releases under the Ministry of Agriculture (MOA) and Animal and Plant Health Inspection Agency (BAPHIQ). However, Taiwan’s regulatory framework contains a critical pathway: if engineered bacteria never leave the sealed device, the system can be classified as “contained-use” equipment rather than an environmental release of GMOs.

ReLEAF’s system is fundamentally cell-free delivery: non-living metabolic byproducts (protective proteins) are discharged into soil, not viable Bacillus subtilis cells. The engineered bacteria remain physically confined via the 0.22 micrometer hollow-fiber membrane. By legally proving “absolute containment” to BAPHIQ and MOA, we bypass the restrictive environmental-release framework and pursue a streamlined contained-use approval pathway.

Our regulatory submission provides comprehensive biosafety validation data: escape-frequency assays, hollow-fiber membrane integrity testing, genetic kill-switch efficacy documentation, physical housing durability testing, and field trial protocols demonstrating no environmental bacterial release during pilot deployment with 20–50 devices. We partner with government-recognized testing units such as Taiwan Agricultural Research Institute (TARI) and Tainan District Agricultural Research & Extension Station to verify containment and provide regulatory credibility.

Strategic regulatory advantage

In parallel, ReLEAF positions itself as a plant biostimulant rather than biopesticide. Under Taiwan’s Fertilizer Regulations, biostimulants follow a streamlined pathway: approximately 20 days for fertilizer specification report plus 7 days for government review equals approximately 30 days total to market. Registering as a biopesticide would require 3–5 years, multi-season field trials, GLP animal testing, and higher capital requirements. We register LEA 14 and ACCD as plant growth regulators and biostimulants under the fertilizer pathway, enabling commercial production and early revenue within 30 days, while later filing for biopesticide designation for BoPEP4 if regulatory circumstances and market demand warrant it.

According to the Philippine Statistics Authority (PSA), agriculture, forestry, and fishing generated approximately ₱1.78 trillion in gross value added in 2024. The country also produced 19.09 million metric tons of palay (unmilled rice) that year, across 4.65 million hectares of harvested area. However, palay production declined by 4.8% compared with 2023, illustrating the importance of improving agricultural resilience and productivity (Philippine Statistics Authority [PSA], 2025). These figures establish the scale of the agricultural sector, although they do not by themselves prove demand for ReLEAF or establish that abiotic stress caused the decline.

The Philippines is also a practical regional expansion candidate because it has an established regulatory authority for agricultural inputs: the Fertilizer and Pesticide Authority (FPA). This provides a clear institutional starting point for determining how ReLEAF’s cell-free output could be classified, what evidence would be required, and which local partners could support testing and distribution. The Philippines should therefore be evaluated as a potential second market after Taiwan, with expansion contingent on regulatory feedback, field validation, affordability, and farmer demand—not on market size alone.

Philippine regulatory clearance

ReLEAF’s entry into the Philippines would require separate assessments of the bioreactor, engineered production culture, and cell-free agricultural output. These components should not be assumed to share a single approval pathway.

  1. Confirm the product classification. ReLEAF should first consult the Philippine Fertilizer and Pesticide Authority (FPA) to determine whether the final cell-free protein/peptide formulation can be registered as a fertilizer or another recognized agricultural input. Our intended positioning is abiotic-stress tolerance rather than pest or pathogen control, but the final classification depends on the product’s composition, intended use, claims, and the FPA’s assessment. The FPA regulates agricultural fertilizers and pesticides and requires applicable product registrations and handler licences before regulated products are marketed.
  2. Clarify biosafety requirements for the engineered culture. Because ReLEAF supplies an engineered Bacillus subtilis culture for use in a bioreactor, the production organism must be assessed separately from the cell-free output. The Philippine Department of Science and Technology Biosafety Committee (DOST-BC) publishes guidelines covering contained-use GMO activities, including work involving microorganisms. Its published guidance also states that biosafety approval does not replace any additional permits required by other authorities. ReLEAF must therefore obtain a determination of which requirements apply to its proposed commercial operation, including culture importation or transfer, storage, containment, transport, and disposal. The published contained-use guideline is marked as under revision, so the applicable current requirements must be confirmed directly with the authorities.
  3. Validate containment and product quality. Before deployment, ReLEAF should generate evidence that the bioreactor retains viable engineered cells under its intended operating conditions and that the cell-free output meets defined identity, consistency, safety, and efficacy specifications. The 0.2 μm hollow-fiber membrane is part of the containment design, but its performance must be demonstrated experimentally rather than treated as proof of complete cell retention.
  4. Assess the bioreactor as equipment. The bioreactor’s pumps, sensors, electrical components, fluid-handling assembly, and any applicable pressure-related features require a separate review of relevant Philippine equipment, importation, and safety requirements. FPA product registration should not be treated as approval of the device itself.
  5. Complete the required registrations before commercial sale. After confirming the applicable pathways, ReLEAF would compile the required technical documentation, test results, labeling, and other supporting evidence; obtain the necessary product registrations, permits, and business or handler licences; and establish compliant local distribution. Commercial launch should occur only after the relevant authorities have confirmed the requirements and ReLEAF has satisfied them.

ReLEAF will be pushing to expand to other Southeast Asian markets as well as the United States of America or the European Union market as mentioned in the Future Expansions of Laws and Regulations page.

Potential risks

Biological risks

Market and adoption risks

Hollow-fiber membrane leakage

ProbabilityLow-Medium

ImpactMedium (regulatory consequence, requires containment re-verification)

Description

Membrane’s molecular-weight cutoff could be miscalibrated, damaged during handling, or degraded over device’s operational lifetime, allowing whole Bacillus subtilis cells (0.5–4 micrometers) to pass into environment.

Mitigation

Experimentally validate membrane cutoff against Bacillus subtilis cell size under multiple conditions and accelerated degradation testing under simulated field conditions (temperature cycling, moisture exposure, mechanical stress). Build membrane integrity checks into formal maintenance and replacement schedule. Conduct simple visual/functional tests monthly with farmers or extension officers.

Physical containment breach

ProbabilityLow

ImpactHigh (most direct pathway to uncontrolled environmental release)

Description

Sealed bioreactor housing could crack, degrade, or be damaged during handling, transport, or extended field use, exposing internal bacterial compartment directly to soil.

Mitigation

Stress-test housing for durability under realistic field conditions (moisture, temperature cycling, mechanical handling, soil microbes). Design bioreactor compartment to show visible proof of tampering or breach so any integrity failure is immediately detectable. Emphasize in farmer training and maintenance protocols that damaged devices must be returned to collection points. Redundancy with genetic and membrane containment means single breach alone is unlikely to cause bacterial escape.

Farmer distrust of unfamiliar technology

ProbabilityMedium

ImpactMedium (slower early market penetration)

Description

Farmers accustomed to familiar chemical sprays may distrust sealed biological device, especially if technology seems difficult to understand or maintain. Slow adoption in pilot phase could limit early market traction.

Mitigation

Clear, farmer-centric messaging avoiding overly technical language. Emphasize practical benefits: fewer spray days, reduced crop loss during heat/wet seasons, decreased daily field monitoring labor. Prioritize pilot deployments with progressive agricultural cooperatives and extension offices interested in precision agriculture and IoT systems. Generate local testimonials and case studies before broader sales.

Slow adoption by cooperatives and extension networks

ProbabilityMedium

ImpactMedium (distribution delays despite scientific validation)

Description

Cooperatives, NGOs, and government agricultural extension offices may be cautious about adopting novel agricultural inputs without long-term, multi-season evidence.

Mitigation

Prioritize institutions already oriented toward innovation. Design pilot trials as small, tightly controlled, outcome-focused engagements with clear success metrics: measurable crop-loss reduction, documented decrease in fungicide spray events, farmer satisfaction above defined thresholds. Deliver tangible, documented wins in first season to build credibility for longer-term scaling.

Production cost exceeds farmer budget

ProbabilityMedium

ImpactHigh (severely limits market penetration and farmer affordability)

Description

Unit cost of bioreactor device, cartridges, hollow-fiber membranes, and bacterial culture may become prohibitively expensive, limiting adoption among smallholder farmers even if technology is effective.

Mitigation

Comprehensive financial sensitivity analysis testing whether ReLEAF remains viable if production costs are 20% higher or selling prices are 20% lower. If direct farmer purchase is unaffordable, pivot to alternative business models: cooperative purchasing (multiple farmers share device ownership), NGO bulk purchase and subsidized distribution, seasonal rental models, or cartridge subscription systems. These flexible approaches ensure cost barriers do not prevent technology adoption.

Frequently asked questions

How does ReLEAF work?

A sensor in your field sends data to our AIoT system, which also forecasts upcoming stress. When stress is detected or forecast, the system uses optogenetic (light) control to trigger our engineered B. subtilis to produce the protectant. The protectant passes through a membrane that keeps the bacteria inside the bioreactor, so only the protectant reaches your crop.

Which crops is ReLEAF best for?

ReLEAF is not tied to one crop. Published work on our three types of protectant covers wheat (LEA; Yang et al., 2022), wheat, maize, rice, pea and tomato (ACC deaminase; Shahid et al., 2023) and broccoli (BoPEP4; Wang et al., 2022). Most of this work tests salt tolerance, and none of it uses our product. We test on the model plant Arabidopsis because the iGEM rules only allow us to work with it.

What stress does it protect against?

ReLEAF targets heat, salinity and biotic stress (pathogenic infection), which are among the biggest causes of crop loss worldwide. We have run experiments on both heat and salinity in Arabidopsis. See our Plant page for the results.

How many bioreactors and sensors do I need?

About 2 bioreactors per hectare (1 for up to 0.5 ha), plus 1 sensor per crop type. Denser crops need more bioreactors. Our online calculator works it out for your farm.

What does ReLEAF cost?

A bioreactor is about US$2,276 (NT$72,180), a sensor about US$126 (NT$4,000), and a refill cartridge about US$180 (NT$5,710). A 0.5 ha farm pays about US$2,762 in year one, then about US$360 a year for refills. These are planning estimates, not quotes.

Is it safe for my soil?

Our bacteria stay sealed inside the bioreactor, and only the protectant reaches your crop and soil. A hollow fiber membrane filters out the bacteria, a droplet sensor detects any liquid that spills inside the bioreactor, and a pressure sensor keeps the pressure constant so the membrane does not break. From the hardware page, it shows that our bacteria does not leak out from the bioreactor.

Has it been tested on a farm?

Not yet. We have lab results only: the enzyme is active, and treated Arabidopsis recovered better from heat and salinity stress. Our first field data will come from four pilot farms.

How is it different from biofertilizers?

Biofertilizers feed or boost plants on a schedule. ReLEAF protects against stress, and a field sensor tells you when to spray.

How often do I refill or replace parts?

About 2 cartridges a year, since each lasts roughly 6 months (still being tested). The bioreactor and sensor are one-time purchases.

How much work is it, and do I need special skills?

You replace a cartridge about twice a year, and apply the protectant to your crop about 9 times in a 10-month tomato season depending on the frequency of stress events happened. We are exploring connecting the system to the irrigation system so spraying is automatic. If you have a drone, you can dilute the protectant according to our guideline and spray with it.

Can I use it on an organic farm?

No. Organic farms cannot use engineered bacteria or their products, even when the bacteria are sealed inside the bioreactor. Taiwan's Organic Agriculture Promotion Act defines organic farming as not using genetically modified organisms and their products (Organic Agriculture Promotion Act, 2018, Art. 3), and bars operators from using inputs that contain genetically modified products (Organic Agriculture Promotion Act, 2018, Art. 15).

Conclusion

ReLEAF represents a transformative approach to agricultural resilience in the face of accelerating climate change. By combining optogenetic control, on-site biomanufacturing, cell-free delivery, and modular design, we address a critical gap in current agricultural technology: the need for adaptive, and sustainable crop protection that responds to real-time stress rather than relying on reactive, broad-spectrum chemical spraying.

Our three-layered containment approach (genetic kill switch, hollow-fiber membrane, physical housing) ensures that even if one containment layer fails, redundant protection prevents environmental release of engineered organisms. This design philosophy allows us to speak with confidence to regulators about biosafety while maintaining strategic flexibility across different regulatory environments. Our sequenced regulatory strategy of prioritizing Taiwan and the Philippines in Phase 2–3 to establish proof-of-concept and build a track record, expanding to Vietnam and the U.S. in Phase 3–4, balances time-to-market, regulatory risk, and commercial opportunity.

Ultimately, ReLEAF’s mission extends far beyond improving agricultural productivity. We aim to support the livelihoods of climate-vulnerable farming communities, stabilize seasonal farm incomes, prevent climate-driven debt cycles, preserve ancestral land assets, and make sustainable agriculture viable for future generations. By shifting from reactive chemical management to proactive, precision crop defense, we reduce environmental contamination, protect soil microbiomes, and help establish agricultural systems that are resilient, adaptive, and sustainable for decades to come.
