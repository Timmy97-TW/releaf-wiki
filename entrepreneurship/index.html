Overview

ReLEAF is an optogenetic bioreactor system that is designed to provide on-demand protection against combined biotic and abiotic crop stresses through cell-free delivery of protective proteins. Our mission is to strengthen agricultural resilience by reducing crop losses while decreasing dependence on chemical interventions.

What we plan to do

Deploy a sealed bioreactor system at the farm level that continuously monitors soil and environmental conditions, detects when stress exceeds thresholds (heat, salinity, pathogens), produces and applies protective proteins directly to the crop. The system operates with minimal farmer intervention and integrates seamlessly into existing irrigation systems.

What the product does

The bioreactor houses engineered Bacillus subtilis that synthesizes three complementary protective proteins: late embryogenesis abundant protein LEA 14 (abiotic stress protection), 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC) deaminase, ACCD (ethylene reduction), and the broccoli plant elicitor peptide BoPep4 (salinity stress tolerance), triggered by green light signals when stress is detected. A hollow-fiber membrane ensures biological containment. The membrane is designed to retain bacteria while allowing cell-free output containing the protectants to pass through.

Mission statement: ReLEAF strengthens agricultural resilience by enabling on-demand protection against combined biotic and abiotic stresses through an optogenetic bioreactor, shifting from reactive chemical management to proactive, precision crop defense while reducing environmental contamination.

Vision: Every farmer is a biomanufacturer.

Our vision is to empower every farmer to have their small bacteria factory for producing biostimulants that help plants withstand environmental stresses. Through our bioreactor system, we wish to reduce long bioprocessing times and challenges, so that farmers can benefit from science at a faster rate than the present.

Climate stresses & vulnerability of agriculture

In a world of climate change, where seasons shift dramatically, temperatures fluctuate uncontrollably, soil salinity escalates, and fungal outbreaks intensify, humanity is forced to choose between immediate technological adaptation or multi-system crop failure in modern agriculture. Abiotic stresses, primarily heat and soil salinity, already cause an estimated 50% of worldwide crop yield losses annually, and climate change further exacerbates the severity and frequency of these issues (Bray et al., 2000; Mittler, 2002). For example, a 1 degree Celsius rise in average growing season temperatures reduces rice yield by 6.2% (Lyman et al., 2013). Additionally, over 20% of the world’s irrigated farmland is already significantly affected by salinity, with an additional 3 hectares becoming unproductive every minute (Zhao et al., 2017). Small farmers produce approximately 70-80% of the global food supply, a proportion that rises to over 80% of all consumed food in many developing sub-regions (Ricciardi et al., 2018). The focus on small farmers is critical because they represent the backbone of local food sovereignty. In East Asia, including Taiwan, where over 90% of agricultural holdings are small-scale family operations averaging less than 1 hectare, these small farmers directly supply more than 80% of domestic fresh vegetables and fruits (Ministry of Agriculture, 2024). In Taiwan’s Dahu area, which produces about 90% of the country’s strawberries, rising temperatures and typhoon rainfall have been linked to lower first-period yields (Chang, 2020), while year-round tomato production in the subtropical lowlands is challenged by summer heat and a growing risk of salinity stress, with yield strongly suppressed even by short-term mild salinity in a highly heat-tolerant cultivar (Liu et al., 2014). For all the smallholder farmers that make up over 500 million smallholder farms, these staggering numbers mean that a single stress event can potentially wipe out an entire season’s income with no recovery mechanism or backup plan.

One of the key issues that make this crisis particularly acute is the combination of heat and salinity stress. Under field conditions, these stresses occur repeatedly, compounding cellular damage in ways that can’t be triggered by either stress independently. At the same time, plants that are weakened by stress can become vulnerable to pathogens, meaning that even a miniscule instance of salinity or heat can initiate a disease outbreak. Current agricultural systems are not equipped to handle such crises - either current solutions damage soil health and burden farmers with costs they can’t afford, or these solutions are reactive and too slow, only able to be applied after visible damage has occurred. Chemical solutions such as fungicides (ex. mancozeb, azoxystrobin, or copper based sprays) and synthetic fertilizers remain the most accessible and affordable remedies, but they only further destroy the conditions of the already stressed plants because they are only broadly applied after visible and often irreversible crop damage has already occurred.

Product description

Product usage and function

Our current bioreactor US$2,280

Our field-deployable bioreactor is a sealed system containing engineered Bacillus subtilis that uses a hollow-fiber membrane filtration system to deliver protectant molecules directly on the vegetable crops. By restricting all living bacteria inside the device while allowing only the secreted protectants to exit, it automates crop defense without the need for manual spraying or application. The device functions with minimal farmer intervention, requiring only occasional maintenance checks or culture-cartridge replacement when the bacterial culture needs replenishment. It is fully automated by an Arduino-based control unit, a circulation pump, a temperature sensor, a salinity sensor and a green and red LED optogenetic control system. This integrated tech stack continuously monitors environmental conditions and automatically activates specific stress-response modules the moment predefined thresholds are breached.

Leaf humidity sensor US$125

The leaf humidity sensor sits on the leaves of crops and tells us the humidity of the leaf surface, with one type of crop using one sensor. The sensor then sends its readings to our artificial intelligence of things (AIoT) system, which uses the readings to forecast when the crop is going to need protectant. By using the sensor data, the AIoT system also predicts upcoming weather conditions, while the sensor tells the user how the crop is doing. If the leaves show signs of stress before the forecast predicted, the AIoT system can bring production forward accordingly. The AIoT translates this multi-source data into a guidance for farmers without technical background understanding of the bioreactor. Together, the sensor and AIoT link conditions in the field directly to production in the bioreactor, ensuring protectant is available exactly when the crop needs it.

Our bacteria cartridge US$90

The bacterial culture replenishment system supplies a sealed Bacillus subtilis inoculum in a cartridge that fits the bioreactor’s filter and cartridge design. The cartridge drops into the reservoir port from above and opens automatically once it is seated, so the culture goes straight into the reservoir. There is no need to handle an open culture, and the bioreactor stays closed throughout. Each package includes two culture cartridges together with the main unit. Thus, a replacement is on hand from the first season. Since the culture arrives ready to use, specialized workers do not need a lab, special equipment, or any microbiology skills to prepare fresh culture themselves.

Our Field Console mobile app Free with product purchase

The app pairs up with the bioreactor and consolidates operational information into five modules: Overview, Field Setup, AI Forecast, Log, and Settings. The Overview page shows a prioritized list of required actions, a summary of the current AI forecast, real-time field and reactor measurements, a schematic of the red/green light-control system, and the status of consumables (medium and culture). The Field Setup module records the inputs from farmers regarding the farm location, the crops grown and the area allocated to each, and the numbers of soil sensors and bioreactors already installed. From this data, the system estimates the recommended equipment and its total cost. The AI Forecast module reports a stress risk level for each crop, the estimated time until stress onset based on the farmer’s location, the target quantity of protectant, and the quantity produced to date. Orders are collected in a cart accessible from the header. In the present version, all sensor readings are simulated for demonstration purposes.

Usage method

The steps to using ReLEAF’s product:

  1. Ask our team to survey your farm. We check how many soil types you have and whether conditions change from one area to another.
  2. Determine how many bioreactors and sensors will be needed to be able to be effective on all of the intended area.
  3. Obtain the bioreactor(s), install them at the farm, and connect them to a pre-existing distribution system or a new one if necessary.
  4. Insert the B. subtilis cartridge (replacements can be bought from a distributor)
  5. Turn on the device and use the AIoT screen, which will help with setup and data collection.
  6. Done!

Business model

What is a social enterprise business model?

A social enterprise, at its core, is a business model that aims to use commercial revenue to sustain its operations and advance a social mission, which makes it distinct from a non-governmental organization (NGO). A good example of this is USE-GH, or Ubuntu Social Enterprise-Ghana, which is actually one of the organizations that inspired the very first project pitch for this. USE-GH buys directly from farmers, reducing the intermediary fees they face. It also teaches farmers to process their raw materials into finished products, so they earn more from the same harvest. As can be seen, the aim is to be able to generate enough profit to continue running all while helping the community the social enterprise wants to support, which in our case is small farmers all over the world.

Why a social enterprise?

Our vision is to have a social impact to empower farmers, which is why we chose this business model instead of a traditional one. Additionally, we want small farmers, who often have difficulties affording high-tech products by themselves, to have the tech that they should. We do this through a gradual development of our business model to ensure that we can slowly expand our business and eventually become financially stable enough so that we can subsidise farmers on our own, like we intended to from the very start. We want to be able to preserve our vision in the best way possible while not becoming an NGO, as then operations would be difficult due to high costs.

SWOT analysis

In order to better understand where our advantages and weaknesses lie, we did a SWOT analysis, carefully considering our strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats.

Strengths

Our SWOT analysis highlights the strong potential of our project while weighing key challenges that must be addressed for successful implementation. The system’s primary strengths lie in its modular multi-chamber design, which allows for the targeted, light-triggered production of three specific protective proteins (LEA, ACCD, and BoPep4). Additionally, its cell-free delivery approach reduces biosafety and regulatory concerns, while its integration of electronic sensors and AI forecasting allows it to deliver precise protectant doses to both soil-based and hydroponic systems. These features provide a sustainable alternative to conventional chemical pesticides or outdated methods while minimizing environmental contamination.

Opportunities

Externally, the project is in a beneficial position due to the rapidly growing biocontrol market, increasing climate-related crop stress, and global support for sustainable agriculture. Additionally, many countries strictly regulate or are in the process of phasing out toxic chemical pesticides, which creates a strong push toward biological alternatives. This widens our market opportunity across both traditional field agriculture and high-tech hydroponic systems, increasing adoption potential amongst growers seeking sustainable solutions. Many companies around the world charge the same amount for microbe fermentation and sensing separately while our product is a two in one for the same price. Furthermore, since the Taiwan government is willing to give subsidies to local farmers, it makes our product more accessible to our stakeholders. Also, our product can be tailor made for different farmers from different backgrounds, which expands our market.

Weaknesses

However, the project also has several weaknesses. There could be potential protectant loss from being washed away by water. Additionally, the bioreactor might not work fast enough when met with a sudden mass onslaught of stress. Imperfections in the bioreactor could lead to dilution of the protectants, while biofouling inside the chambers may reduce protein yield and shorten the system's lifespan. The product may also face limitations in countries with strict organic certification requirements because organic certification standards exclude engineered inputs, so we do not target organic farms. Furthermore, farmers would need to learn how to replace cartridges, which could increase long-term maintenance costs and create an additional barrier to adoption. With the bioreactor, the hollow fibre membrane might get blocked, which can slow down or potentially prevent excretion.

Threats

Finally, several external threats could affect the success of the product. Unclear regulations surrounding cell-free agricultural biologics may delay approval, especially if the product falls between the classifications of a biological pesticide and a synthetic biology-derived compound. Farmer adoption may also be limited by unfamiliarity with the technology, concerns about cost-effectiveness, and reliance on traditional chemical pesticides. In addition, some farmers may view synthetic biology as too risky, while restrictions on organic certification could limit access to high-value organic farming markets. Lastly, the bioreactor may get destroyed by natural disasters such as the frequent earthquakes or typhoons in Taiwan.

PESTEL analysis

Political

Political factors create both challenges as well as opportunities. Government regulations on genetically modified organism (GMO) crops represent a significant barrier to market entry in regions with strict policies. However, this also presents us with potential pathways for government support through green-tech initiatives and agricultural subsidies, which could accelerate adoption rates while reducing development costs.

Economic

Economic considerations directly influence the market viability of our product. High fertilizer and pesticide prices drive demand for sustainable alternatives, which creates favorable market conditions for our bioreactor. Furthermore, global supply chain disruptions affecting chemical fertilizer delivery could actually strengthen demand for locally produced alternatives like our bioreactor, since farmers would face shortages and price spikes. At the same time, shifts in agricultural spending patterns may affect customers’ purchasing power.

Social

Social factors are critical to the success of our product. Public perception and acceptance of GMOs are relatively mixed, which requires strong communication strategies and transparency about the safety and benefits of our product. The growing consumer demand for eco-friendly farming practices aligns well with our value proposition, although we must navigate cultural attitudes toward agricultural innovation in different markets.

Technological

Technological advancement is also a key component. Rapid developments in AI and forecasting tools allow us to create competitive advantages through better performance as well as monitoring fields in real-time. Standardized manufacturing processes for hardware and sensors are able to reduce scaling barriers.

Environmental

Environmental pressures also pose benefits and risks. Severe climate threats including droughts, heat waves, and fungal disease create an immediate demand for crop resilience solutions. The global push to meet UN Sustainable Development Goals (particularly SDGs 2, 4, 10, 12, 15) reinforces the market need for our environmental solution while opening doors to funding focused on sustainability.

Legal

Legal and regulatory frameworks also require a lot of careful navigation. Environmental release and biosafety laws determine how our bioreactor can be deployed in the market, while regulatory limitations for engineered biological products in food are very complex across different institutions. However, at the same time, clear regulatory pathways could potentially accelerate approval timelines.

Current challenges and solutions

Current management methods often depend on repeated chemical spraying, manual monitoring, and frequent maintenance, leading to higher labor costs, chemical waste, environmental pollution, and inefficient use of resources. In addition, these traditional solutions are rarely tailored to the specific conditions of individual farms, making it difficult for farmers to identify the right time to intervene. By combining stress detection and treatment in a single platform, our technology reduces unnecessary chemical inputs, minimizes waste and runoff, lowers daily labor requirements, promotes faster plant recovery, and helps maintain more stable crop yields during challenging growing seasons.

Our solution

Unlike traditional agricultural inputs that rely on centralized chemical manufacturing and reactive spraying, our system introduces novel solutions. Instead of shipping chemical bottles to the field, we deploy an on-site, on-demand field factory directly to the farm. This bridges the gap left by standard solutions, which either rely on chemical inputs that cause runoff, basic biostimulants that lack automatic delivery, or passive alert sensors that identify stress but rely on farmers to dilute and mix protectants on their own. Our bioreactor uses engineered Bacillus subtilis to continuously evaluate crop conditions by pairing real-time field sensors with predictive stress forecasting. When environmental or biological threats pass a safety threshold, the device automatically synthesizes targeted protectants to address climate stress and plant disease precisely when and where intervention is required. Built with a flexible, modular design, the system can be adapted across different crop types and growing setups. It also takes into great consideration of biosafety, ensuring engineered bacteria remain safely contained inside the hardware to allow only active protectants to reach the crops. From our lab testing, Arabidopsis given ACC deaminase before salt stress held about 70% more chlorophyll than the Terrific Broth (TB) control, on one plate per condition. We predict that could be cross-applied to other crop types in the future with further testing. Furthermore, our solutions offer a cheaper solution and more competitive price when compared with other companies that offer similar options.

The global protectant industry is within the agricultural sector, valued at 77.17 billion USD, as rapid climate changes and a growing population demand for more and better crops, this market is rapidly expanding. This market was valued at 9.4B USD in 2024, and is projected to reach 22.8B by 2030, providing strong proof of future growth and hindering market failure. Because our product includes GMOs, it will face legal hurdles such as extensive testing, heavy usage restrictions, and close government surveillance, as several countries and key markets, including the European Union, Australia, and our home country, Taiwan, treat GMOs with much caution. This could also restrict how much of our product we can distribute throughout countries and in Taiwan, and make it so that the person buying our product needs a license to use it. Aside from existing legal challenges, there’s also existing stigma regarding synthetic organisms that may push farmers away from purchasing our product, as they fear it could negatively affect the environment or escape the containment of our bioreactor. The B. subtilis itself could also present a challenge in that its survival rate may be too low to truly be sustainable, causing a need for frequent switches and creating a high cost to using our product that will likely alienate our target demographic, who are smaller, sustainable farmers.

Strategic positioning

We know of no product on the market that responds in real time to the stresses faced by plants, where the protectants are only produced when stress reaches a threshold level and at the exact amount that is suitable for the farm it is used on. Unlike current solutions, which require farmers to determine when, where, and how much protectant to apply, our system automates these decisions. This type of customizability and specificity is unique to our product, and unlike current solutions which require farmers to constantly have to interpret when to use protectants, how much to use, and where to use it, risking overuse or underuse that not only harms plants, but also the environment. Additionally, because our bioreactor has a sensing, response, and production system all in one, our product also has competitive pricing in comparison to other similar products, which can price around 80,000 to 100,000NTD for just sensing or just protectant production. Our bioreactor and B. subtilis is also capable of responding to several specific types of stress, instead of just using one cure-all that could be unsuitable to fully tackle the biological effects of a certain stress type on various crops.

The densities come from the Tainan District Agricultural Research and Extension Station (2012). From these inputs, the model calculates the number of plants and how much enzyme each spray needs. It then works out the hardware the farm needs:

  • Bioreactors: about two per hectare. Each produces a 1 L batch every 16 hours, and two of them cover one hectare’s spray inside a two-day window at the dose this model assumes.
  • Sensors: one moisture sensor per crop type.
  • Cartridges: enough to cover either the protectant the farm uses or the six-month shelf life, whichever runs out first.

That fleet is sized from an assumed dose of 203 U of enzyme per hectare per spray, scaled by planting density. The model marks it as its largest assumption, because no dose has been tested on a crop.

Our Math Model reaches a different figure by a different route, and the two should be read side by side. It takes the volume actually sprayed on a treated plate, 1.67 mL over 78.5 cm², scales that rate over a hectare and applies a modelled rice canopy correction, giving 37.6 kU per hectare per spray: 185 times the dose assumed here, and about four 20-litre reactors per hectare instead of two one-litre ones. The two are not the same quantity. This model assumes a targeted dose delivered per tomato plant; the Math Model scales a whole-plate application rate across a rice field through a canopy factor that has never been measured. Neither has been validated in a field, and the Math Model calls its own figure the dose that worked rather than the smallest that works.

The prices below follow the dose assumed here. If the Math Model’s rate is the one that holds in the field, the reactors per hectare and the cost per hectare rise with it, and the break-even area moves. Settling which dose is right is what the tomato field trials above are for.

Director Wu pointed out costs we had left out: factory, labor, bacterial cultivation and waste management. We added them to the model. Each farmer can then see a Year 1 package price, which includes hardware and cartridges, and a lower yearly cost after that, when they only buy cartridges.

When we tested farm sizes from 0.5 to 20 ha, contribution stayed at about US$2,000 to 2,500 per hectare. This gives a single break-even target of about 226 ha, however many farms that area comes from.

Regulations

Taiwan presents a unique regulatory opportunity forming the foundation of our Phase 1 strategy. Taiwan has stringent restrictions against Living Modified Organisms (LMOs) and GMO field releases under the Ministry of Agriculture (MOA) and Animal and Plant Health Inspection Agency (APHIA). However, Taiwan’s regulatory framework contains a critical pathway: “if engineered bacteria never leave the sealed device, the system can be classified as ‘contained-use’ equipment rather than an environmental release of GMOs” (APHIA).

ReLEAF’s system is fundamentally cell-free delivery: non-living metabolic byproducts (protective proteins) are discharged into soil, not viable Bacillus subtilis cells. The engineered bacteria remain physically confined via the hollow-fiber membrane. By legally proving “absolute containment” to APHIA and MOA, we bypass the restrictive environmental-release framework and pursue a streamlined contained-use approval pathway.

Biosafety validation standards

Escape-frequency assays, hollow-fiber membrane integrity testing, physical housing durability testing, and field trial protocols demonstrating no environmental bacterial release during pilot deployment with 20–50 devices are all ways that we can prove the validity of our biosafety. We will partner with government-recognized testing units such as Taiwan Agricultural Research Institute (TARI) and Tainan District Agricultural Research & Extension Station to verify containment and provide the aforementioned regulatory credibility.

Organic farming and genetically modified organism (GMO) concerns

Organic farming presents another consideration for ReLEAF’s implementation onto the market, because organic certification standards strictly ban GMO usage, which makes farmers hesitant to use our product, even if the protectants produced already exist in plants, as the protectants are produced by a GMO.

We attempt to address GMO concerns through physical containment in our bioreactor, where a hollow-fiber membrane keeps our B. subtilis inside the production compartment and permits only our protectants to pass through. Despite that, we also understand that organic farmers are still unlikely to be our customers.

The distinction is particularly important across ReLEAF’s planned markets. Taiwan’s Organic Agriculture Promotion Act restricts GMOs and their products in organic production. Another example is the fact that the United States Department of Agriculture’s National Organic Program also excludes genetic engineering methods from organic production. In these markets, using a cell-free formulation may not overcome restrictions associated with the engineered production process. In the Philippines and Thailand, ReLEAF must likewise assess the relevant national organic standards and permitted-input requirements rather than assume that cell-free delivery guarantees eligibility.

For this reason, ReLEAF will initially prioritize sustainable and conventional agricultural markets and not approach organic farmers for regulatory concerns. Where necessary, ReLEAF will seek written determinations from, or consult, the competent authorities and organic certification bodies before making claims about suitability for certified organic farms.

Potential risks

Biological risks

Market and adoption risks

Hollow-fiber membrane leakage

ProbabilityLow-Medium

ImpactMedium (regulatory consequence, requires containment re-verification)

Description

Membrane’s molecular-weight cutoff could be miscalibrated, damaged during handling, or degraded over device’s operational lifetime, allowing whole Bacillus subtilis cells (0.5–4 micrometers) to pass into the environment.

Mitigation

Experimentally validate membrane cutoff against Bacillus subtilis cell size under multiple conditions and accelerated degradation testing under simulated field conditions (temperature cycling, moisture exposure, mechanical stress). Build membrane integrity checks into formal maintenance and replacement schedules. Conduct simple visual/functional tests monthly with farmers or extension officers.

Physical containment breach

ProbabilityLow

ImpactHigh (most direct pathway to uncontrolled environmental release)

Description

Sealed bioreactor housing could crack, degrade, or be damaged during handling, transport, or extended field use, exposing internal bacterial compartment directly to soil.

Mitigation

Stress-test housing for durability under realistic field conditions (moisture, temperature cycling, mechanical handling, soil microbes). Design bioreactor compartment to show visible proof of tampering or breach so any integrity failure is immediately detectable. Emphasize in farmer training and maintenance protocols that damaged devices must be returned to collection points. Redundancy with genetic and membrane containment means single breach alone is unlikely to cause bacterial escape.

Farmer distrust of unfamiliar technology

ProbabilityMedium

ImpactMedium (slower early market penetration)

Description

Farmers accustomed to familiar chemical sprays may distrust sealed biological devices, especially if technology seems difficult to understand or maintain. Slow adoption in the pilot phase could limit early market traction.

Mitigation

Clear, farmer-centric messaging avoiding overly technical language. Emphasize practical benefits: fewer spray days, reduced crop loss during heat/wet seasons, decreased daily field monitoring labor. Prioritize pilot deployments with progressive agricultural cooperatives and extension offices interested in precision agriculture and IoT systems. Generate local testimonials and case studies before broader sales.

Slow adoption by cooperatives and extension networks

ProbabilityMedium

ImpactMedium (distribution delays despite scientific validation)

Description

Cooperatives, NGOs, and government agricultural extension offices may be cautious about adopting novel agricultural inputs without long-term, multi-season evidence.

Mitigation

Prioritize institutions already oriented toward innovation. Design pilot trials as small, tightly controlled, outcome-focused engagements with clear success metrics: measurable crop-loss reduction, documented decrease in fungicide spray events, farmer satisfaction above defined thresholds. Deliver tangible, documented wins in first season to build credibility for longer-term scaling.

Production cost exceeds farmer budget

ProbabilityMedium

ImpactHigh (severely limits market penetration and farmer affordability)

Description

Unit cost of bioreactor device, cartridges, hollow-fiber membranes, and bacterial culture may become prohibitively expensive, limiting adoption among smallholder farmers even if technology is effective.

Mitigation

Comprehensive financial sensitivity analysis testing whether ReLEAF remains viable if production costs are 20% higher or selling prices are 20% lower. If direct farmer purchase is unaffordable, pivot to alternative business models: cooperative purchasing (multiple farmers share device ownership), NGO bulk purchase and subsidized distribution, seasonal rental models, or cartridge subscription systems. These flexible approaches ensure cost barriers do not prevent technology adoption.

Organic farming compatibility & regulatory restrictions

Description

Organic agriculture represents a potential market for agricultural technologies, but organic certification standards regulate not only the composition of agricultural products but, in some jurisdictions, the methods used to manufacture them. ReLEAF produces protective proteins and peptides using genetically engineered Bacillus subtilis inside a contained bioreactor. Although the system is designed to retain viable production bacteria behind a 0.2 μm hollow-fiber membrane and deliver a cell-free output, this design does not automatically establish eligibility for certified organic agriculture.

Regulatory overview by target market
  • Taiwan: The Organic Agriculture Promotion Act restricts genetically modified organisms and their products in organic production. ReLEAF must clarify whether its proteins produced using engineered bacteria qualify as prohibited GMO-derived products or can be accepted under the applicable input requirements.
  • Philippines: Organic agriculture is governed by Republic Act No. 10068, as amended by Republic Act No. 11511, and applicable Philippine National Standards. The eligibility of ReLEAF's cell-free formulation and engineered manufacturing process requires confirmation with the relevant authorities and certification bodies.
  • Thailand: The national organic agriculture standard, TAS 9000-2564, establishes requirements for organic production and permitted inputs. ReLEAF must determine whether the production method and final formulation satisfy the applicable restrictions.
  • United States: USDA National Organic Program rules exclude genetic engineering methods from organic production. The use of engineered microorganisms to manufacture the active proteins could therefore prevent organic eligibility even if viable cells are absent from the final product.
  • European Union: Regulation (EU) 2018/848 explicitly restricts GMOs and products produced from or by GMOs in organic production. Because ReLEAF's protective proteins are manufactured using engineered bacteria, this represents a substantial potential barrier to organic-market entry.
Mitigation
  • Prioritize conventional agricultural markets initially. ReLEAF will focus on farmers and agricultural partners whose applicable product-registration requirements can be met without relying on organic certification. Conventional market authorization will be pursued separately from any organic-use claim.
  • Obtain country-specific regulatory determinations. Consult the relevant agricultural authorities and accredited organic certification bodies in the planned market-entry sequence: Taiwan, the Philippines, Thailand, the United States, the European Union.
  • Maintain comprehensive product and manufacturing documentation. Generate evidence of viable-cell retention, membrane integrity, residual genetic material, product composition, and manufacturing controls. These data support biosafety and quality assessments but do not override restrictions on GMO-derived products.
  • Investigate organic-compatible alternatives. Where commercially justified, assess alternative production methods or formulations that may satisfy relevant organic standards. Any proposed alternative must be evaluated independently rather than assumed to resolve the regulatory barrier.
  • Avoid unsupported organic claims. ReLEAF will not advertise its product as suitable for certified organic farming until the applicable requirements have been confirmed and satisfied.

Strategic rationale: ReLEAF is not initially targeting certified organic farms because the regulatory status of agricultural inputs manufactured using genetically engineered microorganisms remains a significant market-access uncertainty, particularly in Taiwan, the United States, and the European Union. Focusing first on conventional agriculture allows ReLEAF to validate product efficacy, demonstrate containment, establish production economics, and build farmer confidence without making commercial viability dependent on organic-input approval. Organic agriculture remains a potential future market, subject to country-specific regulatory review and certification eligibility.

Frequently asked questions

How does ReLEAF work?

A sensor in your field sends data to our AIoT system, which also forecasts upcoming stress. When stress is detected or forecast, the system uses optogenetic (light) control to trigger our engineered B. subtilis to produce the protectant. The protectant passes through a membrane that keeps the bacteria inside the bioreactor, so only the protectant reaches your crop.

Which crops is ReLEAF best for?

ReLEAF is not tied to one crop. Published work on our three types of protectant covers wheat (LEA; Yang et al., 2022), wheat, maize, rice, pea and tomato (ACC deaminase; Shahid et al., 2023) and broccoli (BoPep4; Wang et al., 2022). Most of this work tests salt tolerance, and none of it uses our product. We test on the model plant Arabidopsis because the iGEM rules only allow us to work with it.

What stress does it protect against?

ReLEAF targets heat, salinity and biotic stress (pathogenic infection), which are among the biggest causes of crop loss worldwide. We have run experiments on both heat and salinity in Arabidopsis. See our Plant page for the results.

How many bioreactors and sensors do I need?

About 2 bioreactors per hectare (1 for up to 0.5 ha), plus 1 sensor per crop type. Denser crops need more bioreactors. Our online calculator works it out for your farm.

What does ReLEAF cost?

A 1 L bioreactor costs about US$2,277 (NT$72,200), a moisture sensor about US$125 (NT$4,000), and a refill cartridge about US$90 (NT$2,840). A typical 1 ha farm growing 3 crop types needs 2 bioreactors, 3 sensors and 1 cartridge a year. It pays about US$5,531 (NT$175,400) in the first year, including freight, set-up visits and warranty, and then about US$90 (NT$2,840) a year for refills. These are planning estimates, not quotes.

Is it safe for my soil?

Our bacteria stay sealed inside the bioreactor, and only the protectant reaches your crop and soil. A hollow fiber membrane filters out the bacteria, a droplet sensor detects any liquid that spills inside the bioreactor, and a pressure sensor keeps the pressure constant so the membrane does not break. From the hardware page, it shows that our bacteria does not leak out from the bioreactor.

Has it been tested on a farm?

Not yet. We have lab results only: the enzyme is active, and treated Arabidopsis recovered better from heat and salinity stress. Our first field data will come from four pilot farms.

How is it different from biofertilizers?

Biofertilizers feed or boost plants on a schedule. ReLEAF protects against stress, and a field sensor tells you when to spray.

How often do I refill or replace parts?

About 1 cartridge a year. The bioreactor and sensor are one-time purchases.

How much work is it, and do I need special skills?

You replace a cartridge about once a year, and apply the protectant to your crop about 9 times in a 4-month tomato season depending on the frequency of stress events happened. We are exploring connecting the system to the irrigation system so spraying is automatic. If you have a drone, you can dilute the protectant according to our guideline and spray with it.

Can I use it on an organic farm?

No. Organic farms cannot use engineered bacteria or their products, even when the bacteria are sealed inside the bioreactor. Taiwan's Organic Agriculture Promotion Act defines organic farming as not using genetically modified organisms and their products (Organic Agriculture Promotion Act, 2018, Art. 3), and bars operators from using inputs that contain genetically modified products (Organic Agriculture Promotion Act, 2018, Art. 15).
