Abstract

We claimed engineering successes across four stages of ReLEAF; this page is where each claim is made checkable, with the principle, the full protocol and the raw numbers behind it.

The plant assays put Arabidopsis on half-strength MS agar and in hydroponic culture so that stress dose, protectant and timing move one at a time, and they return root length, fresh weight and chlorophyll. The light plate was matched LED by LED before it drove anything. The protectant assays follow the protein from plasmid to working enzyme: which voltage transforms B. subtilis, whether a band of the right size leaves the cell, how much of it there is, and whether it turns substrate over. The bioreactor instruments read OD600 in the loop without drawing a sample, and pressure and flow across the membrane. Where a measurement failed we say so: the 14 August pressure series does not repeat, no transmembrane pressure has yet been computed, and heat stress in hydroponics flattened the treated plants along with the controls.

Overview

In our project, we claimed several engineering successes. However, how do we make sure what we see is truly a success? In this section, we need to make sure that what we do is credible and reproducible.

Can our engineered Bacillus subtilis detect plant stress and respond by producing and releasing the appropriate plant protectant at the right moment? How well does the protectant aid in plant growth?

That question splits into four measurable pieces, and the four families below answer them in order. Each one is reported the same way: how the measurement works, what it is useful for outside this project, the protocol in enough detail to repeat, and what it returned, including the runs that failed.

The four are not independent. Plant assays

Four measurements share two growing platforms. The agar assay sets the stress and the dose on a flat surface; the hydroponic assay puts the same protectants in front of a root system standing in liquid. The chlorophyll extraction reads how much photosynthetic machinery survived either one, and the physical measurements give the root length and the fresh weight that every chlorophyll number is normalised against.

Agar plate stress test, horizontal and vertical

Our project mainly discusses whether an engineered Bacillus subtilis can supply the right plant protectant at the right moment and how much that protectant will help plant grow and further leads to better crop yield for farmers in real life application. To know whether a protectant truly has a rescue effect on a plant and whether timing matters, we built a plant measurement on Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings grown on ½ Murashige and Skoog (MS) agar (Murashige & Skoog, 1962). It needed to be a controlled and repeatable system where the stress dose, protectant types, and the timing could each be changed one at a time.

Salinity stress damages plants in readable ways. Exposure of Arabidopsis to NaCl alters stress-response gene expression (Cheng et al., 2013; Rolly et al., 2020) and, at the whole-seedling level, stunts root growth, bleaches leaves through loss of chlorophyll (chlorosis), and turns leaves purple through anthocyanin accumulation. Stress ethylene contributes to these effects as it inhibits root elongation (Růžička et al., 2007) and impairs photosynthesis (Ceusters & Van De Poel, 2018), which is the reason why the bacterial enzyme ACC deaminase, which breaks down the ethylene precursor ACC, is one of our protectants (Glick, 2014). With the damage showing up in root length differences, biomass and leaf pigment, a protectant that works should be able to lengthen roots, raise biomass, and preserve chlorophyll compared with a stressed and untreated plate.

We chose agar for three reasons. Firstly, every seedling receives identical nutrients and an exact stress dose, which is hard to guarantee in soil as it contains more uncontrollable variables such as ununiformed moisture levels, pH, and minerals. Secondly, early physiological changes appear within days of observation, allowing many conditions to be screened quickly. Thirdly, it establishes clear baselines before testing in complex soil systems for salinity concentrations that can easily demonstrate stressed environments, protectant concentrations that has obvious rescue effectiveness on plants, and protocol validations.

Vertical square plates

The plates are designed to let roots grow along the surface under gravity, so primary root length and lateral roots can be measured from photographs. We used them for the trehalose experiments in Phase 1 and planned them for the heat tests.

Horizontal circular plates

The plates are designed to hold liquid protectant on the agar surface, which vertical plates cannot fulfill. We used them for the CH bio peptide and ACCD experiments in Phases 2 and 3, where treatment timing of salinity was a variable (pre-treatment, co-treatment, after-treatment). Horizontal circular plates also provide higher capacities of n values in each trial which minimizes inconsistencies; for example, vertical square plates can allow the testing of around 9 to 10 Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings while horizontal circular plates can hold around 40 seedlings.

The experiments ran in three phases, ordered by how well each protectant was understood, commercially used, and researched so that each phase checked the system before the next relied on it. Phase 1 used trehalose, a disaccharide reported to protect Arabidopsis under salinity stress (Kim et al., 2025), to find a NaCl level that gives a readable signal and to confirm the platform can show a rescue. Phase 2 used a commercial peptide (CH Biotech bio peptide) as a positive control and to test application timing. Phase 3 used ACC deaminase, LEA, and BoPEP4 secreted by our engineered Bacillus subtilis. A heat-stress version of the assay, based on published thermopriming protocols (Lee et al., 2023; Auverlot et al., 2025), was designed for the same protectants.

What the platform is useful for

Seedlings on agar are one of the most widely used formats in plant biology and the platform described here applies beyond our project in several ways.

  • Screening for salt-tolerance of plants. Arabidopsis grown on agar supplemented with NaCl is a standard way to test how genes, mutants, or treatments change salt tolerance (Cheng et al., 2013; Rolly et al., 2020). Our dose-response data tested salinity concentrations of 75, 100, 150 and 250 mM NaCl, read against the control group on the same plate. At 75 mM, fresh weight and total chlorophyll stayed near control levels. At 100 mM growth was clearly reduced and the rescue effect was still visible, which is what makes it the working concentration. At 150 mM growth was stunted to about the same degree as at 100 mM but with little rescue, total chlorophyll fell sharply, and anthocyanin accumulated enough to purple the leaves. At 250 mM close to every sample showed chlorosis and severe bleaching and none recovered. The boundaries are approximate percentages of control and not fixed thresholds, because each trial differed. The discovery allows future researchers to skip the pretests and begin in the 75 to 100 mM window where growth is reduced but rescue is still visible.
  • Testing plant growth-promoting bacteria and biostimulants. Microbes that lower plant ethylene with ACC deaminase are used to promote plant growth under stress (Glick, 2014). Plate assays are a common way to test bacterial effects on Arabidopsis stress tolerance (Lee et al., 2023). Our design compares bacterial products at defined timings and with equal liquid controls. The standardized protocols allow a reusable screen for any protectant testing, so other teams and researchers can test their own molecule or microbe with the same readouts, plates, and analysis.
  • Testing exogenous protective compounds. Sugars such as trehalose have been tested on Arabidopsis thaliana under salinity stress (Kim et al., 2025). Our trehalose sets show how to find the usable dose range of a candidate compound. Too high of concentrations might inhibit and harm plants as well because it triggers the toxic accumulation of an intermediate signaling metabolite, trehalose-6-phosphate (T6P), which disrupts normal carbon allocation and energy signaling, causing growth arrest such as stunted roots and undeveloped leaves (Schluepmann et al., 2004).
  • Heat-stress assays. The same plates and readouts can be used with thermopriming protocols (Lee et al., 2023; Auverlot et al., 2025).
  • Timing studies for delivery driven by sensors. The protectant is applied before, with, or after stress; therefore, the platform can test whether early delivery matters. This directly connects to the question behind AIoT-based stress prediction and sensing in the field.
  • Teaching and iGEM labs. The equipment is inexpensive (plates, a camera in a light box, a balance, and free ImageJ software) and every step is described below in the procedural steps.
  • Honest record of failure modes. Especially for contamination issues or failure to see effectiveness in protectant doses, so other teams can avoid repeating them as to also provide improvements and suggestions to protocols for higher quality readouts.

Growth procedure medium, sterilization, stratification and the daily record
Neutral ½ MS medium agar preparation for stratification
  1. Prepare the ½ MS medium powder without sucrose, MES buffer, distilled water, sucrose (1%) and agar powder in the amounts in the materials table.
  2. Add the contents above into a flask.
  3. Use a hot plate to stir, with a magnetic stirring rod, until no sediments are left in the solution.
  4. Use the pH probe and measure the pH of the solution (target pH is 5.7 to 5.8). If the pH is too low, add 0.1 M KOH slowly to increase the pH. If the pH is too high, add 0.1 M HCl slowly to decrease the pH.
  5. Wrap the neck of the flask with aluminum foil and stick a piece of indicator tape on top.
  6. Autoclave for 20 minutes at 127 °C. Wait for the pressure to reach 0 before retrieving the flask.
  7. During the autoclave, label all of the petri dishes with a marker in the biosafety cabinet. Include agar percentage and date the agar was made.
  8. Transfer half of the flasks to a laminar flow hood. Store the other half of the flasks in the hot bath at 60 °C while pouring so that the agar solution does not solidify immediately.
  9. Make sure the surface is dry by wiping the flasks with a paper towel before spraying 70% EtOH on them.
  10. Pour each of the medium mixtures into their petri dishes (around 30 mL for horizontal round plates and 50 mL for vertical square plates).
  11. Transfer the other half of the flasks into the laminar flow hood and pour each of the medium mixtures into the petri dishes.
  12. Let the petri dishes solidify at room temperature (around 25 °C) for around 10 minutes under the laminar flow hood with the plastic lid on.
Seed sterilization and germination
  1. Surface-sterilize seeds, then rinse with sterile water.
  2. Sow seeds on neutral ½ MS agar plates: 40 seeds per plate on the horizontal round plates and 33 seedlings per plate on the vertical square plates, following the seed templates.
  3. Use breathable tape to seal each of the agar plates.
  4. Wrap the agar plates with aluminum foil.
  5. Stratify in the dark at 4 °C. Stratification synchronizes germination so seedlings are uniform and have consistent growth conditions.
  6. Move plates to the growth chamber. Grow for 4 days (horizontal round plates) or 7 days (vertical square plates) before protectant dosing and salinity concentration stress trials.

The sterilizing agent, its concentration and contact time, the number of rinses, and the growth chamber's temperature, light intensity and photoperiod are not recorded.

Recording every day

Vertical square plates. Observe for 10 days. Set up the black background platform and turn on the lights. Take the agar plates one by one out of the growth chamber and place them on the black background platform, bottom lid facing up. Take pictures, clearly recording the labels, with no reflections, avoiding water vapor covering the view of the plants. Place the agar plates back, tilted slightly to the back on the rack, in the growth chamber.

Horizontal round plates. Observe for 10 days. Take the agar plates one by one out of the growth chamber. Spray 70% EtOH on the agar plates and place them in the laminar flow hood. Unwrap the breathable tape. Place the agar plates on a black background platform, open lid, leaves facing up; on the last day, take out the lid and take the picture directly from the top view and measure fresh weight and chlorophyll data afterwards. Wrap a phone in plastic wrap and spray 70% EtOH before placing the phone inside the laminar flow hood. Take pictures, clearly recording the labels. Wrap breathable tape around the agar plates and place them back in the growth chamber.

Stress assays the vertical and horizontal plate assays, step by step
Vertical plate assay (Phase 1, trehalose)
  1. Take the stack of agar plates wrapped in aluminum foil out of the refrigerator.
  2. Spray alcohol on the agar plates before placing them inside the laminar flow hood.
  3. Use a marker to draw a straight baseline along a ruler 5.5 cm from the top of the plate on the back of each salinity plate.
  4. With an alcohol marker, mark 9 dots along the baseline in equal distances. The square agar plate is 12 cm horizontally with 10 spaces between the borders of the plate and between each seedling, so 12/10 = 1.2 cm as distance between each seedling.
  5. Place the square plates vertically at a slight angle.
  6. Choose 9 germinated seeds after vernalization that have similar root lengths or germination status and align the endpoints of their roots to the line drawn on the plate.
  7. Seal the plates with breathable tape and label the incubation date with a marker before placing the vertical plates on the stacks in the growth chamber, slightly tilted towards the back.
Horizontal plate assay (Phases 2 and 3), heat stress

Transferring the seedlings

  1. Take the stack of agar plates wrapped in aluminum foil out of the refrigerator.
  2. Spray alcohol on the agar plates before placing them inside the laminar flow hood.
  3. Draw a straight line along a ruler 5.5 cm from the top of the plate, using the seed template.
  4. Place the plates at a slight angle.
  5. Choose 12 germinated seeds of similar root length or germination status and align the endpoints of their roots to the drawn line.
  6. Set up four groups: control, pre-treatment one day before, co-treatment on day 0, and after-treatment one day after.
  7. Seal the plates with breathable tape, label the transfer date, and place them on the rack in the growth chamber, tilted slightly towards the back.

Applying the treatments

  1. Pre-treatment, one day before. Open the biosafety cabinet. Dilute the CH peptide 1/500, that is 19.96 mL of sterile ddH₂O to 40 µL of peptide, in a 50 mL centrifuge tube and fit a sprayer. Take out the plate labelled pre-treatment, hold it vertically, and spray the peptide over the plants from leaves to roots at a distance of 15 cm, about two sprays, making sure every plant is covered. Seal with breathable tape and return it to the growth chamber.
  2. Co-treatment, day 0. Take out the plate labelled co-treatment, hold it vertically, and spray evenly at 15 cm, about two sprays. Seal with breathable tape.
  3. Place all four groups on a rack, plates standing at a slight angle, and put the rack into the incubator at 37 °C under white light for thermopriming.
  4. Observe the seedlings for a further 5 days in the incubator.
  5. After-treatment, one day after thermopriming. Take the after-treatment plate out of the incubator, hold it vertically, spray evenly at 15 cm, seal with breathable tape and return it to the incubator.

The salinity version of the horizontal assay follows the same four timing arms, with 1 mL of NaCl solution applied to the agar surface in place of the temperature change.

Hydroponic stress test

We transplanted Arabidopsis into a hydroponic box to evaluate the efficacy of protectants on plants. We use hydroponics to test the effectiveness because we can control the environmental factors precisely. First, the agar in the lid and the medium in the hydroponic box ensure a complete nutrient supply for the plants. We also control humidity and temperature in a constant range to fix the outer conditions that may affect the results of experiments.

Agar settles the dose and the timing on a flat surface; hydroponics is where the same protectants meet a root system standing in liquid, which is the condition a field application has to work in. The two platforms share their readouts, so a number taken here can be set beside a number taken on a plate. What hydroponics does not give is root length: the roots grow into the well and cannot be photographed against a scale, so the readouts are fresh weight, chlorophyll and the daily photographs.

Two vessels

Transparent tip box. After testing several prototypes of the hydroponic box, we settled on a 1000 µL tip box with a customized 3D printed board. The box is transparent, which lets us watch the plants and holds thirteen Arabidopsis seedlings. We used the tip box for the pretests of salinity and heat stress rescue by the CH peptide.

Tube rack. We first used the tube rack only to grow seedlings before an experiment began, then began running experiments in it directly. One rack holds forty-eight plants, enough to test ACCD and BoPep4 against both salinity and heat stress on a single rack. We moved to the rack because of time, and the plants in it grew at least as well as those in the tip box with less contamination. The build history of both vessels is on Plants; what follows is the measurement they carry.

What the platform is useful for

A reproducible hydroponic cultivation system. Hydroponics provides a stable environment for plant research by fixing nutrient availability, water purity and stress intensity. We tested two systems, the tip box and the tube rack, and record the materials, the assembly, the nutrient medium and the environmental conditions for both, so another team can rebuild either one.

A salinity-stress protocol. We took the salt concentration from Cortés-Jiménez et al. (2014). After several pretests, 100 mM NaCl is the concentration at which plants are damaged and can still be rescued by a protectant.

A heat-stress protocol. We followed the heat-stress design of Lee et al. (2023) and exposed plants at 37 °C and 42 °C. The groups at 42 °C did not survive the day, while the groups at 37 °C were rescued by the CH peptide. A team setting up a heat assay can start from 37 °C and treat 42 °C as past the point of recovery.

A working dilution range for a bacterial supernatant. We tested 1/40, 1/60, 1/80 and 1/100 dilutions of both protectants. The plants tolerate all four, and 1/40, 1/60 and 1/80 are the three the team reads as giving the best rescue, ACCD under heat and BoPep4 under both heat and salinity. The finding another team can take without qualification is the ceiling: the carrier medium, and not the protectant, sets the strongest dose a plant will tolerate.

A contamination finding. Plants grown in the tube rack were contaminated less often than plants in the tip box. Our working explanation is the transfer step: tip box plants were moved from the rack at four days old, and although a group would usually survive three to four days afterwards, most ended in severe contamination. Plants left in the rack never have their roots handled.

A record of four boxes that did not work. We troubleshot four hydroponic boxes before the fifth, a 1000 µL tip box with a 3D printed board and a 3D printed black cover to keep light off the medium. The tip box is less stable than the tube rack, and we still offer the fifth design as a prototype other teams can modify.

Finding a dilution the plants survive

TB at full strength kills the plants, so the first run was a pretest on TB medium carrying B. subtilis and no protectant, to separate the carrier from anything the protectant might do. We tested 1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 and 1/16, with 0 as the control, at pH 5.7 and pH 7. The optimum for Arabidopsis is 5.7 and ACCD works best between 8.0 and 8.5, which Arabidopsis will not tolerate, so 7 is the compromise we tested against.

Every TB concentration in the pretest was too strong for the plants to survive, including 1/16. That is a failed rescue window, and it is still the reading that set the next run: the damage falls off steadily with dilution, so the usable range had to be below 1/16.

The salinity run that followed tested ACCD at 1/10, 1/20, 1/40 and 1/60 against 0 ACCD, each with and without 100 mM NaCl, and carried a separate group of 1/20 TB alone at both salt levels. We had expected 1/20 to be dilute enough for the plants to live and 1/40 and 1/60 to be almost without effect.

The expectation was wrong in one place and right in the other. 1/20 TB was still too much for the plants, and ACCD at 1/10 and 1/20 was not dilute enough for them to survive; 1/40 and 1/60 were tolerable. We therefore used 1/40 TB as the strongest dose in the heat runs, and these two runs together narrowed the working range from undiluted TB to 1/40 through 1/100.

Chlorophyll extraction

To assess the physiological response of Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings under stress, and evaluate protectant treatment rescue effectiveness in our project, chlorophyll extraction is introduced as a precise tool of measurement to strengthen the analysis of each experiment's results.

Chlorophyll is an essential pigment in the photosynthetic machinery of plants that plays a pivotal role in converting light energy into glucose. Ultimately, varying chlorophyll levels impact plant growth (Roberts, 2023). The metabolic pathways that govern the chlorophyll biosynthesis are complex, which abiotic stress such as salinity stress, drought, extreme temperature variations can substantially perturb these processes (Li et al., 2024).

As connected to our project, we mainly focus on two abiotic stress types: salinity and heat. Given this category, these abiotic stressors trigger the overproduction of reactive oxygen species, leading to lipid peroxidation and chlorosis. Hence, extracting the chlorophyll as a form of data readout and measurement allows quantification on the degree of chlorosis, and permits accurate evaluation of plant physiological response in our experiments that cannot be visually and directly assessed through bare eyes.

Optogenetic control

Can the light plate deliver a controlled signal for optogenetic activation? ReLEAF depends on light as the external control signal connecting environmental prediction to biological production. We therefore tested the light plate to determine whether it could provide reproducible illumination across the culture area.

A predicted environmental condition is turned into a light input that tunes production of ACCD in B. subtilis through the CcaSR red and green light-sensing system (Castillo-Hair et al., 2019). For that to work, light has to act as a reliable dial. We need to know whether low, medium and high intensities give clearly separated levels of ACCD expression, and whether the light itself affects cell growth. The Light Plate Apparatus is an open-hardware, 24-well platform with independently programmable LEDs in every well (Gerhardt et al., 2016), so several intensities of red and green can run side by side in one experiment on one culture. Following the established CcaSR protocol for B. subtilis (Haller et al., 2025), we track the response over time with OD600 and Western blot. The settings that give a reproducible, graded response become the intensity levels ReLEAF uses to control ACCD production, and because every conclusion depends on the plate, the plate is what we characterized first.

What the measurement contributes

The Light Plate Apparatus is a general platform for characterizing light-inducible gene expression, not a CcaSR instrument. A team can survey light-responsive genetic modules beyond CcaSR and fit LEDs matched to each module's activating wavelength, which turns the same plate into a characterization tool for a wide range of optogenetic systems. What we add is the step before that: a worked method for matching cheap single-bulb LEDs by measured output, so the wells in one column receive the same light and the columns are genuinely different from one another.

Matching the LEDs before trusting the plate

Internal resistor variance is a common problem in cheap, single-bulb LEDs. By individually measuring the lumen output value of each LED, we were able to determine which of them have matching internal resistance. Upon finding these similarities among our purchased LEDs, we are able to form 3 groups of 4 LEDs to form columns within our LPA structure for each wavelength we use (we use 2). This will allow us to use mathematical calculations to recalibrate all of the columns of lumens to match, so that we can later create low, medium, and high intensity light to create variance for our B. subtilis protectant production testing.

Protectant assays

Five measurements follow the protein from the plasmid to the working enzyme. Electroporation decides how the construct gets into the chassis at all. The Western blot asks whether a protein of the right size leaves the cell. The ELISA puts a concentration on it. The Berthelot and DNPH assays read the two products of the reaction the enzyme is supposed to catalyse, and so are the only two that say whether the protein does anything. A concentration and an activity answer different questions and are not interchangeable, which is why both are here.

Bacillus subtilis electroporation

To successfully engineer Bacillus subtilis to produce and secrete bioprotectants (such as ACC deaminase, LEA, and BoPEP4), foreign plasmid constructs carrying these genetic sequences must be efficiently introduced into the host cells.

Electroporation is the application of a high-voltage electrical pulse across the cell membrane which creates transient physical pores that permit plasmid DNA uptake. While standard transformation methods in Bacillus subtilis often suffer from strain-dependent variable efficiency, electroporation provides a more controllable, high-efficiency technique critical for expressing recombinant stress-mitigating proteins (Duckert et al., 2022).

In our project, the experimental protocol uses electrocompetent B. subtilis cells and evaluates transformation under different electroporation conditions. Because electroporation efficacy can be influenced by the electrical parameters used, different voltages were tested while maintaining the designated resistance and capacitance for each strain. Following electroporation, cells were allowed to recover for 3 hours before being plated on selective LB agar. Successful transformants were identified based on colony formation under antibiotic selection, and colony abundance was used as an indicator for comparing transformation efficiency between experimental conditions.

Efficiency in B. subtilis is governed by strain physiology as much as by the applied field, so optimization means tailoring both the cell preparation and the pulse. The two strains differ in the density at which they are harvested, the osmoprotectant buffer and the wash steps: 168 after the DTU-Denmark iGEM team (2015) and WB800 after Lu et al. (2012). Evaluating transformant recovery across a range of peak voltages identifies the field strengths that maximise plasmid uptake without lysing the cells.

The purpose of this experiment was therefore to evaluate bacterial growth following electroporation and identify voltage conditions that produced comparatively high numbers of colonies. The results provide a basis for selecting suitable electroporation conditions for future plasmid transformation and genetic engineering of B. subtilis.

What the measurement contributes

Strain-specific electrocompetent cell protocols. We wrote up separate preparation workflows for B. subtilis 168, after the DTU-Denmark iGEM team (2015), and for WB800, after Lu et al. (2012). They differ in the OD600 at harvest, the washing buffer, the number of washes and the resuspension volume, and those differences are the reason a protocol written for one strain does not transfer to the other.

An optimal voltage for each strain and construct. Comparing voltages at a fixed resistance and capacitance identified the settings that gave the highest colony yields. For strain 168, 2.1 kV returned more colonies than 2.3 kV. For WB800 carrying the CcaSR construct, 2.3 kV gave the highest yield at 370 colonies, and 2.5 kV reduced it.

Evidence of where the voltage starts killing cells. Colony counts fall at the top of each range, which is what excessive field strength and cell lysis look like, and sets an upper limit for these strains. A team can use it to avoid spending cells and plasmid on a pulse that destroys both.

To verify whether the engineered B. subtilis could successfully produce and secrete protein or peptide products, we performed a Western blot to detect the secreted protein via the His tag we added at the C-terminus.

Western blotting separates proteins by molecular weight using polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, transfers them onto a PVDF membrane, and uses a specific antibody to visualize the target. Standard quantification assays return a total; they cannot tell our protein from the host's. Competitive His-tag ELISA

To evaluate whether our Bacillus subtilis successfully secretes bioprotectants, the amount of protein in culture samples must be accurately measured. Total protein assays cannot distinguish the target from host proteins, and gel-based methods are only semi-quantitative.

All three of our proteins carry a polyhistidine tag, so an immunoassay against the tag gives a quantitative and independent measure of expression. It is sensitive, and it processes many samples in parallel, which is what comparing strains, constructs and culture conditions needs.

ELISA uses antigen–antibody interactions to detect or quantify a molecule, and comes in four main formats. A direct ELISA uses an enzyme-labelled primary antibody to detect an antigen. An indirect ELISA uses an enzyme-labelled secondary antibody to detect a bound primary antibody, which is the usual way to detect antibodies. A sandwich ELISA captures an antigen between two antibodies that recognise different epitopes, giving high specificity at the cost of needing a compatible antibody pair for each target. A competitive ELISA measures an antigen by letting it compete with a labelled tracer for a limited number of antibody binding sites, so the signal falls as the target concentration rises.

We used the competitive format because our recombinant proteins share a His tag and can therefore be quantified by competition for one anti-His antibody, with no separate antibody pair per protein. His-tagged proteins in the sample compete with an alkaline phosphatase-labelled His tracer for the anti-His monoclonal antibody, which is itself captured on a goat anti-mouse IgG-coated plate. Bound tracer converts pNPP substrate to an absorbance signal read at 405 to 420 nm. Because the signal decreases as concentration rises, a standard curve converts absorbance back into recombinant protein concentration.

What the measurement contributes

The assay gives a concentration for ACC deaminase, LEA14 and BoPep4 in bacterial culture samples, which lets production be compared across experimental conditions. We ran it with a six-point standard curve, duplicate measurements and binding controls, so the assay's own performance can be judged from the same plate as the samples. Alongside the Western blot and the activity assays it separates two questions that are easy to confuse: how much protein is present, and how much of it is working. The approach transfers to any microbial system expressing His-tagged recombinant proteins.

Berthelot assay for ACC deaminase

In our project, 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC) deaminase derived from Pseudomonas putida UW4 was selected as a bioprotectant to mitigate abiotic stress in plants.

Exposure to environmental stresses, such as drought, salinity, and extreme temperatures, triggers the accumulation of the stress hormone ethylene (Dubois et al., 2018), which accelerates leaf senescence and abscission, inhibits primary and lateral root elongation (Růžička et al., 2007), induces programmed cell death (Drew et al., 2000), and impairs photosynthesis (Ceusters & Van De Poel, 2018), ultimately precipitating rapid plant decline. Because ACC serves as the precursor to ethylene biosynthesis, ACC deaminase produced by rhizosphere microorganisms degrades ACC into α-ketobutyrate and ammonia. This enzymatic cleavage depletes rhizosphere ACC levels, establishing a concentration gradient that drives root exudation of ACC and thereby reduces endogenous ethylene synthesis (Glick, 2014). To exploit this mechanism, Bacillus subtilis was engineered to secrete recombinant ACC deaminase and cultured in a bioreactor for mass production. To assess bioreactor yield and confirm the enzymatic activity of the secreted protein, we evaluated the degradation efficiency of ACC in crude bioreactor extracts using two biochemical assays targeting ACC degraded products: a 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) assay to quantify α-ketobutyrate accumulation (Penrose & Glick, 2003), and a Berthelot (indophenol blue) assay to determine ammonia concentrations.

The Berthelot readout applies to the assays in this section. The reactor permeate assays of 7 and 8 October were read by the DNPH method as α-ketobutyrate (Digital Twin). Due to the unavailability of 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH), the catalytic activity of the recombinant ACC deaminase was determined using the Berthelot assay, targeting the released ammonium (NH4+/NH3) as the analytical readout. The Berthelot reaction is based on the reaction between ammonia and hypochlorite (OCl−) to generate monochloramine, which subsequently reacts with sodium salicylate in the presence of sodium nitroprusside as a catalyst to produce an indophenol dye quantifiable by absorbance at 660 nm (A660) (Krom, 1980). However, several interfering factors were encountered under our specific assay conditions. First, rich medium components, notably yeast extract and free amino acids present in the Terrific Broth (TB) medium, exhibit non-specific chromogenic interference in the Berthelot reaction (Ngo et al., 1982). Furthermore, unreacted ACC substrate also reacts with the reagents to form an indophenol-like colored complex. To accurately quantify the ammonium released by enzymatic ACC cleavage, two preliminary optimization steps were undertaken: determining an optimal dilution factor for the TB medium or bioreactor crude extract to suppress matrix background signals sufficiently below the target analyte response, and constructing a substrate-compensated calibration curve in which ACC and NH4+ were combined at complementary molar ratios summing to the fixed initial substrate concentration, allowing A660 to be calibrated directly against fractional ACC conversion.

What the assay is useful for

The Berthelot assay is predominantly employed in water quality and environmental monitoring, encompassing the determination of ammoniacal nitrogen in surface water, seawater, and industrial wastewater, as well as the quantification of Kjeldahl nitrogen in digested samples (Searle, 1984). In clinical diagnostics, coupling urease activity with ammonia production has established this reaction as a classical method for assessing blood urea nitrogen.

Soil and agricultural science represent another domain historically reliant on this methodology. Key applications include quantification of soil inorganic nitrogen pools, wherein exchangeable ammonium ions are extracted with 1 to 2 M KCl and the supernatant directly subjected to indophenol chromogenesis, a standardized procedure for evaluating available nitrogen and soil fertility; total nitrogen analysis of plant and soil digests, where the sodium salicylate-based indophenol modification is compatible with both 2 M KCl soil extracts and acid digests, offering superior sensitivity over legacy phenol formulations while substantially mitigating operational hazards (Kempers & Zweers, 1986); measurement of net nitrogen mineralization and nitrification rates, fundamentally indexing the soil nitrogen cycle by tracking net shifts in ammoniacal nitrogen across incubation periods; determination of soil urease activity, quantifying ammonium liberated from soil incubated with urea substrate via modified indophenol chromometry, an approach widely adopted in soil enzyme profiling (Kandeler & Gerber, 1988); and evaluation of fertilizer efficacy and urease inhibitors, monitoring urea hydrolysis kinetics to assess the capacity of inhibitors to delay ammonia release and curb volatilization losses. Additionally, in screening plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria and biostimulants, microbial ammonia production itself serves as a hallmark trait of plant-beneficial capacity.

Within biochemistry and enzymology, the Berthelot assay acts as a universal coupled detection system for any enzymatic reaction releasing free ammonia. Consequently, it is broadly applicable for profiling the catalytic activities of ureases, glutaminases, asparaginases, and diverse amino acid or nucleoside deaminases (Searle, 1984).

In our application, ACC deaminase catalyzes the cleavage of ACC to liberate ammonium, which theoretically permits activity assays via the Berthelot method. In practice, however, severe analytical interferences arising from both the rich culture medium and the substrate itself preclude direct quantification. Here, we address and systematically resolve these matrix and substrate interferences, establishing the Berthelot assay as a viable analytical alternative to monitor ACC deaminase activity when DNPH is unavailable.

DNPH assay for ACC deaminase activity

To determine whether engineered Bacillus subtilis WB800 can produce and secrete active ACC deaminase, we measure the enzymatic cleavage of ACC into α-ketobutyrate and ammonia. The Berthelot assay detects the ammonium; the 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) assay reads the other product directly.

Under acidic conditions DNPH reacts with the carbonyl group of α-ketobutyrate to form a hydrazone, which is made visible with alkali and quantified at 540 nm (Penrose & Glick, 2003; Kozaeva et al., 2022). The method answers the three objections to reading ammonium in our samples: the ACC substrate does not interfere, the background in a rich culture medium is far lower, and the assay is nineteen times more sensitive. That sensitivity is what makes initial velocities measurable and so puts true kinetic parameters, Km and kcat, within reach. Paired with growth and expression readouts, it tells us whether the enzyme leaving the cell is catalytically intact and not merely present.

What the measurement contributes

We scaled the conventional protocol of Penrose and Glick (2003) down twenty-fold while holding the reaction proportions, which moves the assay onto a 96-well microplate and makes screening a strain library practical. With it we compared ACC deaminase activity across constructs, tested what pyridoxal 5'-phosphate supplementation does to that activity, and checked that recombinant ACCD secreted by our engineered B. subtilis still functions outside the cell. We also set up the standard curve and the arithmetic that converts an absorbance into an α-ketobutyrate concentration, a total product yield and an activity, so the comparison between strains rests on a stated calculation.

Bioreactor assays

The reactor carries its own instruments, so these measurements are as much about the instrument as about the culture. Two of them have produced data and one of them is still a design with a single offline check behind it.

Dual light path inline photometer

We built an in-line flow-cell photometer that reads OD600 directly from our hollow-fiber bioreactor without ever needing to draw a sample.

OD600 is the field standard for bacterial density but is almost always measured through sampling, which not only interrupts the run it describes but is also a manual action that creates the opportunity for contamination or accidental measured value error, especially when placed in public use for small-scale farmers. So we moved the measurement into the loop itself. A light beam generated by an amber LED, whose peak lies close to 600 nm, passes through a 90/10 beam splitter that diverts part of the path to a reference sensor before the rest crosses our flow cuvette and reaches the main sensor.

A single sensor would be enough if the light source never changed, and over a multi-week run it does. LED output drifts with temperature, current and age, and one sensor cannot tell a dimmer lamp from a denser culture, because both make the beam weaker. The reference sensor reads the source on a path that never touches the medium, so dividing one by the other cancels whatever the source did and leaves what the sample did. That, together with burst sampling and a MAD filter for bubble rejection, is what lets a roughly $150 build compete with professional OD600 instruments such as the BioDrop at around $15,000.

What another team can take from it

Our central contribution is a validated, calibration-free in-line OD600 measurement device. Our raw output already agrees with a $15,000 reference instrument, so another team can rebuild and use it directly. A device that requires the calibration against a benchtop spectrophotometer doesn't remove the pricey spectrophotometer from the workflow, while ours cuts that dependency. 4 main pieces of this photometer can be taken to be learned and reused by others.

  • In-line, non-destructive geometry. Our flow cuvette sits in the circulating loop, outside of our tubing. No volume of medium needs to be removed, nor does anyone need to adjust or operate it once it's set up, and the tilt of the photometer ensures almost all bubbles that enter leave within a quarter of a second.
  • Reference sensor normalisation. The use of a 90/10 beam splitter sends 10% of our light into a sensor that avoids the path of our medium sample. This allows us to set a calibration for our main sensor in the case of source-intensity variability. Some possible instances could be from inconsistent current (our PN2222 transistor mostly accounts for that) or LED degradation. Over a multi-week run though, precautions have to be set in place.
  • Bubble-rejection filter. Each point of measurement from our photometer is determined by a burst of 16 data points. These data points are condensed by median and median absolute deviation (MAD), with a fence at median ± 3×MAD. By using the median twice, as our center and as our spread, we let large bubbles inflate its own fence and pass through as an outlier. The live rejection rate is reported to the user and when flagged as above 50%, triggers a hardware alarm that indicates inaccurate photometer data.
  • Optogenetic-compatible wavelength measurement. 600 nm wavelength LEDs keep us on the standard for measuring turbidity, while simultaneously sitting right between the wavelengths of green (~535 nm) and red (~672 nm), the 2 actuation peaks for CcaS/CcaR. This ensures that our photometer measurements don't affect the activation or deactivation of our optogenetic circuit in B. subtilis.

A fifth piece was added on 14 October, after the bioreactor run of 8 to 10 October showed what the first four do not cover. Bubbles and a drifting reference can move the reading while the culture stands still: across that run a sixth of the windows carried more than 20% bubbles, and the reference beam, which never passes through the medium, moved by a factor of 4.8. The acceptance gate now checks each window against the last one accepted; a window that fails holds the previous value and is flagged instead of being published. On the 14 to 16 October run it turned away 31 of 1,083 windows, and the reference held within 17%. The gate also marks the top of the instrument's range: above an optical density of about 2.7 the sample beam falls to a tenth of the reference, and what the photometer reports above that is flagged and not used (Digital Twin).

Our sensors, splitter, and lens, all sit in TPU-lined slots in the PLA body. The two BH1750 sensors share SDA and SCL. Acquisition and filtering 16 readings, a median fence, one logged point every 10 minutes
  1. Discard 2 warm-up readings from each sensor.
  2. Take a burst of 16 readings per sensor.
  3. Compute the median of the burst.
  4. Compute the median absolute deviation (MAD), the median of the absolute distances of each reading from that median.
  5. Reject any reading outside median ± 3·MAD.
  6. If more than 50% of readings are rejected, retry and flag the point; the dashboard displays the live rejection rate in red and reports excessive bubbling to the user.
  7. Log the surviving result every 10 minutes.

The median is used for both centre and spread deliberately. A bubble produces one or two readings far above the true value; a mean would be dragged upward and a standard deviation inflated, so the outlier would widen its own acceptance window and survive. Median and MAD are insensitive to a few extreme points, so the fence stays tight and the bubble is rejected.

SiO₂ microsphere cross-validation the written protocol: two photometers in series against a benchtop reference
Objective

To evaluate the response of our inline photometers across a range of optical densities, we designed a calibration experiment using monodisperse silicon dioxide (SiO₂) microspheres. The experiment compares two inline photometers installed in series in a recirculating loop with a benchtop spectrophotometer measuring OD₆₀₀ at a standard 1 cm path length.

SiO₂ microspheres provide a particle-based scattering standard with a specified particle size and stock concentration. By gradually increasing their concentration in the loop, we can examine how each inline photometer responds to increasing turbidity and determine how its readings relate to the benchtop reference. This allows us to assess measurement consistency and establish a conversion to reference OD₆₀₀ within the experimentally validated linear range.

Materials and setup

The experiment uses a monodisperse SiO₂ microsphere suspension (Tomicro; mean particle diameter 1 µm; stock concentration 25 mg/mL), deionized water, a peristaltic pump, a stirred reservoir, two inline photometers, and a benchtop spectrophotometer. The microsphere stock is diluted with deionized water to prepare a working suspension of 2.5 mg/mL. The two inline photometers are connected in series in a recirculating loop. Continuous stirring helps keep the microspheres suspended, while circulation allows the same suspension to pass through both optical measurement points.

Experimental procedure
  1. Establish the blank. Fill the system with deionized water, remove visible air bubbles, and circulate the liquid for at least three complete loop turnovers. Record the blank readings from both inline photometers. Measure a deionized-water blank on the benchtop spectrophotometer as well.
  2. Increase the microsphere concentration. Conduct up to 50 dosing cycles. In each cycle, add 1 mL of thoroughly mixed working suspension to the reservoir, restoring the liquid volume after the preceding 1 mL sample withdrawal.
  3. Allow the readings to stabilize. Keep the pump and stirrer running and wait at least three loop turnovers before recording the readings from both inline photometers.
  4. Measure the reference OD₆₀₀. Withdraw a 1 mL sample from the reservoir and measure it promptly using the benchtop spectrophotometer. Discard the sample after measurement.
  5. Repeat and record. Repeat the dosing, stabilization, and measurement sequence until the planned concentration range is covered. Record all raw measurements and any deviations, including bubbles, settling, dilution errors, or readings outside the instrument's linear range.

The blank and each subsequent measurement form paired observations across the two inline photometers and the benchtop reference. These paired data allow direct comparison of the instruments at corresponding stages of the experiment.

Concentration calculations

Because each cycle replaces a 1 mL sample with 1 mL of working suspension, the concentration increases cumulatively rather than by a constant amount. For a well-mixed system with total liquid volume V, the theoretical concentration after n dosing cycles is

Cn = Cstock × [1 − ((V − 1)/V)n]

where Cstock is the working suspension concentration and volumes are expressed in millilitres. Under the protocol's assumed conditions of V = 50 mL and Cstock = 2.5 mg/mL, the theoretical concentration after 50 cycles is approximately 1.590 mg/mL. The theoretical OD₆₀₀ estimates are useful for planning the experiment, but they are not substitutes for measured values. Particle scattering depends on instrument geometry and suspension properties, and multiple scattering can cause deviations from linearity at higher concentrations.

Data analysis and calibration

For each dosing cycle, plot the readings from both inline photometers and the benchtop spectrophotometer against the calculated SiO₂ concentration. Identify the range over which each instrument's response is approximately linear, and fit a linear regression using only the appropriate data points. Use the benchtop spectrophotometer as the external reference to establish a conversion from each inline photometer's reading to reference OD₆₀₀. Evaluate the fits using the coefficient of determination and residual standard deviation. Compare the two inline photometers to identify any systematic differences that may arise from sensor position, flow conditions, or optical design. Measurements outside the validated linear range should be flagged rather than automatically included in the regression. The final calibration coefficients and usable measurement range should be reported only after experimental data have been collected and analyzed.

Quality control and limitations
  • Sedimentation. Continuously stir the reservoir and measure withdrawn samples promptly.
  • Air bubbles. Remove bubbles before recording measurements because they can introduce scattering artifacts.
  • Unequal readings between inline photometers. Investigate possible positional effects, settling, or differences between the optical measurement channels.
  • Volume errors. Maintain consistent pipetting because small errors can accumulate across repeated dosing cycles.
  • Limited linear range. At high particle concentrations, multiple scattering may cause deviations from the expected linear relationship.

This experiment characterizes the optical response of the photometers to SiO₂ microspheres. It does not, by itself, establish that the same conversion applies directly to bacterial cultures, whose optical properties may differ. Further validation with the intended culture medium and biological samples is necessary before applying the calibration to bioreactor monitoring.

Transmembrane pressure and flowrate meter

Flow and transmembrane pressure were measured together, alongside our pump and specific dials and outputs.

These two numbers decide whether the hollow fiber membrane can run our culture at all. Flow rate tells us how hard the fibers are sweeping the cells; transmembrane pressure tells us whether the protectant the reactor produces can be pushed through the membrane.

Transmembrane pressure is the mean lumen pressure minus the shell, or permeate, pressure, and it has to be positive for anything to pass. A positive TMP drives fluid from the lumen through the fiber wall to the shell side, and permeate flux rises with it, so a TMP at or below zero means nothing leaves with the permeate (Millipore Corporation, 2003). Past a certain point flux stops rising and the fouling layer on the membrane is compressed instead, which only adds resistance (Millipore Corporation, 2003). We therefore read TMP at each pump setting, looking for dials that keep it positive without pushing it into that plateau.

Flow through the fiber lumens sets the wall shear that sweeps the membrane. For laminar flow in a lumen the wall shear rate is

γ̇w = 4(Q/n) / (πr³)

where Q is the total flow rate, n the number of fibers and r the lumen radius, so shear is fixed by the fiber inner diameter, the fiber count and the flow (Yuen, 2025). Our module has 11 fibers of 1 mm inner diameter, giving r = 0.5 mm. Across the flow meter readings for dials 10 to 24, about 113 to 450 mL/min, the calculated wall shear rate is about 1,750 to 6,950 s⁻¹.

That range is a problem, and it is the reason this measurement exists. In a Couette flow bioreactor B. subtilis grew at shear rates up to 1,482 s⁻¹, but the cells shrank to about half their size and intracellular reactive oxygen species rose 9.3-fold (Sahoo et al., 2003). We therefore treat roughly 1,500 s⁻¹, about 1.5 Pa in a water-like medium, as the upper limit for lumen wall shear, and our whole operating range sits above it. Culture viability after circulation through the module has to be checked before any dial is settled on. At the low end the concern reverses: B. subtilis biofilms and streamers formed at every wall shear stress tested from 0.068 to 0.67 Pa, and biofilm growth slowed as shear rose (Wittig et al., 2025). We found no lower survival limit for planktonic cells. Both studies used a bioreactor and a flow channel, not a hollow-fiber module, so the limits are transferred and not measured here.

Taken together, the two readings choose a pump setting: the flow must keep lumen shear below the survival limit and high enough to slow fouling, and TMP at that flow must stay positive. Both also feed the scale-up question. Larger vessels mix less well, and poor mixing leaves gradients in dissolved gases, pH, substrate and shear that cells meet as they circulate (Lara et al., 2006); mass and heat transfer coefficients both depend on power input and mixing time, and the formulas used to scale them are empirical (Villadsen, 2015). In our system TMP drives the protectant across the membrane, the flow rate sets how well material reaches the fiber wall and how much is swept away from it, and the circulating flow carries heat between the culture and the rest of the loop. Fouling, which shows up as a rising TMP, would reduce mass transfer over a long run.

ReLEAF has a third transfer to keep in mind, because the system is driven by light. As the culture scales the light path lengthens and the cell density rises, so less light crosses the vessel and it eventually becomes too opaque for the light to reach every cell or to pass through our photometer. Optogenetic circuits have given homogeneous induction in bioreactors of at least 5 L, with illumination limited above OD600 40 (Zhao et al., 2021). Flow rate also sets how fast cells move through the lit region and how well they mix within it.

How flow was measured, three ways

For measuring flowrate, we first set the pump dial and collect outflow in a measuring cylinder over fixed time intervals, with only a tube and no membrane connected. The pump covers dials from 0 to 100, and the flowrate was tested every 10 dials. Three trials were conducted per setting, except for output levels 70 to 100.

August 14, we connected the hollow fiber membrane to test both the flowrate and transmembrane pressure simultaneously. At this stage flow rate was still measured manually. Similarly, we set up the dial and collected outflow over fixed time intervals. This time, however, we tested output levels every 2 dials from 10 to 24 instead, where we are most likely to operate. Three rounds were conducted, all of which had three trials.

Finally, on August 16 we turned to using a flow meter instead of manually testing, due to results being too dependent on the operator's technique. Two configurations were tested at the same pump settings (10 to 24% output): a Background configuration, in which a plain silicone tubing replaced the membrane module while every valve, fitting, and flow cuvette remained in place, and an HFM configuration, in which the actual hollow-fiber membrane module was installed with everything else unchanged.

An experiment was conducted for validating the accuracy of the flow meter, which works on a Hall-effect principle. A small magnet embedded in an internal rotor spins as water flows through it, and a stationary sensor mounted beside the rotor detects the resulting oscillation in magnetic field strength as the magnet passes by. The frequency of this oscillation is then converted into the corresponding flow rate. We ran the test with a flow meter operating while simultaneously measuring the outflow manually, same method as previously used. Results show strong goodness of fit, with coefficient of determination reaching 0.995.

How pressure was measured

As for transmembrane pressure measurement, three pressure sensors were installed at the feed, retentate, and permeate ports of the hollow fiber membrane module. Feed measured pressure as the medium entered the lumen side, retentate measured pressure as it exited the lumen side, and permeate measured pressure on the shell side where fluid passed through the membrane. The feed and retentate readings were used to calculate the transmembrane pressure using the standard approximation TMP = (Pfeed − Pretentate) / 2. We read the pressure transducer, which recorded psi and converted them to bar.

The pressure transducer outputs a current signal, with a native range of 0 to 1 A. It originally converts to 5 V, the intensity would overwhelm our sensor. Therefore, two resistors were used when converting this current output into a voltage, producing a 0 to 3.3 V signal compatible with our microcontroller's analog input instead.

The quantity recorded in these tables was computed as (Pfeed − Pretentate) / 2, which is a pressure drop along the lumen and not the transmembrane pressure defined in the introduction. TMP needs the permeate reading: the mean of feed and retentate, minus the shell pressure. The permeate channel is not in these tables, so no transmembrane pressure has yet been computed from this rig, and whether TMP stays positive at a working dial is still open.

Hollow fiber membrane breach detector

The hollow fiber is where the culture and harvest stream touch, and a 0.2 µm pore is what keeps B. subtilis on the lumen side. If breached, the cells would be able to come through, so a detector is installed on the shell side to watch for signs that may indicate unwelcomed particles.

Fitted, but no breach test is on record. It currently rests on an offline proxy that was run once. During version 1, 2 mL was drawn from the shell side and spread on LB agar, and no colonies were recovered, so no culturable B. subtilis reached the shell side in that run. It is a single plating, and the replicate count, incubation time and incubation temperature were not recorded.

No detectable B. subtilis colonies were observed in the shell-side sample under the tested conditions. This provides preliminary evidence that the hollow-fiber membrane retained bacterial cells during the tested run.

What this plate can carry is a containment claim for one run; it cannot stand in for the detector, because a plate takes a day to answer and an instrument has to answer while the reactor is running. The build is on Hardware → Bioreactor and the containment argument it belongs to is on Safety.

Not measured yet

These measurements are planned and named in our protocol set but have no result written against them. They are listed here rather than given a block of their own, because a five-part write-up with nothing in it is the one thing this page must not do. Two of them now have a full protocol and are waiting only on data; the rest have neither.

On the plant side

  • MDA content, for lipid peroxidation. Protocol written, after Zhang and Huang (2013): seedlings ground in liquid nitrogen into 0.1% TCA, reacted with 20% TCA containing 0.5% TBA, boiled for 15 minutes, and read at 532 nm against a standard curve from 1 to 50 µM. It would replace a judgement of leaf colour by eye with a number (Morales and Munné-Bosch, 2019).
  • Total protein content. Protocol written: ten seedlings per group ground in liquid nitrogen, extracted in 0.5 mL of protein extraction buffer for 1 hour at 4 °C, centrifuged twice at 13,000 ×g, and quantified with a Pierce BCA assay kit. Proteases degrade protein under stress, so total protein should fall in proportion to the damage, and a protectant that works should reduce that loss (Fan and Jespersen, 2025; Kumar Parida et al., 2004).
  • Relative gene expression. Neither protocol nor data.

On the protectant side

  • Purifying the secreted protein from liquid culture, as a protocol in its own right. The imidazole elution in the Western blot chapter is the only purification on record.
  • Km and kcat for ACC deaminase. The DNPH assay is sensitive enough to measure initial velocities; no time-course has been run to extract them.
  • A reactor sample carrying both the Csn and SamyQ constructs. The activity comparison between them rests on one plate of single wells.

On the bioreactor side

  • Transmembrane pressure itself. The permeate transducer is fitted but its readings are not in the August series, so only the lumen pressure drop has been computed.
  • Culture viability after circulation, which the calculated wall shear rate of 1,750 to 6,950 s⁻¹ makes the open question on this rig.
  • Bioreactor leakage detector, fitted and never exercised.
  • Turbidity meter for bacterial escape, not built.

What the page can support today: two plant platforms with known working windows and written failure records, a light plate matched LED by LED before it drove anything, a transformation route with counted colonies and an optimal voltage for the construct the reactor runs on, a blot that puts secreted ACC deaminase at the size its sequence predicts, a concentration for LEA that sits inside its own standard curve, an activity assay that shows ACC deaminase does nothing without its cofactor, an interference assay that states the substrate load above which its own readings are meaningless, an inline photometer that agrees with the benchtop reference without being calibrated against it and that since 14 October states its own upper limit, and one flow series that survives.

What it does not support is a claim resting on the 14 August pressure run, on the 24 hour optical densities, on any ELISA value that falls outside the 20 to 80% band, on a single-well activity reading taken as a fold difference, on a transmembrane pressure that has not been computed, or on the two safety instruments that have never been tested against a breach. The heat runs in hydroponics returned no rescue at any dilution, and that is reported as a result and not as a gap.
