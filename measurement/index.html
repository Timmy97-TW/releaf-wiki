Measurement

Abstract

We claim engineering successes across four stages of ReLEAF. This page is where each claim is made checkable from outside the team: the principle, the control it rests on, the protocol in full, the raw numbers, and what of it transfers to another project.

The bioreactor instruments read OD600 in the loop without drawing a sample, and pressure and flow across the membrane. That reading is referenced to a blank and carried onto the conventional 1 cm path, so it is quoted in the units a benchtop instrument gives and needs no transform fitted against one. The software algorithm calibration goes a step behind it: we opened our own firmware, derived the bias of every step between the light and the number, and measured those biases against a 1 µm silica ladder, replaying eleven reduction algorithms on one raw data set. The raw bursts, the replay script and the predictions we registered before the run are published, so the comparison can be rerun on another team’s photometer. The light plate was matched LED by LED before it drove anything. The plant assays put Arabidopsis on half-strength MS agar and in hydroponic culture so that stress dose, protectant and timing move one at a time against an untreated control on the same plate, and they return root length, fresh weight and chlorophyll. The protectant assays follow the protein from the cell to the working enzyme: whether a band of the right size leaves the cell, how much of it there is, and whether it turns substrate over, the last two read net of the untransformed host. Where a measurement failed we say so: five of our eight pre-registered predictions about the photometer were overturned by our own data, the 14 August pressure series does not repeat, and heat stress in hydroponics flattened the treated plants along with the controls.

Overview

Can our engineered Bacillus subtilis detect plant stress and respond by producing and releasing the appropriate plant protectant at the right moment? How well does the protectant aid in plant growth?

That question splits into measurable pieces, and the five families below answer them in the order the material moves: the reactor makes the protein, the software decides what the reactor's own sensor is allowed to say, light sets how much protein is made, the plant says whether any of it worked, and the protectant assays check that the protein we sprayed was really there. Each one is reported the same way: how the measurement works, what it is useful for outside this project, the protocol in enough detail to repeat, and what it returned, including the runs that failed.

Bioreactor assays

The reactor carries its own instruments, so these measurements are as much about the instrument as about the culture. Two of them have produced data; the third is fitted but has only a single offline check behind it.

Dual light path inline photometer

We built an in-line flow-cell photometer that reads OD600 directly from our hollow-fiber bioreactor without ever needing to draw a sample.

OD600 is the field standard for bacterial density, and it is almost always read by drawing a sample. That interrupts the run it describes, and every draw is a manual step that can contaminate the culture or be misrecorded, which matters most when the reactor is in a farmer's hands rather than a lab's. So we moved the measurement into the loop itself. A light beam generated by an amber LED, whose peak lies close to 600 nm, passes through a 90/10 beam splitter that diverts part of the path to a reference sensor before the rest crosses our flow cuvette and reaches the main sensor.

A single sensor would be enough if the light source never changed, and over a multi-week run it does. LED output drifts with temperature, current and age, and one sensor cannot tell a dimmer lamp from a denser culture, because both make the beam weaker. The reference sensor reads the source on a path that never touches the medium, so dividing one by the other cancels whatever the source did and leaves what the sample did. That, together with burst sampling and a MAD filter for bubble rejection, is what lets a build of parts hold its reading against a benchtop BioDrop. The bill of materials below is itemised line by line and comes to US$371.78; the BioDrop it was read against cost NT$15,000, about US$470.

Two builds, and what the second one cost

We built the instrument twice. The first, the Pro, is a beam-splitter design: an amber LED, a collimating lens, a 90/10 splitter and two lux sensors in a printed body, on a tilted stand so bubbles leave the cuvette instead of sitting in the beam. It works, and it is the one on the reactor. The second, the Slim, is a stripped build that replaces the splitter and the free-space path with a fibre collimator feeding the same 2 mm flow cuvette, in a block small enough to clip anywhere on the loop. Building the cheap one second, once we knew what the first measured, is what let us say which parts of the cost were doing work.

The two disagree. The Pro is about 5.3 times more sensitive, so it resolves small changes better at low density. The Slim places its source closer to the cuvette and the sensor, so with 1 µm particles scattering at small forward angles more of that light still lands on the 3.5 × 2 mm receptor instead of being lost. Less apparent attenuation per unit concentration costs sensitivity and buys range: over the whole silica series the Slim fits the mass standard at R² = 0.992 against the Pro's 0.974, the gap sitting entirely at the top where the Pro approaches its limit and curves. Within 0 to 1 OD the two are comparable, 0.9947 against 0.9874. The Slim's reference channel also works, and the Pro's does not. For a long run we would now put the Slim first.

What another team can take from it

Our central contribution is an in-line OD600 measurement that needs no instrument-specific transform fitted against a benchtop spectrophotometer. The raw output agrees with the benchtop reference to a median paired difference of 3.8%, so another team can rebuild it and use the number it gives. Four pieces of it transfer.

  • In-line, non-destructive geometry. Our flow cuvette sits in the circulating loop, outside of our tubing. No volume of medium needs to be removed, nor does anyone need to adjust or operate it once it's set up, and the tilt of the photometer ensures almost all bubbles that enter leave within a quarter of a second.
  • Reference sensor normalisation. The use of a 90/10 beam splitter sends 10% of our light into a sensor that avoids the path of our medium sample. This allows us to set a calibration for our main sensor in the case of source-intensity variability. Some possible instances could be from inconsistent current (our PN2222 transistor mostly accounts for that) or LED degradation. Over a multi-week run though, precautions have to be set in place.
  • Bubble-rejection filter. Each point of measurement from our photometer is determined by a burst of 16 data points. These data points are condensed by median and median absolute deviation (MAD), with a fence at median ± 3×MAD. By using the median twice, as our center and as our spread, we let large bubbles inflate its own fence and pass through as an outlier. The live rejection rate is reported to the user and when flagged as above 50%, triggers a hardware alarm that indicates inaccurate photometer data.
  • Optogenetic-compatible wavelength measurement. 600 nm wavelength LEDs keep us on the standard for measuring turbidity, while simultaneously sitting right between the wavelengths of green (~535 nm) and red (~672 nm), the 2 actuation peaks for CcaS/CcaR. This ensures that our photometer measurements don't affect the activation or deactivation of our optogenetic circuit in B. subtilis.

From lux to optical density

The sensors report illuminance in lux, and the optical density is derived from those readings in software. The microcontroller does no arithmetic of its own: it forwards the readings intact, so every OD in our logs can be recomputed from the original readings after a run rather than existing only as a number the instrument chose to report.

One measurement is twenty readings from each sensor, four with the LED off and sixteen with it on. The median of the four dark readings is subtracted, which removes ambient light and the sensor's own offset, and the first two lit readings are discarded as settling time. The remaining fourteen per channel are reduced to one value by averaging those inside median ± 3·MAD. That leaves two numbers: S, the intensity through the flow cuvette, and R, the intensity at the reference sensor. With the blank values S₀ and R₀ stored from sterile medium,

OD = log10[ (S₀/R₀) / (S/R) ] × plf

S/R is the sample intensity relative to the reference intensity at the same instant, so dividing the blank's value of that quantity by the current one gives the factor by which transmission has fallen, and its base-10 logarithm is the optical density. The path-length factor plf is 5.0, because our cuvette has a 2 mm internal path and OD600 is conventionally quoted through a 1 cm cuvette.

Why there is no calibration step

A single sensor cannot tell a grown culture from a dimmed lamp, since both reduce the intensity at the detector. The reference channel makes that separation algebraic. Let P be the LED output at the moment of measurement, T the transmittance of the cuvette contents, and k the fixed gain of the sample path relative to the reference path: the 90/10 split and the responsivity difference between the two sensors. The reference path never crosses the cuvette, so T enters only the sample channel, and after dark subtraction

S / R = k × T

P has gone. Whatever the LED did at that instant hit both readings equally and divided out. We never measure k either; it is eliminated by referencing a blank taken on sterile medium, for which S₀/R₀ = k × Tblank, so that

(S/R) / (S₀/R₀) = T / Tblank

which is transmittance relative to medium, the quantity optical density is defined on. Accuracy rests on those two cancellations: the first removes source intensity at the instant of measurement, the second every fixed difference between the channels. It is also why the 9:1 split never appears in the equation despite being a real property of the optics. It sits inside k and cancels, along with sensor responsivity differences, one-sided transmission losses and small alignment errors, none of which has to be characterised on its own.

The blank is correspondingly the one input that has to be right, since it carries k and Tblank into every later point. It is refused if either sensor is saturated, if too few readings survive the filter, or if either channel reads zero; with no blank stored, the software returns no OD rather than an uncorrected number. The same model makes R a direct measure of source stability, because k is fixed and T never enters it:

R / R₀ = P / P₀

Reported on every measurement, that ratio holds at unity for a whole run regardless of culture density, and a value outside 0.85 to 1.15 raises a drift flag. Dividing by R keeps the data correct under drift; R/R₀ is what tells us the drift happened, which over a multi-week run is what says the LED needs attention. On a single optical path the reference terms are unavailable and the software falls back to OD = log10(S₀/S) × plf, where P does not cancel and any source change reads directly as an OD change. Both forms are logged for every measurement.

A fifth piece, an acceptance gate that holds a reading back instead of publishing it, was added on 14 October after the reactor run of 8 to 10 October. Why that gate exists, what it catches and what it costs are the subject of the next section, which is where all of the arithmetic between the light and the number is reported.

Mechanical and electrical build TPU-lined slots, two supply rails, and five wires to the Arduino
Mechanical

Our sensors, splitter and lens all sit in TPU-lined slots in the PLA body, which grip them tightly without cracking the glass. That is the one change that most improved stability across long runs. The body joins the base at a diagonal, which gives the instrument the tilt that lets bubbles rise out of the cuvette and lets both parts print without supports. Snap-fit covers allow easy service and access while keeping the light path clear of anything outside it.

Electrical

Sensors run at 3.3 V and the LED at 5 V on separate supplies, since the LED supply is expected to rise as the bioreactor scales; a MOSFET switches the LED independently so the source is not held on continuously. The two BH1750 sensors share SDA and SCL. Grounds are commoned on a soldered PCB, leaving five connections (SDA, SCL, D3, 3.3 V, 5 V, GND) to the Arduino UNO Q, which runs the acquisition loop standalone with no tethered computer.

Acquisition and filtering 16 readings, a median fence, one logged point every 10 minutes
  1. Discard 2 warm-up readings from each sensor.
  2. Take a burst of 16 readings per sensor.
  3. Compute the median of the burst.
  4. Compute the median absolute deviation (MAD), the median of the absolute distances of each reading from that median.
  5. Reject any reading outside median ± 3·MAD.
  6. If more than 50% of readings are rejected, retry and flag the point; the dashboard displays the live rejection rate in red and reports excessive bubbling to the user.
  7. Log the surviving result every 10 minutes.

The median is used for both centre and spread deliberately: a bubble that pushed one or two readings far off would drag a mean with it and inflate a standard deviation, widening the very window meant to exclude it. What that choice is actually worth, measured against a silica standard, is reported in the next section.

Two standards, two runs

We ran this instrument against two standards, eight weeks apart, with different particles and a different question.

Titanium dioxide, 23 August. Dosed by mass into the loop and read against a BioDrop at every step. The raw, uncalibrated output agrees with the BioDrop to a median paired difference of 3.8% and tracks the mass standard at least as closely as the reference instrument does (R² = 0.9999 against 0.9998). We ran the test twice and the two iterations agree with the reference independently. Two points were excluded on objective grounds and both were BioDrop readings, a handling error in the first run and a monotonicity violation in the second; removing them did not move our own readings at all.

We did not calibrate against the BioDrop, because the runs showed we did not need to. Against the mass standard the BioDrop is the less linear of the two, so fitting to it would tie our readings to that instrument's own errors while we already agree within a few percent.

Silicon dioxide, 10 October. Monodisperse 1 µm microspheres, close to the size of a B. subtilis cell, with both builds in series on one loop and the concentration axis set by the volumes added instead of by any instrument's reading. Because the two sat in series they saw the same suspension at the same instant.

The BioDrop is not a valid comparator over the whole series: above OD600 2 each sample had to be diluted fourfold and multiplied back, which amplifies any pipetting or reading error. Every instrument-to-instrument comparison below is therefore restricted to the 0 to 1 OD window, inside the BioDrop's own specified range.

The Pro. Read directly against the BioDrop it gives a slope of 1.001, which reproduces the titanium dioxide result on a different particle. The one difference is a constant offset of about 0.12 OD, intercept −0.123, the Pro reading low at every point.

Both halves of that sentence are testable, so we tested them rather than asserting them. Against a null of unit slope the regression returns t(6) = 0.03, p = 0.98, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.892 to 1.110: the data are consistent with the Pro reading on the BioDrop's scale, and the width of that interval is the honest limit of what eight points can say, namely that the slope is within about 11% of one. Against a null of zero intercept the same regression returns t(6) = −4.38, p = 0.005, 95% CI −0.192 to −0.054, so the offset is not an artefact of scatter. The paired difference says the same thing directly: −0.123 OD, 95% CI −0.150 to −0.095, t(7) = −10.7, p < 0.001. A scale error would have moved the slope and left the intercept alone; this run does the opposite, which is what sends us to the blank rather than to the optics. The fits, the intervals and the figure above are one calculation, recomputed from the workbook by build/measurement_stats.py in our wiki repository.

The offset is additive rather than multiplicative, and the two runs together locate it. Fixed channel differences cancel against the blank and cannot reach the reported OD; the slope is the same across titanium and silicon dioxide while the intercept was effectively zero in the first run and −0.12 in this one, so the optics are unchanged and the blank is what differs. The likely cause is that the loop was not completely clean when the blank was stored, which depresses every later reading by a constant and leaves the slope untouched. That is to be confirmed by re-blanking on a clean loop. No offset correction is applied to any reading on this wiki. Unit slope across two independent standards is what says the Pro needs no calibration transform; the blank-referenced offset is the one item still open.

The Slim. This run is its first validation, since it was built after the titanium dioxide work. Its slope, 0.0086, is about a fifth of the BioDrop's and the Pro's, a ratio of 5.3, and against the Pro it fits a straight line at R² = 0.993. That is a linear rescaling, not a different response shape. It does not read on the BioDrop's scale and is not built to, but its own response is linear at R² = 0.9947, reproducible against the Pro on the same suspension, and related to the reference scale by a single constant rather than a fitted curve: one coefficient, with no curve shape to characterise per instrument. It trades direct scale agreement for range and cost.

Headroom of roughly five times the BioDrop's range follows from that slope ratio, and it is an estimate rather than a measurement: the series was not carried past the points shown, so the Slim's true upper limit still needs a dedicated high-density run.

This is also the run that produced the raw data behind the next section, where the ladder, the working range of each build and the bubble behaviour are reported in full. Further numbers are on the photometer page and under R1 on Digital Twin.

Transmembrane pressure and flowrate meter

Our reactor is meant to be run by a farmer, not a technician, so it has to show what is happening inside it.

Two of the parameters we sense are the most useful for that: the flow rate through the hollow fiber module and the transmembrane pressure across the membrane.

Both work as early warnings. If pressure or flow at a given pump setting drops suddenly, that points to a leak. If TMP climbs while the flow rate stays where it was, cells are most likely sticking, building a cake layer and blocking the fibers (Field et al., 1995), and the module is then washed with an alkaline solution to clear it (Millipore Corporation, 2003).

They also decide how the module is operated. Normally the shell-side valve is closed, so the culture runs parallel along the fibers and sweeps their surface, which is what keeps B. subtilis from settling on them (Millipore Corporation, 2003; Wittig et al., 2025). To collect the protectant we open the shell-side valve and close the pinch valve, which forces liquid across the fiber wall instead of along it: the cells stay inside and the protein they secrete passes through the 0.2 µm pores. That only works while TMP is positive, since TMP is the pressure pushing liquid out through the membrane. We also intend to try driving TMP negative, which would let us pump medium back into the reactor.

Flow also sets the shear the cells meet inside a fiber (Yuen, 2025). At our operating settings it is about 4,000 to 6,000 s⁻¹, which we read as comfortably inside what B. subtilis tolerates. That reading is not settled here, and the one published figure we have found points the other way: B. subtilis grew in a Couette flow bioreactor at up to 1,482 s⁻¹, but the cells shrank to about half their size and intracellular reactive oxygen species rose 9.3-fold (Sahoo et al., 2003). That was a different vessel, so the number transfers only loosely, but until culture viability has been read after circulation at our own dials, the margin is an expectation and not a measurement.

How flow was measured, three ways

First, by hand with no membrane fitted: set the pump dial, catch the outflow in a measuring cylinder over a fixed interval. The pump covers dials 0 to 100 and we tested every tenth, three trials per setting except 70 to 100.

On 14 August we fitted the membrane and read flow and pressure together, still catching the outflow by hand, every second dial from 10 to 24 where we expect to operate: three rounds of three trials.

On 16 August we replaced the hand method with the flow meter, because the hand readings depended too much on who took them. Two configurations ran at the same dials, 10 to 24: a background loop with plain tubing in place of the module and every valve, fitting and cuvette still fitted, and the same loop with the module installed.

The meter is a ZJ-TM02, a Hall-effect type: a magnet in an internal rotor spins with the flow and a fixed sensor emits a pulse each time it passes, so pulse frequency is proportional to flow. Ours gives 2,580 pulses per litre, about 43 Hz per L/min. The microcontroller counts rising edges and ignores any pulse arriving within 0.5 ms of the previous one, which rejects switching noise, and reports the pulse count, the time between the first and last pulse and a running total every second. The software converts to mL/min as pulses / 2,580 × 1,000 per minute.

We validated the meter by reading it and catching the outflow by hand at the same time, and the two agree with R² = 0.995.

How pressure was measured

Pressure is read by three transmitters, Jingfeng BP8G-AGB, on the feed (lumen in), the retentate (lumen out) and the permeate (shell side). Each runs from 12 V and outputs a voltage proportional to the pressure on its sensing element, 0 V at 0 bar to 5 V at 10 bar. The board's analog pins tolerate only 3.3 V, so each signal first passes a 15 kΩ / 22 kΩ divider, which scales 5 V down to about 2.97 V.

The microcontroller reads the three channels 100 times a second, each sample the sum of 16 readings to average out noise, and sends them to the computer in batches every 200 ms. The software converts each count to a pin voltage, undoes the divider and rescales 0 to 5 V onto 0 to 10 bar. The three pressures from the same instant are then combined as TMP = (Pfeed + Pretentate) / 2 − Ppermeate (Millipore Corporation, 2003), and the value reported is the median over a 30 second window.

That is the instrument as it now stands. The August series below predate the permeate channel being logged, so the quantity in those tables and plots is (Pfeed − Pretentate) / 2, a pressure drop along the lumen rather than across the wall. Readings are in psi, converted to bar.

Conclusion

Flow and pressure can be measured reliably enough to act on. The flow meter matches manual measurement with R² = 0.995, and flow rises by about 22 mL/min per pump dial up to dial 50. The 4,000 to 6,000 s⁻¹ shear band we work to corresponds to 260 to 390 mL/min, or roughly dials 16 to 22, which is where the reactor is run.

That is enough to set the two alarms the farmer actually needs. A leak shows as a sudden drop at a fixed dial, well below the flow normally seen there, which is about 355 mL/min at dial 20. A blockage shows as pressure rising while flow does not move: with the module installed the drop runs about 0.19 to 0.37 bar across 260 to 390 mL/min, so we propose flagging anything above roughly 1.3 times that, about 0.24 to 0.48 bar, at the same flow. Both are thresholds proposed from these calibration runs; neither has yet fired on a real fault.

What the two readings still owe is a TMP. Every pressure figure in this chapter is the lumen drop, and the 14 August series stayed positive across 0.01 to 0.26 bar, which is consistent with filtrate being pushed out of the lumen but does not demonstrate it, because the permeate reading is not in those files. The permeate channel is now logged and the formula is in the software, so the next run on this module can close the one gap this chapter leaves open.

Hollow fiber membrane breach detector

The hollow fiber is where the culture and harvest stream touch, and a 0.2 µm pore is what keeps B. subtilis on the lumen side. If breached, the cells would be able to come through, so a detector is installed on the shell side to watch for signs that may indicate unwelcomed particles.

Fitted, but no breach test is on record. It currently rests on an offline proxy that was run once. During version 1, 2 mL was drawn from the shell side and spread on LB agar, and no colonies were recovered, so no culturable B. subtilis reached the shell side in that run. It is a single plating, and the replicate count, incubation time and incubation temperature were not recorded.

No detectable B. subtilis colonies were observed in the shell-side sample under the tested conditions. This provides preliminary evidence that the hollow-fiber membrane retained bacterial cells during the tested run.

What this plate can carry is a containment claim for one run; it cannot stand in for the detector, because a plate takes a day to answer and an instrument has to answer while the reactor is running. The build is on Hardware → Bioreactor and the containment argument it belongs to is on Safety.

Software algorithm calibration

An OD600 reading is not a measurement until you know what the arithmetic between the light and the number did to it. This assay opened our own photometer firmware, derived the bias of every step in it, and then measured those biases against a scatterer that does not grow.

The four other families on this page calibrate instruments against standards. This one calibrates the software that turns a sensor into an instrument. It began because our longest culture to date returned a curve we could not defend, and it ended with a configuration change to the bioreactor, a pre-registered decision rule, and a raw data set complete enough for another team to replay on their own photometer.

What the instrument had already recorded

Our 434-hour culture produced a curve full of steps. The steps were not in the culture. They were in the reference channel, and the log had been carrying the evidence all along.

Between 21 July and 8 August the inline photometer ran continuously and wrote 2,671 valid readings. We reopened that log and looked only at the reference channel, the sensor on the 10% tap that never crosses the medium. It sat at about 32 sensor counts for the whole run. A detector resolves whole counts, so the fewer counts a channel carries, the more optical density a single count is worth: at 32 counts and our path-length factor of 5, one count moves the reported OD600 by 0.067.

The firmware takes sixteen samples per reading and averages the ones that survive an outlier gate. If that averaging were working, the results would spread between the integers. Instead 91.5% of them sit exactly on an integer. The sub-count resolution that averaging is supposed to buy was being thrown away before it reached the log.

The second figure is the consequence. Across the quiet stretch from hour 40 to hour 230 the reference channel changed count 29 times, and 26 of those changes arrived with a jump in reported OD600 larger than 0.04. Those jumps belong to the instrument. A culture does not step.

Reading the firmware explained the first figure. The outlier gate keeps samples within three median absolute deviations of the median. When more than half of sixteen samples carry the same integer, the median absolute deviation is zero, so the fence has zero width and every sample that differs from the median is discarded as a bubble. The filter collapses into taking the most common count. Our firmware offers two further estimators in its interface, qn and biweight, and all three share a scale floor of 10−6, so on quantised data all three do the same thing. The rejection percentage the dashboard had been reporting as "bubbles" was, in that regime, counting quantisation flicker.

The hypothesis

Every step between the light and the number carries a bias whose direction and size can be written down before anything is measured. We derived six of them: resolution lost at the sensor (ΔOD ≈ 0.434 k/N), the gap between averaging and taking the logarithm (0.217 kσr2), one-sided occlusion by bubbles, the degenerate outlier gate above, a stray-light ceiling at −k log10(s), and the delay a smoother adds. The sharpest prediction fell out of the third: if bubbles only block light, then an estimator that trusts the brighter half of each burst should scatter far less than a symmetric one. That prediction is what the calibration run was built to overturn or confirm.

One data set, eleven algorithms

The design fits in a sentence: record the raw samples once, then replay every algorithm on those same samples offline. Any difference between two algorithms is then a difference in arithmetic and nothing else.

Comparing estimators by switching the firmware between them does not work, because each estimator then sees a different burst and the comparison carries burst-to-burst randomness. So the first change was to the firmware itself: version 1.2.0 writes the individual samples inside every burst, logs all eight estimators on every reading, and records which profile was active in an alg_profile column. With that in place, one run feeds every algorithm identical input.

Eleven algorithms, and why seven of them are controls

Four are the estimators already selectable in our firmware. The other seven exist to bound the answer from both sides. A plain mean and a median are the naive baselines any team would reach for. The log-then-mean algorithm is a deliberate negative control: theory says it must read high by 0.217 kσr2, so if the replay does not reproduce that offset, it is the replay that is wrong and not the algorithm. The percentile band and the mean of paired ratios test two specific ideas, one about bubbles and one about a flickering source. And mad with its scale floor raised to one count is the single-variable control on the degeneracy above: it differs from the shipped estimator in exactly one constant.

Written down before the data existed

A comparison chosen after seeing the results is not a comparison. The algorithm list above, the five metrics, the selection rule and eight numbered predictions were committed to our repository before the first measurement, and the commit timestamp is the evidence that the plan preceded the data. The metrics are bias against the line through the lowest third of the ladder, repeatability as the standard deviation of bursts at one level, a limit of detection at three times the blank standard deviation and a limit of quantification at ten times, the highest level at which bias stays within 5%, and hysteresis between the ascending and descending passes. The rule: among algorithms holding bias within 5% in every arm across the validated range, adopt the one with the lowest repeatability under bubbles; on a tie, the simplest. Results for all eleven are published whichever is chosen.

The exclusion rule was fixed in the same file, and it is narrow on purpose. A burst is excluded only for a logged I²C failure or a logged operator error. Nothing is excluded for looking wrong.

The silica run, and what it overturned

Separating instrument bias from biology needs a scatterer that does not grow, change shape or die. On 10 October we ran one for four and a half hours and logged every sample it produced.

The standard, and where the units come from

The ladder is built from monodisperse silicon dioxide microspheres (Tomicro, mean diameter 1 µm, stock 25 mg/mL), close to the size of a B. subtilis cell and stable over the length of a run. Forty-one concentrations were made by the withdraw-one, add-one protocol, so the concentration at step n follows from the volumes alone:

Cn = Cstock × [1 − ((V − 1)/V)n]

That matters more than it looks. The concentration axis of this calibration is defined by pipetted volumes and a measured hold-up of 50 mL, not by any instrument's reading. The benchtop BioDrop is in the loop as a third opinion on the same liquid, not as the truth; two of the eight predictions, H1 and H2, exist to check the referee before the referee is used.

Two photometer builds ran in series on one loop: V1, the Pro beam-splitter build described in the bioreactor section, and V2, the simplified fibre build, the Slim. One reading every 5 s, all eight estimators logged on every reading, raw bursts written to disk. As an arithmetic control, dropping the first two samples of each burst, subtracting the dark level, estimating each channel and then taking the ratio reproduces all eight logged OD columns from the raw bursts to within 0.005 OD, which is how we know the replay is doing what the firmware does.

In clean flow the estimator is not the lever

Across the forty concentrations with the loop running clean, the largest gap between any two of the eight estimators was 0.0039 OD on V1 and 0.0018 OD on V2. The residual scatter of our own August calibration was 0.024 OD, so in clean flow the choice of estimator is worth less than a fifth of the noise. H5 held. Everything that had been argued about in the interface turned out not to matter when nothing goes wrong.

With bubbles the premise fails

H6 predicted that upper, which trusts only the brighter half of each burst, would scatter at most half as much as mad. It scattered 0.77 against 0.78 on V1 and 0.15 against 0.16 on V2, and on V1 its mean error was twice that of mad. The reason is visible in the raw bursts: a bubble does not only block light. On V1, at OD 3.2, a bubble replaces a slice of scattering liquid with air and up to 2.8 times more light arrives; on V2, whose fibre collects over a wide angle, a bubble refracts light out of that cone and the reading darkens. The two units disagree in direction, so any estimator that assumes a direction is betting on the geometry it happens to be sitting in. No estimator put more than 35% of bubble readings within ±0.05 of the true level.

Worse, the firmware's own bubble flag catches the wrong failure. When the fourteen samples in a burst disagree, the flag fires on 94% of them on V1 and 79% on V2. That makes the failure detectable, if not correctable. When the flow cell fills completely with air the fourteen samples agree perfectly and are all wrong together: V1 read 0.74 to 0.91 against a true 3.18, and the flag caught none of it. Nothing inside such a burst looks abnormal. Only a comparison with the previous reading reveals it.

The lever is the gate after the estimator

We replayed the same data at the bioreactor's 30-second cadence through five reporting pipelines, from the configuration that was running on 10 October to one with two gates added: a spread gate that rejects a reading when mad and the 90th percentile disagree by more than 0.03 OD, and a jump gate that holds back any reading more than max(0.05 OD, 5%) from the last accepted value and accepts a new level only after three agreeing readings.

The reference channel, measured against a real disturbance

Partway through the run someone touched the fibre or the source and both of V2's channels fell together to 0.6 of their brightness. Dual-channel OD drifted −0.0005 OD per minute across that plateau while sample-only OD drifted 0.025, so on V2 the reference does the job it was built for. On V1 it does not: the fraction of a reference change that reaches the sample channel runs from −1.2 to 0.97 with a median near 0.3, and in a 24-minute hold the dual-channel reading moved further than the sample channel alone would have. The two sides of that beam splitter are not seeing the same light. Within plateaus V1 drifts up to 0.015 OD per minute while V2, on the same liquid at the same moment, shows 0.0000.

Three predictions held, five failed

Both numbers are reported. The failure that matters is H6, because it was the premise the shipped estimator was chosen on. Two thresholds in the gates were set on the data that produced them, the spread threshold of 0.03 OD having come from the largest spread among clean readings. The next bioreactor run is their out-of-sample test, with the thresholds frozen in the pre-registration before it starts. The jump threshold and the three-reading rule were fixed beforehand and not tuned.

What this calibration does not establish

Silica calibrates the instrument, not the organism. A 1 µm bead is a sphere of refractive index 1.46 and density 2.3 g/cm³; a vegetative B. subtilis cell is a rod, often in chains, nearer 1.33 to 1.41 and 1.1 g/cm³. The ladder therefore converts a reading into equivalent particle counts and not into cell mass, and a dilution series of washed cells is still owed before the two can be related. The gates have a stated limit of their own: when an air slug lasts longer than 90 seconds, three agreeing readings make the wrong level acceptable. That is why line 3 above raises an alarm instead of silently correcting: a growing culture does not move 0.05 OD in 30 seconds, so any accepted step that large deserves a human look. On 10 October the real cause was an inlet tube that had fallen out of the beaker, and one alarm would have had someone put it back inside the first minute.

Optogenetic control

Can the light plate deliver a controlled signal for optogenetic activation? ReLEAF depends on light as the external control signal connecting environmental prediction to biological production. We therefore tested the light plate to determine whether it could provide reproducible illumination across the culture area.

A predicted environmental condition is turned into a light input that tunes production of ACCD in B. subtilis through the CcaSR red and green light-sensing system (Castillo-Hair et al., 2019). For that to work, light has to act as a reliable dial. We need to know whether low, medium and high intensities give clearly separated levels of ACCD expression, and whether the light itself affects cell growth. The Light Plate Apparatus is an open-hardware, 24-well platform with independently programmable LEDs in every well (Gerhardt et al., 2016), so several intensities of red and green can run side by side in one experiment on one culture. Following the established CcaSR protocol for B. subtilis (Haller et al., 2025), we track the response over time with OD600 and Western blot. The settings that give a reproducible, graded response become the intensity levels ReLEAF uses to control ACCD production, and because every conclusion depends on the plate, the plate is what we characterized first.

What the measurement contributes

The Light Plate Apparatus is a general platform for characterizing light-inducible gene expression, not a CcaSR instrument. A team can survey light-responsive genetic modules beyond CcaSR and fit LEDs matched to each module's activating wavelength, which turns the same plate into a characterization tool for a wide range of optogenetic systems. What we add is the step before that: a worked method for matching cheap single-bulb LEDs by measured output, so the wells in one column receive the same light and the columns differ from one another by a measured amount.

Plant assays

Four measurements share two growing platforms. The agar assay sets the stress and the dose on a flat surface; the hydroponic assay puts the same protectants in front of a root system standing in liquid. The chlorophyll extraction reads how much photosynthetic machinery survived either one, and the physical measurements give the root length and the fresh weight that every chlorophyll number is normalised against.

Agar plate stress test, horizontal and vertical

To know whether a protectant rescues a stressed plant, and whether the timing of the dose matters, we needed a platform where the stress dose, the protectant and the timing could each be changed one at a time. Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings on ½ Murashige and Skoog agar (Murashige & Skoog, 1962) are that platform.

Salinity stress damages plants in readable ways. Exposure of Arabidopsis to NaCl alters stress-response gene expression (Cheng et al., 2013; Rolly et al., 2020) and, at the whole-seedling level, stunts root growth, bleaches leaves through loss of chlorophyll (chlorosis), and turns leaves purple through anthocyanin accumulation. Stress ethylene contributes to these effects as it inhibits root elongation (Růžička et al., 2007) and impairs photosynthesis (Ceusters & Van De Poel, 2018), which is the reason why the bacterial enzyme ACC deaminase (ACCD), which breaks down the ethylene precursor ACC, is one of our protectants (Glick, 2014). With the damage showing up in root length differences, biomass and leaf pigment, a protectant that works should be able to lengthen roots, raise biomass, and preserve chlorophyll compared with a stressed and untreated plate.

Agar buys three things soil cannot. Every seedling gets identical nutrients and an exact stress dose, where soil varies in moisture, pH and minerals from pot to pot. Physiological changes show within days, so many conditions can be screened quickly. And it fixes the salinity window, the protectant dose and the protocol before any of them are carried into soil.

Vertical square plates

The plates are designed to let roots grow along the surface under gravity, so primary root length and lateral roots can be measured from photographs. We used them for the trehalose experiments in Phase 1 and planned them for the heat tests.

Horizontal circular plates

The plates are designed to hold liquid protectant on the agar surface, which vertical plates cannot fulfill. We used them for the CH bio peptide and ACCD experiments in Phases 2 and 3, where treatment timing of salinity was a variable (pre-treatment, co-treatment, post-treatment). Horizontal circular plates also provide higher capacities of n values in each trial which minimizes inconsistencies; for example, vertical square plates can allow the testing of around 9 to 10 Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings while horizontal circular plates can hold around 40 seedlings.

The experiments ran in three phases, ordered so that each one checked the platform before the next relied on it. Phase 1 used trehalose, a disaccharide reported to protect Arabidopsis under salinity stress (Kim et al., 2025), to find a NaCl level that gives a readable signal and to confirm the platform can show a rescue. Phase 2 used the CH Bio peptide, a commercial product from CH Biotech, as the reference protectant and to test application timing. Phase 3 used ACC deaminase, LEA14, and BoPep4 secreted by our engineered Bacillus subtilis. A heat-stress version of the assay, based on published thermopriming protocols (Lee et al., 2023; Auverlot et al., 2025), was designed for the same protectants.

What the platform is useful for

Seedlings on agar are one of the most widely used formats in plant biology and the platform described here applies beyond our project in several ways.

  • Screening for salt-tolerance of plants. Arabidopsis grown on agar supplemented with NaCl is a standard way to test how genes, mutants, or treatments change salt tolerance (Cheng et al., 2013; Rolly et al., 2020). Our dose-response data tested salinity concentrations of 75, 100, 150 and 250 mM NaCl, read against the control group on the same plate. At 75 mM, fresh weight and total chlorophyll stayed near control levels. At 100 mM growth was clearly reduced and the rescue effect was still visible, which is what makes it the working concentration. At 150 mM growth was stunted to about the same degree as at 100 mM but with little rescue, total chlorophyll fell sharply, and anthocyanin accumulated enough to purple the leaves. At 250 mM close to every sample showed chlorosis and severe bleaching and none recovered. The boundaries are approximate percentages of control and not fixed thresholds, because each trial differed. The discovery allows future researchers to skip the pretests and begin in the 75 to 100 mM window where growth is reduced but rescue is still visible.
  • Testing plant growth-promoting bacteria and biostimulants. Microbes that lower plant ethylene with ACC deaminase are used to promote plant growth under stress (Glick, 2014). Plate assays are a common way to test bacterial effects on Arabidopsis stress tolerance (Lee et al., 2023). Our design compares bacterial products at defined timings and with equal liquid controls. The standardized protocols allow a reusable screen for any protectant testing, so other teams and researchers can test their own molecule or microbe with the same readouts, plates, and analysis.
  • Testing exogenous protective compounds. Sugars such as trehalose have been tested on Arabidopsis thaliana under salinity stress (Kim et al., 2025). Our trehalose sets show how to find the usable dose range of a candidate compound. Too high of concentrations might inhibit and harm plants as well because it triggers the toxic accumulation of an intermediate signaling metabolite, trehalose-6-phosphate (T6P), which disrupts normal carbon allocation and energy signaling, causing growth arrest such as stunted roots and undeveloped leaves (Schluepmann et al., 2004).
  • Heat-stress assays. The same plates and readouts can be used with thermopriming protocols (Lee et al., 2023; Auverlot et al., 2025).
  • Timing studies for delivery driven by sensors. The protectant is applied before, with, or after stress; therefore, the platform can test whether early delivery matters. This directly connects to the question behind AIoT-based stress prediction and sensing in the field.
  • Teaching and iGEM labs. The equipment is inexpensive (plates, a camera in a light box, a balance, and free ImageJ software) and every step is described below in the procedural steps.
  • Honest record of failure modes. Especially for contamination issues or failure to see effectiveness in protectant doses, so other teams can avoid repeating them as to also provide improvements and suggestions to protocols for higher quality readouts.

Growth procedure medium, sterilization, stratification and the daily record
Neutral ½ MS medium agar preparation for stratification
  1. Prepare the ½ MS medium powder without sucrose, MES buffer, distilled water, sucrose (1%) and agar powder in the amounts in the materials table.
  2. Add the contents above into a flask.
  3. Use a hot plate to stir, with a magnetic stirring rod, until no sediments are left in the solution.
  4. Use the pH probe and measure the pH of the solution (target pH is 5.7 to 5.8). If the pH is too low, add 0.1 M KOH slowly to increase the pH. If the pH is too high, add 0.1 M HCl slowly to decrease the pH.
  5. Wrap the neck of the flask with aluminum foil and stick a piece of indicator tape on top.
  6. Autoclave for 20 minutes at 127 °C. Wait for the pressure to reach 0 before retrieving the flask.
  7. During the autoclave, label all of the petri dishes with a marker in the biosafety cabinet. Include agar percentage and date the agar was made.
  8. Transfer half of the flasks to a laminar flow hood. Store the other half of the flasks in the hot bath at 60 °C while pouring so that the agar solution does not solidify immediately.
  9. Make sure the surface is dry by wiping the flasks with a paper towel before spraying 70% EtOH on them.
  10. Pour each of the medium mixtures into their petri dishes (around 30 mL for horizontal round plates and 50 mL for vertical square plates).
  11. Transfer the other half of the flasks into the laminar flow hood and pour each of the medium mixtures into the petri dishes.
  12. Let the petri dishes solidify at room temperature (around 25 °C) for around 10 minutes under the laminar flow hood with the plastic lid on.
Seed sterilization and germination
  1. Surface-sterilize seeds, then rinse with sterile water.
  2. Sow seeds on plain ½ MS agar plates, with no NaCl and no protectant: 40 seeds per plate on the horizontal round plates and 33 seedlings per plate on the vertical square plates, following the seed templates.
  3. Use breathable tape to seal each of the agar plates.
  4. Wrap the agar plates with aluminum foil.
  5. Stratify in the dark at 4 °C. Stratification synchronizes germination so seedlings are uniform and have consistent growth conditions.
  6. Move plates to the growth chamber. Grow for 4 days (horizontal round plates) or 7 days (vertical square plates) before protectant dosing and salinity concentration stress trials.

The sterilizing agent, its concentration and contact time, the number of rinses, and the growth chamber's temperature, light intensity and photoperiod are not recorded.

Recording every day

Take the agar plates one by one out of the growth chamber and place them on the black background platform, bottom lid facing up. Take pictures, clearly recording the labels, with no reflections, avoiding water vapor covering the view of the plants. Place the agar plates back, tilted slightly to the back on the rack, in the growth chamber.

Unwrap the breathable tape. Place the agar plates on a black background platform, open lid, leaves facing up; on the last day, take out the lid and take the picture directly from the top view and measure fresh weight and chlorophyll data afterwards. Wrap breathable tape around the agar plates and place them back in the growth chamber.

Stress assays the vertical and horizontal plate assays, step by step
Vertical plate assay (Phase 1, trehalose)
  1. Take the stack of agar plates wrapped in aluminum foil out of the refrigerator.
  2. Spray alcohol on the agar plates before placing them inside the laminar flow hood.
  3. Use a marker to draw a straight baseline along a ruler 5.5 cm from the top of the plate on the back of each salinity plate.
  4. With an alcohol marker, mark 9 dots along the baseline in equal distances. The square agar plate is 12 cm horizontally with 10 spaces between the borders of the plate and between each seedling, so 12/10 = 1.2 cm as distance between each seedling.
  5. Place the square plates vertically at a slight angle.
  6. Choose 9 germinated seeds after vernalization that have similar root lengths or germination status and align the endpoints of their roots to the line drawn on the plate.
  7. Seal the plates with breathable tape and label the incubation date with a marker before placing the vertical plates on the stacks in the growth chamber, slightly tilted towards the back.
  8. Photograph the plates daily in the light box. A set runs for 6 to 14 days, depending on how fast the stress reads.
  9. At the end of the set, measure root length in ImageJ, weigh fresh weight and extract chlorophyll.

Each plate carries one condition, so the NaCl and trehalose concentrations are poured into the medium rather than applied to the surface.

Horizontal plate assay (Phases 2 and 3), heat stress

Transferring the seedlings

  1. Take the stack of agar plates wrapped in aluminum foil out of the refrigerator.
  2. Spray alcohol on the agar plates before placing them inside the laminar flow hood.
  3. Draw a straight line along a ruler 5.5 cm from the top of the plate, using the seed template.
  4. Place the plates at a slight angle.
  5. Choose 12 germinated seeds of similar root length or germination status and align the endpoints of their roots to the drawn line.
  6. Set up four groups: control, pre-treatment one day before, co-treatment on day 0, and post-treatment one day after.
  7. Seal the plates with breathable tape, label the transfer date, and place them on the rack in the growth chamber, tilted slightly towards the back.

Applying the treatments

  1. Pre-treatment, one day before. Open the biosafety cabinet. Dilute the CH peptide 1/500, that is 19.96 mL of sterile ddH₂O to 40 µL of peptide, in a 50 mL centrifuge tube and fit a sprayer. Take out the plate labelled pre-treatment, hold it vertically, and spray the peptide over the plants from leaves to roots at a distance of 15 cm, about two sprays, making sure every plant is covered. Seal with breathable tape and return it to the growth chamber.
  2. Co-treatment, day 0. Take out the plate labelled co-treatment, hold it vertically, and spray evenly at 15 cm, about two sprays. Seal with breathable tape.
  3. Place all four groups on a rack, plates standing at a slight angle, and put the rack into the incubator at 37 °C under white light for thermopriming.
  4. Observe the seedlings for a further 5 days in the incubator.
  5. Post-treatment, one day after thermopriming. Take the post-treatment plate out of the incubator, hold it vertically, spray evenly at 15 cm, seal with breathable tape and return it to the incubator.
Horizontal plate assay (Phases 2 and 3), salinity
  1. Label each plate with its timing group, pre-treatment, co-treatment, post-treatment or control, and its NaCl level, 0, 75 or 100 mM. That gives twelve conditions. For the ACC deaminase runs the control is TB medium rather than water.
  2. CH bio peptide, Phase 2. Dilute the peptide 1/500 in sterile water. Pipette 3 mL onto the agar surface to submerge it, then draw 2 mL back off, leaving 1 mL. The extra volume is what overcomes the surface tension of the agar, which otherwise leaves the liquid sitting unevenly. Add 1 mL of sterile water to every group not receiving the protectant, so the liquid volume is the same in all of them.
  3. ACC deaminase, Phase 3. Filter the preparation through a 0.22 µm filter inside the biosafety cabinet. Hold a sterile spray nozzle 10 cm above the plate and give three sprays, one top left, one top right and one at the bottom. Control plates are sprayed with TB medium.
  4. Apply the protectant on day −1 for pre-treatment, day 0 for co-treatment or day +1 for post-treatment. NaCl goes onto every corresponding plate on day 0, at the same time as the co-treatment.
  5. Photograph the plates in the biosafety cabinet and observe for 5 days.
  6. On the last day, harvest the seedlings for fresh weight and chlorophyll extraction.

Results

Across eight experimental sets the agar platform answered the three questions it was built for. The stress dose has a usable window: rescue is readable between 75 and 100 mM NaCl, 150 mM stunts growth to roughly the same degree as 100 mM but leaves little room for a protectant to show an effect, and 250 mM bleached close to every seedling past recovery. Protectant dose matters in both directions, with 1 mM trehalose toxic and 0.5 mM too weak before the range was narrowed. And timing is readable: in the heat series above, ACC deaminase applied a day before the stress gave the longest roots of the four timing arms, which is the observation the whole delivery argument depends on.

Hydroponic stress test

We transplanted Arabidopsis into a hydroponic box to evaluate the efficacy of protectants on plants. We use hydroponics to test the effectiveness because we can control the environmental factors precisely. First, the agar in the lid and the medium in the hydroponic box ensure a complete nutrient supply for the plants. We also control humidity and temperature in a constant range to fix the outer conditions that may affect the results of experiments.

Agar settles the dose and the timing on a flat surface; hydroponics is where the same protectants meet a root system standing in liquid, which is the condition a field application has to work in. The two platforms share their readouts, so a number taken here can be set beside a number taken on a plate. What hydroponics does not give is root length: the roots grow into the well and cannot be photographed against a scale, so the readouts are fresh weight, chlorophyll and the daily photographs.

What the platform is useful for

A reproducible hydroponic cultivation system. Hydroponics provides a stable environment for plant research by fixing nutrient availability, water purity and stress intensity. We tested two systems, the tip box and the tube rack, and record the materials, the assembly, the nutrient medium and the environmental conditions for both, so another team can rebuild either one.

A salinity-stress protocol. We took the salt concentration from Cortés-Jiménez et al. (2014). After several pretests, 100 mM NaCl is the concentration at which plants are damaged and can still be rescued by a protectant.

A heat-stress protocol. We followed the thermopriming designs of Lee et al. (2023) and Auverlot et al. (2025), the same two sources the agar assay uses, and exposed plants at 37 °C and 42 °C. The groups at 42 °C did not survive the day, while the groups at 37 °C were rescued by the CH peptide. A team setting up a heat assay can start from 37 °C and treat 42 °C as past the point of recovery.

A working dilution range for a bacterial supernatant. We tested 1/40, 1/60, 1/80 and 1/100 dilutions of both protectants. The plants tolerate all four, and 1/40, 1/60 and 1/80 are the three the team reads as giving the best rescue, ACCD under heat and BoPep4 under both heat and salinity. The finding another team can take without qualification is the ceiling: the carrier medium, and not the protectant, sets the strongest dose a plant will tolerate.

A contamination finding. Plants grown in the tube rack were contaminated less often than plants in the tip box. Our working explanation is the transfer step: tip box plants were moved from the rack at four days old, and although a group would usually survive three to four days afterwards, most ended in severe contamination. Plants left in the rack never have their roots handled.

A record of four boxes that did not work. We troubleshot four hydroponic boxes before the fifth, a 1000 µL tip box with a 3D printed board and a 3D printed black cover to keep light off the medium. The tip box is less stable than the tube rack, and we still offer the fifth design as a prototype other teams can modify.

Finding a dilution the plants survive

TB at full strength kills the plants, so the first run was a pretest on TB medium carrying B. subtilis and no protectant, to separate the carrier from anything the protectant might do. We tested 1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 and 1/16, with 0 as the control, at pH 5.7 and pH 7. The optimum for Arabidopsis is 5.7 and ACCD works best at about 7.5 (Liu et al., 2022), which Arabidopsis will not tolerate, so 7 is the compromise we tested against.

Every TB concentration in the pretest was too strong for the plants to survive, including 1/16. That is a failed rescue window, and it is still the reading that set the next run: the damage falls off steadily with dilution, so the usable range had to be below 1/16.

The salinity run that followed tested ACCD at 1/10, 1/20, 1/40 and 1/60 against 0 ACCD, each with and without 100 mM NaCl, and carried a separate group of 1/20 TB alone at both salt levels. The spacing was deliberate: the pretest had already ruled out everything down to 1/16, so most of the new series went below that, and 1/10 was kept as the single dose stronger than anything the pretest tolerated, to check that the damage really did continue to rise with concentration rather than plateau. We had expected 1/20 to be dilute enough for the plants to live and 1/40 and 1/60 to be almost without effect.

The expectation was wrong in one place and right in the other. The 1/20 WB800 negative control itself showed a clear stress response, smaller plants and anthocyanin accumulation, so 1/20 of the carrier affects growth on its own. ACCD at 1/10 and 1/20 bleached leaves even at 0 mM NaCl, while 1/40 did not, and the 1/40 group carried the highest fresh weight and chlorophyll content of the unstressed groups. We therefore fixed 1/40 as the strongest dose in every later run, and these two runs together narrowed the working range from undiluted TB to 1/40 through 1/100.

BoPep4 across the same dilutions, under salt

BoPep4 was then run over 1/40, 1/60, 1/80 and 1/100, with and without 100 mM NaCl. The series starts at 1/40 because that was the strongest dose the ACCD salinity run tolerated. The controls are the same two: 1/40 WB800, which is the carrier without a protectant, and 0.1× MS medium alone.

Without salt, the BoPep4 wells show slight anthocyanin accumulation at every dilution from 1/40 to 1/100, while neither 1/40 WB800 nor plain 0.1× MS medium reddens. On an unstressed plant, in other words, BoPep4 looks mildly costly rather than beneficial.

Under 100 mM NaCl the result reverses. Most groups bleached and died; the exception is 1/40 BoPep4, where the plants mostly stayed green, and that group also carries the highest fresh weight and the highest chlorophyll per plant of anything at 100 mM. The reading we take from the set is narrow: BoPep4 at 1/40 improves salinity tolerance, and the weaker dilutions do not separate from their controls.

Both readouts are single plates with no technical replication, so the gap between 1/40 BoPep4 and the rest of the salted block is what the run supports, and the ordering among 1/60, 1/80 and 1/100 is not.

Chlorophyll extraction

To assess the physiological response of Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings under stress, and evaluate protectant treatment rescue effectiveness in our project, chlorophyll extraction is introduced as a precise tool of measurement to strengthen the analysis of each experiment's results.

Chlorophyll is the pigment that captures light energy for photosynthesis, so how much of it a leaf holds tracks how well the plant is growing (Roberts, 2023). The metabolic pathways that govern the chlorophyll biosynthesis are complex, which abiotic stress such as salinity stress, drought, extreme temperature variations can substantially perturb these processes (Li et al., 2024).

As connected to our project, we mainly focus on two abiotic stress types: salinity and heat. Given this category, these abiotic stressors trigger the overproduction of reactive oxygen species, leading to lipid peroxidation and chlorosis. Hence, extracting the chlorophyll as a form of data readout and measurement allows quantification on the degree of chlorosis, and permits accurate evaluation of plant physiological response in our experiments that cannot be visually and directly assessed through bare eyes.

Comparison of fresh weight-normalized and non-normalized chlorophyll content

Dividing by fresh weight is the standard way to report chlorophyll, and it is the wrong way round when stress has destroyed the biomass that sits in the denominator. The case that showed us this is the ACC deaminase heat stress assay described in the physical measurements chapter, and the numbers below come from that one experiment.

Seedlings given no protectant and held at 37 °C for 5 days lost 92 to 97% of their fresh weight: the stressed groups weighed about 0.02 g against about 0.67 g unstressed, weighed per group on the analytical balance. Expressed per gram, those flattened seedlings then appeared to hold about three times more chlorophyll than the unstressed plants, 0.366 against 0.121 mg g−1, computed from the same absorbance readings as every other value in this chapter. Total chlorophyll in the extract, the same readings without the division, had fallen by about 91%. The two measures point in opposite directions, and only one of them agrees with the photographs.

Fresh weight also includes roots, which hold no chlorophyll, so a per-gram value moves with the shoot-to-root ratio as well as with pigment. We therefore report both measures and choose between them by the situation: chlorophyll per gram of fresh weight where biomass is comparable across groups, and total chlorophyll in the extract where it is not.

Protectant assays

Three measurements follow the protein from the cell to the working enzyme. The Western blot asks whether a protein of the right size leaves the cell. The ELISA puts a concentration on it. The DNPH assay reads a product of the reaction the enzyme is supposed to catalyse, and so is the only one that says whether the protein does anything. A concentration and an activity answer different questions and are not interchangeable, which is why both are here.

A plant bioassay is the most complete test of whether a protectant works, and it is also the slowest and the least forgiving. A run depends on plant husbandry and cultivation stability, and a contaminated box or an uneven stress spoils weeks of work before it says anything about the protein. These biochemical assays exist so that production can be optimised in the reactor without waiting on a plant.

They are ordered by cost. The Western blot is the cheap first screen: it says whether a band of the right size appears under a given production condition. Once the conditions are set, the ELISA is what turns that into a yield in µg/mL. Which assay settles a protectant then depends on what the molecule is.

  • Enzymes. ACC deaminase has catalytic activity of its own, so it can be validated functionally and quantified directly by the DNPH colorimetric assay.
  • Non-enzymatic proteins. LEA14 catalyses nothing, so there is no activity to read and the ELISA is what quantifies it.
  • Peptides. BoPep4 is small enough that a standard Western blot does not resolve or reliably detect it, which leaves the ELISA as its only detection and quantification route here.

Western blot

To verify whether the engineered B. subtilis could successfully produce and secrete protein or peptide products, we performed a Western blot to detect the secreted protein via the His tag we added at the C-terminus.

Western blotting separates proteins by molecular weight using polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, transfers them onto a PVDF membrane, and uses a specific antibody to visualize the target. Standard quantification assays return a total; they cannot tell our protein from the host's. The blot answers a narrower question that the total cannot: is a band present, and is it the size the construct predicts.

What the measurement contributes

Sample preparation from a bioreactor. We established a workflow for separating and concentrating protein from bioreactor culture supernatant, which arrives dilute and carrying medium components.

Verification of recombinant protein expression. Using an anti-His antibody against our ACCD construct, we confirmed the presence and the molecular weight of the expressed bioprotectant. We also recorded where the expressed protein ran larger than its amino acid sequence predicts, which fusion tags or incomplete denaturation can both cause, so a team reading an unexpected band has a documented case to compare against.

Semi-quantification across a run. We tracked ACCD across bioreactor sampling points, collecting liquid every two hours, to find the phase of peak production.

Competitive His-tag ELISA

To evaluate whether our Bacillus subtilis successfully secretes bioprotectants, the amount of protein in culture samples must be accurately measured. A total protein assay such as Bradford cannot separate the target from the host’s own proteins, and gel-based methods are only semi-quantitative.

All three of our proteins carry a polyhistidine tag, the same handle used for affinity purification (Bornhorst & Falke, 2000), so an immunoassay against the tag gives a quantitative and independent measure of expression. It is sensitive, and it processes many samples in parallel, which is what comparing strains, constructs and culture conditions needs.

ELISA quantifies a molecule through antigen–antibody binding. We use the competitive format, in which the target competes with a labelled tracer for a limited number of antibody binding sites, so the signal falls as the target concentration rises. The sandwich format is more specific but needs a matched antibody pair for every target, which we would have had to raise three times over.

We used the competitive format because our recombinant proteins share a His tag and can therefore be quantified by competition for one anti-His antibody, with no separate antibody pair per protein. His-tagged proteins in the sample compete with an alkaline phosphatase-labelled His tracer for the anti-His monoclonal antibody, which is itself captured on a goat anti-mouse IgG-coated plate. Bound tracer converts pNPP substrate to an absorbance signal read at 405 to 420 nm. Because the signal decreases as concentration rises, a standard curve converts absorbance back into recombinant protein concentration.

What the measurement contributes

The assay gives a concentration for ACC deaminase, LEA14 and BoPep4 in bacterial culture samples, which lets production be compared across experimental conditions. We ran it with a six-point standard curve, duplicate measurements and binding controls, so the assay's own performance can be judged from the same plate as the samples. Alongside the Western blot and the activity assays it separates two questions that are easy to confuse: how much protein is present, and how much of it is working. The approach transfers to any microbial system expressing His-tagged recombinant proteins.

DNPH assay for ACC deaminase activity

To determine whether engineered Bacillus subtilis WB800 can produce and secrete active ACC deaminase, we measure the enzymatic cleavage of ACC into α-ketobutyrate and ammonia. The 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) assay reads the α-ketobutyrate directly.

Under acidic conditions DNPH reacts with the carbonyl group of α-ketobutyrate to form a hydrazone, which is made visible with alkali and quantified at 540 nm (Penrose & Glick, 2003; Kozaeva et al., 2022). The method answers the three objections to reading ammonium in our samples: the ACC substrate does not interfere, the background in a rich culture medium is far lower, and the assay is nineteen times more sensitive. That sensitivity is what makes initial velocities measurable and so puts true kinetic parameters, Km and kcat, within reach. Paired with growth and expression readouts, it tells us whether the enzyme leaving the cell is catalytically intact and not merely present.

What the measurement contributes

We scaled the conventional protocol of Penrose and Glick (2003) down twenty-fold while holding the reaction proportions, which moves the assay onto a 96-well microplate and makes screening a strain library practical. With it we compared ACC deaminase activity across constructs, tested what pyridoxal 5'-phosphate supplementation does to that activity, and checked that recombinant ACCD secreted by our engineered B. subtilis still functions outside the cell. We also set up the standard curve and the arithmetic that converts an absorbance into an α-ketobutyrate concentration, a total product yield and an activity, so the comparison between strains rests on a stated calculation.

For future projects

Most of what is above was built because nothing off the shelf answered the question in front of us. Each piece is written up so it can be lifted out of ReLEAF and used on a different organism, a different stress or a different instrument.

  • Audit your own signal chain before you calibrate it. Our 434-hour culture had been recording the evidence of its own instrument bias for weeks. Any team logging a long time course can run the same check in an afternoon: plot the distribution of the reference channel and see whether readings land on whole sensor counts. If they do, the averaging step is not working and the curve carries steps that belong to the detector.
  • Replay algorithms instead of switching them. Logging the individual samples inside a burst, and not only the value they reduce to, lets every reduction algorithm be compared on identical input. It is usually one flag in firmware. Our eleven-algorithm replay script and the line-for-line estimator ports take any raw burst file.
  • Gate the reading, not the estimator. For any inline optical measurement in a circulating loop, the choice of robust estimator turned out to be worth under 0.004 OD, while a spread gate and a jump gate took the worst bubble error from 2.44 to 0.016 OD. Teams building flow-cell sensors can take the two gate rules directly.
  • Calibrate turbidity against a particle that does not grow. The withdraw-one, add-one silica ladder gives a concentration axis defined by pipetted volumes rather than by another instrument, and it separates instrument bias from biology. It runs on any photometer and costs about 100 mg of 1 µm microspheres.
  • Match cheap LEDs by measured output before trusting a light plate. Internal resistor variance makes nominally identical single-bulb LEDs deliver different doses. Our grouping method turns a bag of mismatched LEDs into columns that differ by a measured amount, and it works for any optogenetic system, not only CcaSR.
  • Report chlorophyll both ways. Per-gram normalisation inflates the number when stress has suppressed biomass: in one heat assay the stressed group appeared to hold three times more chlorophyll per gram while total chlorophyll in the extract had fallen by about 91%. Reporting total alongside per-gram, and choosing between them on whether biomass is comparable, keeps that artefact out of the conclusion.
  • Use the stress windows we found instead of rediscovering them. On agar, rescue is readable between 75 and 100 mM NaCl; 150 mM stunts and 250 mM bleaches past recovery. In hydroponic culture, 42 °C killed every group within a day and 37 °C left a window. TB medium carrying B. subtilis stressed Arabidopsis at every dilution down to 1/16, so protectant preparations have to be carried below that.

Three of these came out of a failure rather than a success. The bubble gate exists because an estimator we had argued about for weeks turned out not to matter; the chlorophyll recommendation exists because a per-gram number pointed the opposite way from the photographs; the TB dilution floor exists because a pretest found no concentration that plants tolerated. Each one is cheap to repeat and tells you something about your instrument or your assay before it tells you anything about your biology.

Conclusion

Could another team repeat these measurements?

Every chapter carries its own materials, its quantities per plate or per well, and its procedure in the order the steps are carried out, so each measurement can be rebuilt without reference to us. The runs that failed are reported beside the runs that worked, because a protocol that does not say how it stopped failing cannot be repeated: the 14 August pressure series does not reproduce from one run to the next, five of our eight pre-registered predictions about the photometer were overturned by our own data, and heat stress in hydroponic culture flattened the treated plants along with the controls. For the measurement that is wholly ours the raw material is published rather than described. The CSVs from both photometer builds, both bubble tests, the hand-written log sheet, the eleven-algorithm replay script, the line-for-line firmware ports and the pre-registration are in od600-signal-processing/ on the iGEM GitLab, and that protocol runs in four working days on any photometer that can be made to log the individual samples inside a burst. The limits are stated where they apply: the plant stress windows rest on one plate per group, and the reactor concentration and activity time courses on single wells.

Is the protocol well described?

Each block names its materials with the manufacturer and the catalogue or lot number where one exists, gives the amount needed per plate or per well, and then lists the steps. The arithmetic is described to the same depth as the bench work. The conversion from illuminance to optical density is given as equations, including the path-length factor of 5.0 that carries a 2 mm flow cuvette onto the 1 cm basis OD600 is quoted on, and the firmware that performs it is ported line for line into the analysis, so what is tested is what ships rather than a description of it. Image analysis is calibrated from a 150 mm ruler standing in every photograph, so the scale comes from an object in the frame instead of an assumed camera distance. Eleven problems that shaped the agar sets are tabulated with the set each appeared in and what we changed in response. No protocol on this page is offered as a separate file to download; each is written out in full where it is used.

Is it useful to other projects?

Each measurement block closes with what transfers out of ReLEAF. The central one is an in-line OD600 measurement that needs no instrument-specific transform fitted against a benchtop spectrophotometer: the raw output agrees with the benchtop reference to a median paired difference of 3.8%, and on the silica run its slope against that reference is 1.001, consistent with unit scale, so another team can rebuild the instrument and use the number it gives. Four further pieces are written for lifting out. The burst filter and the two acceptance gates took the worst bubble error from 2.44 to 0.016 OD and apply to any flow-cell sensor. The silica ladder calibrates a turbidity reading against a particle that does not grow, on a concentration axis set by pipetted volumes. The agar platform is a general screen for protectants and plant growth-promoting bacteria, with the usable stress window already found, 75 to 100 mM NaCl, so another team need not repeat the pretests. And chlorophyll is reported both per gram and in total, because per-gram normalisation inflates the number when stress has suppressed biomass, by a factor of three in one of our own heat assays while total chlorophyll had fallen by about 91%.

Were controls used to validate the process and calibrate the units?

Every optical reading here is referenced to a blank, and the blank is refused if either sensor is saturated, if too few readings survive the filter or if either channel reads zero, so the software returns no OD rather than an uncorrected one. The units are tied to physical quantities and not to another instrument: the path-length factor converts the flow cuvette to the conventional 1 cm basis, and the concentration axis of both calibration runs is set by weighed mass and pipetted volume. The instrument was then checked against two independent mass standards eight weeks apart, titanium dioxide on 23 August and 1 µm silica on 10 October, with a benchtop BioDrop reading in parallel at every step. Those comparisons are tested rather than asserted: against the reference the slope is 1.001, p = 0.98 against a null of unit slope, while the intercept is −0.123 OD, p = 0.005 against a null of zero, which locates the one remaining discrepancy in the stored blank and not in the optics. The flow meter was validated against outflow caught by hand over the same dials, R² = 0.995. In the assays the controls are a plate control on every set, equal liquid volumes in the untreated arms, an unstressed-plus-protectant arm to separate rescue from toxicity, blank-subtracted ELISA readings quoted as %B/B₀ against a standard curve with the coefficient of variation between replicate wells, and DNPH values taken net of the untransformed WB800 host background and read only over the five standard levels at and below 2.049 mM. Where a control defeated the measurement we report that as the result: the untransformed WB800 control returned 5.452 and 4.562 µg/mL in the ELISA, the same range as four of the six constructs beside it, so no difference in that table can be attributed to expression.
