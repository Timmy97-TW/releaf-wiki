Biomanufacturing

The vessel, pump, inline photometer and control boards on the lower shelf of a growth chamber, with the plants they make protectant for on the shelf above. ReLeaf is built at this scale, so its manufacturing questions are not those of a fermentation plant.

ReLeaf makes a protein where it is used. This page is the biomanufacturing view of the project: the organism and the parts, light as the production control, the three instruments we built, and the regulatory and market constraints that shaped all of them. Biomanufacturing Village

ReLeaf competes in the Biomanufacturing Village. This page answers the village question in one line and then evidences it: ReLeaf moves protein production out of a plant and onto a farm, by making secretion, induction, retention and monitoring the same four design choices.

Synthetic biology in biomanufacturing

ReLeaf detects environmental stress through electronic sensors and activates engineered B. subtilis using green light. The bacteria produce plant-protective molecules inside a hollow-fiber bioreactor, which releases the protectants while retaining the engineered cells.

Each of those is a manufacturing choice as much as a biological one. The chassis secretes, so there is no lysis step. The trigger is light, so there is no inducer to buy, meter or wash out. The vessel keeps the cells and passes the protein, so the product stream is cell free with no separate downstream unit. Taken together they remove three of the stages a conventional protein process needs, which is what lets the whole thing sit at 400 mL beside a field.

Product

A secreted protein protectant. ACC deaminase is the one every bench result on this wiki was produced with; LEA14 and BoPep4 are designed, not yet made in quantity. Protein Design

Chassis

Bacillus subtilis, non-pathogenic and a natural secretor. Work to 19 September is strain 168; the production strain is WB800N. Parts

The modular build is also the manufacturing argument. Splitting the design into sensor, cofactor, regulator and output modules means the output can be swapped without rebuilding the control layer, so the same machine makes a different protectant by changing one cassette. The assembly standard, the clone records and the three failures are on Parts and Results.

Optogenetics for bioproduction

Green light activates the CcaSR optogenetic system in engineered Bacillus subtilis, triggering the production of ACC deaminase, LEA14, or BoPep4.

For a process, light is an actuator with no mass. A chemical inducer has to be bought, stored, metered into a sterile vessel and then accounted for in the product stream; IPTG and its relatives are also a running cost that scales with every batch. Light adds no line into the loop, leaves no residue in the permeate, and can be re-dosed from software without anyone opening the vessel. That is what makes a forecast able to drive production at all: the command path ends at an LED, not at a pump full of inducer.

The first thing a process needs to know about an optogenetic switch is what it costs the culture. Across the six arms, growth rate spans 0.279 to 0.317 h−1, all within one standard error of each other, and the highest-green arm reaches the highest plateau. Green induction therefore carries no measurable growth penalty at these intensities, so inducing does not trade against biomass the way a metabolic burden would.

The induction curve is still missing

No experiment in this project shows PcpcG2 output changing with green light. Every ACC deaminase result on this wiki was produced with the constitutive cassette, the array's wavelengths are taken from the CcaS/CcaR literature [2], not measured on our parts, and the kinetics on Math Model are model output. One dose series with an expression readout, green against dark, closes it; it is the single experiment that unblocks the most of this page. Ledger on Results.

Instrument

Three instruments were built, all of them from parts a school lab can buy: the vessel that produces and retains, the array that characterises the light switch, and the twin that measures a batch nobody is standing next to. Each is documented as an object on Hardware and as a result on the pages linked below.

Bioreactor

The vessel is a perfusion loop, not a batch fermenter. Cells circulate on the lumen side of a hollow-fibre cartridge, the protein crosses to the shell side, and the shell-side liquid is what leaves the machine. One cartridge therefore does two unit operations at once, retention and recovery, and that is the whole reason the process has no downstream train.

What a bioprocess engineer told us to fix foaming, driving force and module size, from two meetings with Professor Chang

Excessive foaming was identified as a major concern, particularly because it can damage the hollow fiber membrane. Professor Chang pointed us toward two possible solutions: mutating the gene responsible for surfactant production or using chemical defoamers.

One of the most significant gaps Professor Chang identified was that our design lacked a pressure or driving force to push the protectant from the lumen side to the shell side. He explained that increasing flow rate would not solve this problem, since it only recirculates the fluid rather than generating outward pressure. He recommended adding a valve to build pressure while reducing tube diameter to concentrate the outward force.

Professor Chang advised that our current membrane may be oversized, increasing the risk of cells adhering to or clogging the fiber walls, and stressed that monitoring flow rate and transmembrane pressure is essential, as these indicate membrane fouling.

The pinch valve in Figure 3 answers the driving-force point and the pressure trace from the 102.4 h run answers the monitoring point. Foaming is still open: no defoamer was used and no surfactant-deficient strain was built. Both meetings are recorded on Integrated Human Practices.

Light plate apparatus

DiOPAL is the characterisation rig, not part of the control loop. It holds twenty-four cultures over twenty-four LEDs in a light-tight box, two wavelengths at three intensity tiers with four replicates each, so one run compares all six conditions under identical surroundings. A process that intends to dose light has to know what a dose is, and this is the fixture that defines it.

The result worth carrying into any other team's optogenetic rig is about the parts, not the biology. Forty single-bulb LEDs were measured individually at 5 V in a fixed geometry: the green units spanned 2.01 to 2.54 kLux, a 26% spread, and the red units 360 to 438 lux, a 22% spread, across parts sold as identical. Sorting by measured output gives groups of four that agree to within about 1%, but the gap between the sorted tiers is only 7% green and 10% red, so intensity has to come from the pulse-width duty cycle and matching only makes a column internally consistent. Anyone building light-controlled expression on cheap LEDs inherits that problem; the measurement tables are on Hardware: DiOPAL.

Not claimed for the array

That the tiers are calibrated intensity tiers, and that the twenty-four positions receive an even dose. Evenness was never measured. Closed by a lux map across the positions with the duty cycles recorded. On Results.

Digital twin

A reactor beside a field has nobody to sample it, so measurement is the part of this process that had to be invented, not bought. The twin reads seven channels live, moves six valves, tags every value by how it was obtained, and runs its interlocks on the microcontroller, not in the browser so no model can drive the loop past a limit.

The 434 h record is also the clearest statement of the limit: one run, one process event, one instrument event. It is a worked counterexample and a method, not a detection rate, and the page that reports it says so.

Regulation and biosafety

We know that farmers want to emphasize that their crops are organic, so we created our bioreactor to meet their needs. We adopted a hollow fiber membrane, pump, and jar of culture medium in our bioreactor so that when the B. subtilis produces protectant, only the protectant is pumped out and the B. subtilis stays inside.

In this process, containment is not an added safeguard around the unit operation; it is the unit operation. There is no filtration or kill step between culture and product, so the membrane both recovers the protein and holds the organism, and if the module fails the two fail together. That is why pressure and flow are logged continuously, why the microcontroller opens the pinch valve above 0.50 bar, and why a droplet sensor sits under the rig.

Regulation shaped the design in the same direction, because ReLeaf is three regulatory objects rather than one. The bioreactor is equipment. The engineered culture is a contained GMO, supplied as a vial. The cell-free permeate is the agricultural product, and in the EU its intended function, tolerance to abiotic stress, falls under the PFC 6(B) plant-biostimulant category, which is the clearest pathway we found and the reason we avoid pesticidal claims. Keeping the organism inside the machine is what allows that separation to hold. The three-market comparison is on Laws and Regulations.

Not claimed for containment

A retention figure. The record is one plating per run with no replicate and no stated detection limit, and the cell dimension the geometric argument uses is quoted two different ways in our own pages. A poster figure filed under a log reduction value of 7.5 has no measurement behind it and must not be quoted. Closed by plating the shell side at every sampling point of a run. On Results.

Market and scaling

Instead of shipping a protectant to the field, we grow it there. There is no cold chain, no shelf life to manage, and no shipment to pay for since the protectant is produced metres from the plants it protects on the day they need it.

Vaccine distribution is the nearest measured analogue for what a temperature-controlled biological supply chain costs. In one field logistics study, cold-chain infrastructure accounted for 43% of logistics cost at the national store and transport a further 34%, 77% together [3]. A protectant made on the farm never enters that chain. In exchange every farm needs its own machine, and the case for ReLeaf rests on that trade.

We scale out, not up. That came from the same bioprocess advice: heat transfer behaves differently in large vessels and B. subtilis favours higher temperatures for faster growth, which gets harder to control as the vessel grows, so a unit that stays at 400 mL avoids the problem that a bigger tank would create. What it inherits instead is unit count, and the design answers that with a swappable biological cartridge so a farm never opens a culture. That one is an intention, with no prototype built.

No yield and no unit cost are published here

The team estimate that one reactor could cover roughly a 30 m diameter of land has no measured titre behind it and is not a specification. The business plan has working figures for a bacterial kit and for the reactor, but they are drafts, so this page gives no unit cost and no saving until the bill of materials is settled. Both on Entrepreneurship.

Set against our own success criteria, production counts as demonstrated only when a detectable target protein sits above a validated detection limit and the activating condition produces significantly more of it than the controls, and delivery only when the protectant is quantifiable on the output side of the membrane. Neither is met. What ReLeaf demonstrates today is the manufacturing platform underneath them: a contained perfusion vessel that ran four days on its own hydraulics, an array that defines a light dose, and a twin that knows when its own measurement has stopped being true. The criteria table stage by stage is on Development, and what another team can lift from all of it is on Contribution.
