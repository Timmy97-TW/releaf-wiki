What we make, and where

ReLEAF detects environmental stress through electronic sensors and activates engineered B. subtilis using green light. The bacteria produce plant-protective molecules inside a hollow-fiber bioreactor, which releases the protectants while retaining the engineered cells. This creates a contained and responsive system designed to make plant protection more accessible to small-scale farmers.

Each part of that design is also a manufacturing choice. The chassis secretes, so there is no lysis step. The trigger is light, so there is no inducer to buy, meter or wash out. The vessel keeps the cells and passes the protein, so the product stream is cell free without a separate downstream step. And the unit sits at the edge of a field, which limits its size, its cost and how much attention it can ask of the farmer who owns it.

Product

A secreted protein protectant. ACC deaminase is the one every bench result on this wiki was produced with; LEA14 and BoPep4 are designed and not yet made in quantity.

Chassis

Bacillus subtilis, non-pathogenic and a natural secretor, so the product leaves the cell into the medium.

Vessel

A perfusion loop around a PES hollow-fibre module: 200 cm², 11 fibres, 0.2 µm pore rating, about 400 mL working volume.

Control

Green light at 520 nm through the CcaS/CcaR system, commanded by a stress forecast rather than by a person.

Making it where it is used

Biofertilizers, biostimulants, and bioprotectants offer promising alternatives to conventional agrochemicals, but they remain difficult to deliver affordably and reliably. These products are typically manufactured at centralized facilities, purified and formulated for stability, transported to farms, and stored under appropriate conditions. Each step adds time and cost, while farmers may still struggle to determine when the protectants should be applied.

Instead of shipping a protectant to the field, we grow it there. There is no cold chain, no shelf life to manage, and no shipment to pay for since the protectant is produced metres from the plants it protects on the day they need it.

To estimate what a temperature-controlled biological supply chain costs, we used vaccine distribution as the nearest analogue. In one field logistics study, cold-chain infrastructure accounted for 43 per cent of total logistics costs at the national vaccine store and transportation a further 34 per cent, 77 per cent together. A protectant made on the farm never enters that chain. In exchange, every farm needs its own machine, and the case for ReLeaf depends on that trade. The business side is on Entrepreneurship.

The other half is timing, which a central factory cannot fix. Production starts from a stress forecast rather than from an order placed weeks earlier, so the machine, not the farmer, decides when to apply the protectant. That question kept coming up on our farm visits. The stress index and the forecast that drive it are on Math Model and Digital Twin.

The reactor as a unit operation

The vessel is a perfusion loop rather than a batch fermenter. Cells circulate on the lumen side of a hollow-fibre cartridge, the protein crosses to the shell side, and the shell-side liquid is what leaves the machine. The same cartridge keeps the cells in and recovers the product, and most of the design on this page follows from that.

Those numbers come from the second of two build cycles. In the first, the culture in the loop did not grow while a control at the same temperature did. We wrote at the time:

The divergent OD600 values between the tube and reservoir indicated poor microbial viability and pointed to two key hardware limitations for the next DBTL cycle. Exhibiting these factors, we hypothesize that the lack of active mixing led to nutrient stratification and cell sedimentation within the reservoir, limiting growth.

Improving on the previous design, a 200 rpm magnetic stir was added to the medium reservoir to prevent sedimentation and ensure a homogeneous nutrient distribution. In contrast to our last attempt, this time the experiment ran successfully, helping us secure the full growth curve of our bacteria, with the bioreactor reaching the plateau phase (OD600 ~0.8 to 0.9) after 2 hours, and the flask reaching the plateau phase (OD600 ~0.6 to 0.8) after 4.5 hours.

On this reactor, mixing decides whether the culture grows at all. The build history, the fittings, the pump calibration and the pressure sweeps are on Hardware: Bioreactor; the run data and its limits are on Results.

What a bioprocess engineer told us to fix foaming, transmembrane pressure and module size, from two meetings with Professor Chang

Excessive foaming was identified as a major concern, particularly because it can damage the hollow fiber membrane. Professor Chang pointed us toward two possible solutions: mutating the gene responsible for surfactant production or using chemical defoamers. Mutating the gene stops the problem at the root, but is more challenging, whereas chemical defoamers are easy to implement but may be less effective.

One of the most significant gaps Professor Chang identified was that our design lacked a pressure or driving force to push the protectant from the lumen side (where it is produced) to the shell side (where it leaves the hollow fiber membrane). He explained that increasing flow rate would not solve this problem, since it only recirculates the fluid rather than generating outward pressure. He recommended adding a valve to build pressure while reducing tube diameter to concentrate the outward force.

Professor Chang advised that our current membrane may be oversized, increasing the risk of cells adhering to or clogging the fiber walls. He recommended decreasing the membrane size and emphasized that we should be prepared to clearly justify our choice of hollow fiber technology to judges. He also stressed that monitoring flow rate and transmembrane pressure is essential, as these indicate membrane fouling (caking).

The pinch valve in Figure 2 answers the second point, and the transmembrane pressure trace from the 102.4 h run answers the third. Foaming is still open: we used no defoamer and built no surfactant-deficient strain. Both meetings are recorded in full on Integrated Human Practices.

Light as the actuator

Green light activates the CcaSR optogenetic system in engineered Bacillus subtilis, triggering the production of ACC deaminase, LEA14, or BoPep4. Each protectant targets a different mechanism involved in heat, salinity, or biotic stress.

Using light means one less consumable and one less line into the vessel. A chemical inducer has to be bought, stored, metered into a sterile vessel and accounted for in the product stream. Light needs none of that, and software can change the dose from a forecast without anyone opening the loop. The cost is an optical problem. A dense culture shades itself, so what matters is the intensity at the vessel wall and how deep it reaches, not the output of the LED.

We have no induction curve yet. The array is built and its wavelengths are measured, but no experiment in this project has shown PcpcG2 output changing with green light. Every ACC deaminase result on this wiki was produced with the constitutive cassette. The kinetics simulated on Math Model, a half time of 3.3 to 5.7 h after a green step at 22 °C, are model output based on the literature [2]. The array is on Hardware: DiOPAL and the gap is recorded on Results.

Measurement without a technician

A machine at the edge of a field has nobody standing next to it, so it has to report its own state. The loop carries an inline dual-beam photometer with a 0.2 mm optical path and a reference channel taken before the sample, reading every few minutes into a dashboard that also logs flow, the three pressures and the transmembrane pressure derived from them.

Against a BioDrop benchtop spectrophotometer reading the same titanium dioxide suspension, the inline instrument gives ours = 1.018 × BioDrop − 0.031 with R² = 0.995, and above 30 mg of added TiO2 it is the steadier of the two. This shows the reading is linear in turbidity. It does not give a cell count, and the silica titration that would tie a reading to biomass is written up but not yet run. The instrument is on Hardware: Photometer, the trust layer that decides which readings to keep is on Digital Twin, and the calibration numbers with their limits are on Results.

Containment as a process requirement

The engineered bacteria remain inside a hollow-fiber membrane bioreactor. The membrane allows the produced protectants to enter the delivery pathway while retaining the bacterial cells within the system. This allows ReLEAF to provide localized protection when stress is predicted, without releasing engineered bacteria into the environment.

We know that farmers want to emphasize that their crops are organic, so we created our bioreactor to meet their needs. We adopted a hollow fiber membrane, pump, and jar of culture medium in our bioreactor so that when the B. subtilis produces protectant, only the protectant is pumped out and the B. subtilis stays inside.

Here, keeping the cells in is part of the process itself. There is no separate filtration or kill step between the culture and the product, so the membrane is the containment. If the module fails, production and containment fail together. That is why pressure and flow are logged continuously and a droplet sensor sits under the rig.

We have measured one plating per run: shell-side medium spread on LB and read after 24 h at 37 °C, with no colonies. We have not measured a log reduction value, and this wiki quotes none. The plates and their caveats are on Results; the full safety argument, including what happens if the membrane is breached, is on Safety.

Scale, and what one unit covers

We scale by building more of the same small unit, not a bigger vessel. That choice came from a bioprocess engineer who explained what scaling up would cost: heat transfer behaves differently in small and large vessels, and B. subtilis favours higher temperatures for faster growth, which gets harder to control as the vessel grows. A farm unit that stays at 400 mL avoids those problems. What it has to deal with instead is the number of units.

Predicted that around one bioreactor could produce a diameter of 30 m of land, even more if we could connect the bioreactor to irrigation systems.

That is a team estimate. No titre has been measured, so there is no yield number behind it, and it is not a specification.

No cost per unit is published here

The business plan has working figures for a bacterial kit and for the reactor, but they are still drafts. Until the bill of materials is settled, this page gives no unit cost and no saving. The draft figures and the model behind them are on Entrepreneurship.

We also want the biological part to be swapped like a cartridge, so the farm never opens a culture. That came from the same advice: the more farmer-friendly the system, the more attractive it is. So far it is a design intention with no built prototype.

What we are not claiming

This page leaves out four results because we have not measured them.

Not claimedWhat would close it
A titre. No protectant concentration has been measured in the permeate.An anti-His ELISA or an activity assay on permeate samples from a run.
Any enzyme activity. There is no ACC deaminase activity assay anywhere in our archive.A ninhydrin or alpha-ketobutyrate assay on the purified fusion.
Light induction. No experiment shows expression changing with green light.One dose series on the array with an expression readout, green against dark.
A retention figure. One plating per run, no replicate, no detection limit.Plating the shell side at every sampling point of a run, with a stated limit.

Our success criteria set the same bar more strictly: production counts as demonstrated only when a detectable target protein sits above the assay's validated detection limit and the activating condition produces significantly more of it than the controls, and delivery only when the protectant is quantifiable on the output side of the membrane. Neither is met yet. The full criteria table, stage by stage, is on Development.

What we have demonstrated is the vessel: a contained perfusion loop that grew cells for four days without intervention, held its crossflow and transmembrane pressure while doing so, logged its own state, and kept the engineered organism on one side of a membrane. That is the part of this first farm-scale build we ask judges to look at.
