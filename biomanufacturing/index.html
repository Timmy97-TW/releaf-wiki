Biomanufacturing

Abstract

ReLEAF grows a secreted protein protectant in a 1 L perfusion reactor beside the plants that need it, and works out what breaks when that unit is replicated.

Cells circulate on the lumen side of a 0.2 µm hollow-fibre cartridge and the protein leaves on the shell side, so one cartridge retains and recovers and the process has no downstream train. The loop has run 102.4 hours unattended at OD600 0.8 to 3.8, with transmembrane pressure within about ±0.1 bar. Green light is the command path, so production would be commanded from software without opening the vessel. Replicating the module holds wall shear and permeate flux fixed, so medium, pumping and power bind before the membrane does.

Synthetic biology for agricultural biomanufacturing

ReLEAF works on agricultural biologicals: plant-protective molecules that support crops under the heat, drought and salinity they cannot escape.

Such a molecule also has to be produced reliably, delivered when the stress arrives, and kept affordable and contained; those barriers usually decide whether it reaches a field. ReLEAF makes it at the point of use, on the day it is needed, in a machine small enough to stand beside the crop.

ReLEAF detects stress with electronic sensors and switches engineered B. subtilis on with green light. The cells make the protectant inside a hollow-fibre bioreactor, which releases it and retains them.

ACC deaminase is the protectant every bench result on this wiki was produced with. It interrupts the ethylene route shown in the lower panel: abiotic stress raises ACC, ACC oxidase converts ACC into ethylene, and ethylene reduces nodulation and growth. ACC deaminase competes for the same substrate and degrades ACC first, lowering ethylene while leaving the rest of the plant's stress signalling intact.

Optogenetics for bioproduction

We wanted production that could be commanded while the culture was running, so ReLEAF uses the green-light-responsive CcaS/CcaR system. When CcaS receives green light it activates CcaR, which switches on whichever protectant sits in the output position: ACC deaminase, LEA14 or BoPEP4.

Four of the collection's five modules build that command chain: a ho1:pcyA operon supplying the chromophore, without which the photoreceptor is apoprotein; CcaS‑m3 as the sensor; CcaR as the response regulator; and PcpcG2-veg driving a secretion tag fused to the cargo protectant. The fifth carries the same cargoes behind a constitutive promoter, so a negative protectant result has one fewer explanation to rule out.

Light was chosen for three process reasons. A chemical inducer such as IPTG has to be bought, stored and metered into a sterile vessel, which adds a recurring cost, leaves residue to account for in the product stream, and means opening the system to dose it. Light adds no line into the loop and no residue to the permeate, and can be commanded from software through an LED. It also addresses the growth–production tradeoff: continuous expression taxes the chassis for the whole run, while a light-responsive culture builds biomass first and switches to protectant when the forecast indicates stress.

Before using optogenetics for production we tested whether the light conditions themselves affected growth. Across six light arms on 19 September the growth rates span 0.279 to 0.317 h−1, all within one standard error of each other, and the strongest green arm reaches the highest final plateau. Green induction therefore carries no measurable growth penalty at these intensities.

Protein engineering, secretion and recovery

The product is one of three proteins: ACC deaminase, which lowers stress-induced ethylene; LEA14, a late embryogenesis abundant protein that stabilises other proteins under osmotic stress; and BoPEP4, a twenty-three residue elicitor peptide that signals drought through the PEPR1 receptor. Before any DNA was ordered, sequence analysis, structural modelling and targeted substitutions tested the candidates against two constraints at once: the receptor has to recognise the molecule, and B. subtilis has to translate it, secrete it through the Sec pathway and leave it whole in a protease-rich supernatant.

Two of those results changed a construct. BoPEP4's uncharged GILIGS opening makes no receptor contact and is conserved nowhere, and removing residues 1 to 6 leaves the docking score flat. The six-histidine tag was also on the wrong end: fused after Asn23 it spends the free carboxylate that salt-bridges Arg487, a bond that appears in no frame of the tagged trajectory and in every frame of the wild type.

The cell exports the protectant, it crosses the membrane, and it leaves on the shell side, separated from the producer by the same component that contains it. The fractionation result opposite is qualitative evidence of extracellular recovery; identity, concentration and activity in the permeate are not yet quantified, and that work is tracked on Engineering.

The bioreactor system

The culture circulates through the lumen side of a hollow-fibre cartridge rated at 0.2 µm, sweeping along the fibre walls instead of through them, which is cross-flow. Whole cells stay in the loop, smaller soluble proteins cross into the shell side under the pressure difference across the membrane, the transmembrane pressure, and that shell-side permeate is what leaves the machine. One cartridge retains and recovers at once, so the process has no downstream train. Unlike a batch fermenter the cells are not harvested after each cycle: the culture stays in circulation while product is removed continuously, so one culture supports repeated production.

The light plate apparatus defines what a light dose is and sets the duty cycle each arm receives.

The digital twin

With no operator on site, the monitoring and the decisions are carried in software. ReLEAF's digital twin reads optical density, pH, four pressures and cross-flow from the loop while it runs, and commands the pinch valve and the four Luer valves from the same interface. It is an AIoT system: sensors and valves are wired to a board that logs every reading, and models are fitted to those logs and to regional weather data.

Two models have been tested against recorded runs. An autoencoder trained on the healthy first 120 hours of the 434-hour run flagged the inline photometer 26.7 hours before its reading became unusable. A Kalman filter forecasting two hours ahead cut the forecast error by 41% against assuming no change, and a growth rate written down on 1 October, before the run it was tested on, came out inside its interval when the 14 to 16 October run was fitted. The forecast sets when the green light is switched on and for how long, and a person approves that. Green light and dissolved oxygen are tested separately and not yet joined to the live loop; the models so far run on recorded data after each run.

Regulation and biosafety

Farmers want to be able to say their crops are organic, and the bioreactor was built around that: the membrane keeps the engineered B. subtilis in the culture loop, and only the protectant passes into the product stream.

Containment and recovery are the same unit operation here. No filtration or kill step sits between the culture and the product, so the membrane both recovers the protein and retains the organism, and a module failure affects the two together. Monitoring the membrane is therefore part of monitoring containment: pressure and flow are logged continuously, the microcontroller opens the pinch valve above 0.70 bar, and a droplet sensor beneath the rig checks for external leakage.

That separation also keeps the regulatory picture tractable: ReLEAF would be regulated as three components, each on a different route.

The bioreactor is equipment

Pressure integrity, leak prevention, material compatibility, electrical safety, cleanability and routine maintenance of fluid-contact parts.

The culture is a contained GMM

The engineered B. subtilis sits in a replaceable cartridge and would be regulated separately as a genetically modified microorganism for contained use, with controls on handling, transport, storage and cartridge replacement. In the EU that is Directive 2009/41/EC.

The permeate is the product

Cell-free, so it is classified by composition, intended function and product claim rather than by how it was made. In the EU a non-microbial product whose claimed function is improving tolerance to abiotic stress can fall under Regulation 2019/1009, PFC 6(B) plant biostimulant.

In Taiwan and the United States classification is less straightforward, depending on whether the product counts as supporting nutrient use, altering plant physiology or acting as a plant regulator, so we evaluate the biostimulant, biofertiliser and biopesticide routes separately for each protectant and each market.

Keeping the organism inside the machine gives the production strain and the agricultural product two independent regulatory paths, and that split holds across all three markets: containment rules for the engineered microorganism, and a separate plant-biostimulant or fertiliser route for the recovered output, decided by the claimed function of improving tolerance to abiotic stress.

Field Deployment

Shipping a biological to a farm means a cold chain: refrigerated storage, refrigerated transport and a shelf life to manage. A protectant made on the farm never enters that chain, but every farm needs its own machine, and the case for ReLEAF rests on that trade.

Feasibility depends on whether the protectant a unit produces justifies the cost of running it. The prototype build is the only cost figure we have, and it is a hand-built one-unit cost, not a unit cost at volume: NT$52,578 for the bacterial kit and NT$30,450 for the bioreactor, NT$83,028 together, roughly US$2,800. A techno-economic analysis would set production and operating cost against it to give a cost per unit of protectant, a break-even yield and a comparison with what a farmer buys today.

Scaling

Capacity can be increased along one of two routes. Scale-up enlarges a single vessel, so every quantity that depends on a length scale has to be re-established at the new size: mass transfer (how fast a dissolved substance crosses from one phase into another), oxygen supply, heat removal, mixing time and membrane area per unit volume. Scale-out holds the unit fixed and replicates it, so every module runs at conditions already characterised. ReLEAF scales out (AGC Biologics, n.d.).

We chose that route because tangential-flow filtration, the cross-flow arrangement described above, has a formal method for it, which is also why a hollow-fibre module was selected over a stirred tank with filtration on the side. Linear scale-up holds every size-independent flow quantity constant, the wall shear rate above all, and increases only the channel count, with transmembrane pressure controlled so that the concentration at the membrane wall does not change; van Reis and colleagues (1997) report a 400-fold increase in scale carried this way without an intermediate pilot stage. Hollow fibre suits the method because a scale-down module has the same fibre length as an industrial one, so our module serves directly as the unit cell to be replicated.

We worked each requirement of one module through to eight, asking in each case whether the quantity binds. The membrane is the last to bind, which follows from the method: replication holds wall shear, permeate flux and the fouling margin fixed by construction. What binds is the support systems around it, oxygen transfer and energy per unit, medium supply and cross-flow pumping in aggregate, and none of the four is addressed by the reactor arithmetic.

One change would reduce three of those four together. Running the recirculation pump on a duty cycle instead of continuously cuts the dominant electrical load, the cumulative shear exposure and the medium draw at once, and the reactor has margin for it: at the design flux it already delivers more medium per day than a week in the field could carry, and the biology responds over hours. The cost would be settling in the fibres during the off phase, which we have not measured, and that is the experiment we would run first on the hardware side.

Control bandwidth does not bind, but it does set what the device can be used for. The CcaSR activation half-time of 105.1 ± 1.5 min (Castillo-Hair et al., 2019) exceeds every other lag in the loop by an order of magnitude, so the loop is dead-time dominated at every flux we can run, and ReLEAF is specified for drought and salinisation developing over hours to days.

Wall shear and the operating envelope

Wall shear sweeps the membrane surface, sets the critical flux, and is the quantity held constant to make the scale-up linear. In a round lumen, while the flow stays laminar, meaning it moves in smooth layers rather than mixing turbulently, the wall shear rate is set by the flow per fibre alone:

Conventional hollow-fibre microfiltration of bacterial cultures runs at 2000 to 8000 s−1, and Maiorella and colleagues (1991) put the balance between sweeping the membrane and damaging the cells nearer 1500 to 3000 s−1. The pump's vendor floor of 230 mL/min (ZP4000-N79H peristaltic pump, n.d.) already produces 4881 s−1, in the upper half of the conventional band, and the band closes at 377 mL/min, 6% of a dial that reads to 2600 mL/min, so the lowest available setting is the only one inside it.

B. subtilis is walled, so the 3 to 5 Pa wall-stress ceiling quoted for mammalian perfusion does not apply, and single-pass exposure carries a wide margin at every setting in the table. Cumulative exposure we have not established: at 230 mL/min a 100 mL loop passes the fibres about 138 times an hour. Van Reis and colleagues (1997) attribute aggregation in such loops to pump passages and air entrainment rather than shear.

Critical flux and the fouling margin

Below a critical permeate flux, particle deposition is negligible and transmembrane pressure does not drift; above it, cells accumulate as a cake layer and the membrane fouls (Field et al., 1995). For a particulate suspension in laminar cross-flow, the shear-induced diffusion model estimates that threshold (Belfort et al., 1994; Zydney & Colton, 1986):

\[ J_{crit} = 0.072 \left( \frac{a^{4}}{L} \right)^{1/3} \dot\gamma_w \ln\!\left( \frac{\phi_w}{\phi_b} \right) \]

We take a = 0.71 µm as the equivalent-sphere radius of a 0.8 × 3 µm rod, pin the wall fraction at random close packing (0.60), the densest a jumble of equal spheres reaches without settling into a lattice, and compute the bulk fraction from cell density (OD600 1.454 at 109 cells per mL per OD unit). At the 263 mL/min operating point, with eleven fibres and a wall shear of 4064 s−1, the threshold is 46 LMH against a design flux of 2 LMH, so J/Jcrit is 0.04.

The membrane is therefore oversized for the duty by more than an order of magnitude, a consequence of selecting the module for its fibre geometry. Flux could be raised about 23-fold before fouling becomes limiting, still short of the roughly 60 LMH that cell-specific perfusion would require for a litre of culture (below), so on this module the membrane and the medium supply become limiting together. Two qualifications apply. Local flux is not uniform, so low-level fouling can occur below the nominal threshold and no operating point should be quoted as sitting exactly at Jcrit (van Reis & Zydney, 2007); and the model treats the feed as cells in water, whereas secreted protein, media components and cell debris each add resistance it does not account for.

Cell-specific perfusion rate

The governing quantity is the cell-specific perfusion rate, the volume of medium delivered per cell per day. The chemostat washout inequality D < µmax, between the dilution rate and the maximum growth rate, does not apply, because a 0.2 µm membrane retains rods of 1 to 2 µm: permeate leaves and biomass does not, so the dilution rate can exceed the growth rate without washing the culture out.

Modern perfusion practice sits between 0.02 and 0.5 nL per cell per day; at 2 LMH, with OD600 1.454 in 1 L and 109 cells per mL per OD unit, ours is 0.0007, about thirty times under the band, so the cells are fed very lean. The bottom of the band, 0.02, needs about 60 LMH, above the 46 LMH fouling threshold, and 0.05 needs about 150 LMH. The medium arithmetic agrees: at 2 LMH the module draws 0.67 mL/min, 0.96 L a day and about ten turnovers of the 100 mL loop; at 60 LMH it is about 29 L a day. For a bench run over a shift that is unremarkable; for a device left unattended in a field for a week, the medium needed to feed the cells conventionally is the binding constraint, and more membrane alone does not remove it.

Oxygen, energy and dead time

Oxygen supply limits cell density before anything else does. At the carrying capacity of OD600 1.454, taking 0.4 g dry cell weight per litre per OD unit and a conservative uptake of 3 mmol g−1 h−1, the culture consumes 1.74 mmol L−1 h−1 and requires a kLa of 12.5 h−1, modest for a sparged fermenter and demanding for a closed loop with no gas port. The dissolved inventory shows the constraint more clearly: a 100 mL loop held at 30% of saturation contains 0.006 mmol against a consumption of 0.174 mmol per hour, so an interruption exhausts it in about two minutes, and in just under seven from full saturation. Oxygen is a throughput quantity here, and cell density has to be capped at what the loop can transfer.

Energy decides whether a unit can be deployed in a field at all. We sized the supply against Taiwan's worst-case winter peak-sun-hours of about three per day, not the annual average. At a 75% harvest efficiency from panel through battery to load, a 45 W panel returns 101 Wh per day against a draw of 72 to 192 Wh; at a mid-point draw of 5 W the balance supplies 0.84 of the requirement, and a 53 W panel would close it. That spread comes from one unmeasured quantity, the continuous draw of the recirculation pump, which a clamp-meter reading would resolve.

Loop dead time θ sums sensor sampling, controller cycle, LED ramp, the CcaSR activation half-time, secretion lag and pump transit, and the half-time of 105.1 ± 1.5 min (Castillo-Hair et al., 2019) dominates it. The system time constant τ is the shell exchange time, 150 minutes at 2 LMH and 15 minutes at 20 LMH, so θ/τ falls between 0.7 and 7.0: at the design flux the dead time is comparable to the time constant, and above it it dominates. Such a loop remains controllable but responds slowly, and raising the flux shortens τ and increases the ratio, so the delivery rate cannot be set on plant-response grounds alone.

The transmembrane pressure the method is supposed to hold constant is resolved only to ±10 kPa against an axial drop, the pressure lost along the length of a fibre, of 11.6 kPa at the pump floor (BP8G-AGA pressure transmitter, n.d.), so fouling has to be read from a trend, not a single reading. A 0 to 100 kPa sensor resolves ±1 kPa and is the cheapest upgrade available to this reactor (specification on Measurement).

Where the geometry comes from

Our own records give the module as both 150 cm² with eight fibres and 200 cm² with eleven, and the one place the arithmetic has been checked against the bench agrees with eleven. On 16 August 2026, in water at 22 °C, we ran eight pump settings, once with the module fitted and once with a jumper in its place, and fitted ΔP = aQ + bQ² through the origin to both, the module fit at R² 0.9954. At 263 mL/min the net module drop is 0.096 bar, 1.03 times the Hagen–Poiseuille prediction, the laminar pressure drop expected in open tubes, for eleven open fibres; a lumen weighed on 13 August gave 7.0 to 7.2 mL of holdup against a computed 3.74 mL. The shear table above uses eight fibres, so its values are the conservative, higher ones, while the membrane area, permeate and medium figures use the 200 cm² and eleven fibres that the bench check and the rig software's constants agree on. Bench calibration puts pump setting 10 at about 100 mL/min against the vendor floor of 230, and three calibrations across three days span 100 to 140, widening the usable shear window. The hydraulic fit is R1 on Digital Twin.
