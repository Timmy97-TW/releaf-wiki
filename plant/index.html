Agar

Why agar

Agar trials were conducted to fine-tune protectant treatments and NaCl stress within a controlled laboratory environment, ensuring uniform nutrient availability and precise stress exposure. This setup enabled the evaluation and screening of protectant efficacy, helping identify top performing protectant candidates while establishing clear physiological baselines prior to testing in complex soil systems. Furthermore, agar media provide essential nutrients and an accelerated timeframe for observing early physiological changes in Arabidopsis seedlings, facilitating rapid and high-throughput testing across subsequent experimental iterations.

Square plates stand upright so roots can be traced; round plates lie flat and give enough leaf for a chlorophyll extraction.

Set design

In total, we conducted 8 experiments under NaCl-induced salt stress. Among these, six sets evaluated the non-reducing disaccharide trehalose, which has been shown to have a protective effect under salinity stress (PMID: 25400644). The remaining two sets used commercially available CH Biotech’s peptide as our positive control. One additional set used purified ACC deaminase against salinity stress. Our pre-test conditions of experimental set 1-2 were surrounded against testing out protocols and the initial response of plants to NaCl, which is why we only used 50 mM, 100 mM and 150 mM of NaCl and 0.5 mM and 1 mM of trehalose. However, we soon realized that the 150 mM of NaCl was too strong for the plants and they dried out easily, and while our data predicted the effect of NaCl on the plants, there were no visual differences between adding protectant and not adding any.

Continuing with the belief that higher NaCl stress should be fought with higher trehalose content, we conducted experimental set 6, where 0 mM and 5 mM of trehalose was used against 0 mM, 75 mM, 100 mM and 150 mM of NaCl.

For our seventh experimental trial, we made a few key changes in our plant stress and protection protocol. Firstly, we wanted to verify the purpose and need for an AIoT prediction model and stress sensors in the farming environment, so one of our chosen independent variables was the treatment time. The pre-treatment group would have CH biopeptide added 1 day before stress strikes, the co-treatment group would have the protectant added on the same day, then the after-treatment group would be treated one day after the NaCl is added. Next, the CH biopeptide had a recommended dilution level, which was 1/500, for optimal usage, so we did not change the concentration as it would not be used in real fields with a changed concentration level either. Lastly, this group of plants went through 4 days of stratification and 4 days at 22 degrees celsius before the pre-treatment group started directly in their plates. Unlike previous experimental sets, we did not want the salt stress and protectant applied on the first day, and as Arabidopsis plants are extremely fragile and vulnerable to transfer, we decided to add the NaCl and CH biopeptide directly into the agar plate (see below for protectant application).

Moving on to the first ACCD pre-test, we purified our first round of ACCD and contained it in multiple 15 mL centrifuge tubes for treatment. We had changed the method of treatment to spraying, one up, one left, and one right, yet we followed the same pre-treatment, co-treatment, and after-treatment protocols (see protectant application for more details).

Application

Phase 1 · Trehalose

In phase 1 of trehalose testing (including 3 sets of pre-tests and 4 sets of official testings) fine tuned concentrations of trehalose protectants were added directly into the mixture with agar before solidifying, meaning each experimental group contains the corresponding trehalose and salinity concentrations. We selected vertical planting of agar plates to allow clearer and better visions for root length observation and analysis.

On Day 0, sterilized Arabidopsis seeds were stratified on ½ MS neutral vertical agar plates for 4 days at 4 °C, and moved to the incubator for 4 days germination. On day 8, 4 days old Arabidopsis seedlings were transferred to each of the newly made vertical salinity agar plates containing a corresponding mixture of trehalose with NaCl. During the transferring process, we screen for seedlings with similar root lengths and similar growth levels, and place 12 seedlings per salinity plate with each of their tips of the roots onto a straight baseline drawn by a marker. Thus we could immediately start observing the seedling’s root length difference and physiological changes after they are exposed to different concentrations of NaCl and trehalose, evaluating the rescue effectiveness and growth for each combination over a course of 14 days.

Phase 2 · CH Bio peptide

Using commercial CH bio peptide, the primary objective is to evaluate the efficacy of commercial protectants’ effectiveness and identify its best application timing in response to the exact point where salinity stress hits. Therefore, CH bio peptide application is categorized into 3 treatment timing groups: pre-treatment, co-treatment, and after-treatment. Different treatment timings are essential connecting to our project goal, “to supply protectants at the optimal timing before stress harms the plants”. These 3 treatment timing groups and the control group (no protectant) were each subjected to three NaCl concentrations (0 mM, 75 mM, and 100 mM), giving a total of 12 experimental conditions.

Looking at the entire experiment timeline, on day 0, Arabidopsis seeds were stratified on ½ MS neutral agar for 4 days at 4 °C, then transferred to a growth chamber for 4 days germination. Protectant applications were initiated subsequently starting from Day 8, and administered at 24-hour intervals between each treatment timing. Treatment timings can be considered as another set of days (Day −1: pre-treatment, Day 0: NaCl and co-treatment, Day 1: after-treatment). Thus, the day of salt stress application is Day 0, whereas NaCl (75 mM and 100 mM NaCl) was applied to all corresponding treatment groups, coinciding with the application for the co-treatment group.

On the technical side, we applied the protectant directly onto the surface of the agar by adding 3 mL then removing 2 mL of excess to achieve a 1 mL of final protectants added (the surface tension of the agar causes uneven distribution of protectant, therefore we added a larger volume of protectant to completely submerge the surface first, then suck out the excess protectants lastly). Furthermore, to prevent varying factors of growth between groups with and without protectants added (as liquids can affect plants’ absorption and growth), we added the equal volume of sterile water (1 mL) to every other group to simulate equal liquid effects on plants.

Phase 3 · Our own proteins

Secreted by our engineered Bacillus subtilis, ACC deaminase, LEA and BoPep4 were used as the protectant against salinity stress in Phase 3. Before applying, the protectant was purified through a 0.22 µm filter. The main difference between this phase’s protectant application is that we utilized a spraying method. We used a sterilized spraying nozzle to dispense 3 sprays of ACCD onto the surface of plants, administered by spraying in 3 equal areas of individual sections (one top left, one top right, and one bottom) through a distance of 10 cm between the nozzle and agar plate.

The overall experimental flow still follows the timeline from phase 2 of CH bio peptide testing, while we extended the germination time from 4 days to 7 days (this suggestion was provided by Prof. Cheng to use stronger plants for stress testing).

Heat stress

However, considering that vertical plates cannot hold protectant on the surface like horizontal plates can, we used a spray nozzle that dispensed 1.67 mL for every 3 sprays which we administered by spraying one up, one left, and one right. If there were more, the protectant would be carried downwards by the weight, and we decided to not spray one at the bottom since it would flow downwards regardless.

From set 8, plants move onto plates that already hold the treatment, so the dose belongs to the plate and the pre-treatment carry-over is gone.

Hydroponics

Why hydroponics

Abiotic stress, such as salinity stress and heat stress, negatively affects crop productivity. To test the effectiveness of our self-produced protectants under both types of stress, we chose to use a hydroponic system that allows for precise control of environmental factors. Compared with soil-based systems, hydroponics reduces the number of uncontrolled variables, such as differences in soil composition, microbial communities, and water distribution. Therefore, we can establish and maintain consistent experimental conditions by precisely controlling environmental parameters, including pH, electrical conductivity, temperature, and dissolved oxygen. In addition, nutrients and stress treatments can be delivered more uniformly to all plants through the nutrient solution, improving experimental consistency. Because treatments are added directly to the root environment, the hydroponic cultivation system allows us to rapidly observe plant responses to different stress conditions and evaluate the protective effects of our target protectants.

We are inspired by Matthew Gillham’s hydroponics culture system [7]. First, Arabidopsis seeds were sown on 0.6% agar containing black microcentrifuge-tube lids, each with a 3 mm diameter hole punched using a leather punch. Then, the lids containing seeds were placed on a microcentrifuge tube rack with the agar side facing down. Each well was filled with 2.1 mL of ½ MS medium, allowing the agar to remain in contact with the medium throughout stratification and germination. The seeds were stratified at 4 °C in the dark for at least 48 hours, and then transferred to a growth chamber maintained at 22 °C under a 16-hour light and 8-hour dark photoperiod. After germination, 4-day-old seedlings were directly transferred onto the floating board or holder and left to grow in the hydroponic box. The tank was kept dark to reduce algal growth and continuously aerated with air stones to maintain sufficient dissolved oxygen. The nutrient solution was regularly replaced every 2 to 3 days, and the plants were then used for experimental treatments and physiological measurements.

Three rules came out of the prototypes: seedlings go in from agar, evaporated medium is never topped up, and the bottom of the box stays dark.

Soil

Why soil

Soil trials were done to simulate real field conditions and allow protectants to be validated under complex conditions after testing in controlled environments such as agar and hydroponics. Soil introduces physical matrix interactions, including water retention and pore size distribution, which affects how the NaCl and protectant diffuses and reaches the plant roots. Additionally, soil contains native microbial flora, allowing us to validate its real world viability. Hydroponics and agar assays are for baseline fine tuning, protocol simulations, and controlled proof of concept, while demonstrating efficacy in real soil reflects smallholder farming realities.

Planting

Getting a consistent stand of plants in a pot took three methods.

  • We first tested transferring 7 day old Arabidopsis from vertical agar plates to soil pots. During the transfer process the plant roots are easily damaged and stressed, which led to inconsistency in the growth of the plants.
  • We then tested using seeds directly, stratified in sterile water for 3 days and sown individually in the soil pots, 4 per pot. The n value was too small and plant growth was not consistent enough to give us enough sets to collect measurable data.
  • Lastly we tested stratifying seeds in 1 mL of sterile water for 3 days and dispersing the seeds using a micropipette, then picking out smaller plants in later growth stages to maintain similar sizes of plants in each experimental group. This was suggested by Prof. Cheng.

The age of the plant took two stages to settle as well. We first used 1 month old plants, which were already close to the end of their life cycle, then moved to 2 weeks old according to Prof. Cheng’s outreach suggestions.

Salt schedule

Dr. Verslues’s ramp added 25 mM a day toward 100 mM [8]. Prof. Cheng judged it too mild for month-old plants, and watering between doses washed the salt back out.

The schedule now: equal volumes of 100, 200, then 300 mM with no water between, then 300 mM again until the effect shows [9]. A pot takes 30–50 mL before a little runs through.

Fix 2 · the salt in the written protocol

Our own agar salinity protocol, Salinity stress on agar.pdf, section C, does not make the concentrations it labels. Step 5 splits a 600 mL batch into three flasks of 200 mL and labels them 0, 150 and 250 mM. Step 6 adds 2.192 g of NaCl to the 150 mM flask and step 7 adds 3.652 g to the 250 mM one. At 58.44 g mol−1, 2.192 g in 200 mL is 187.5 mM and 3.652 g in 200 mL is 312.5 mM. Both are exactly 1.25 times the label, which is what those masses give if they were weighed for 250 mL and the volume was later changed to 200.

Two readings are open and the file settles neither. Either the masses are right and step 5 should read 250 mL, in which case the labels are correct and nothing changes; or the volume is right and every plate made to this sheet carried a quarter more salt than its label.

How far it reaches is also open. This protocol describes the 0, 1, 5 and 10 mM trehalose against 0, 150 and 250 mM NaCl design, which is experiment set 5 only. The salt ladder in Figure 2 comes from set 6, at 0, 75, 100 and 150 mM, and no recipe for set 6 is written down anywhere in the archive: the agar formula column of the master run sheet is empty for all eight sets. Nothing in Figure 2 is restated here, and no concentration anywhere on this page has been rewritten, because the labels stay as the team recorded them until somebody settles which end of the protocol is wrong.

If it does turn out to cover set 5, it partly explains two things this page already reports: that 150 mM was harsher than expected and dried the seedlings out, and that 250 mM killed almost everything by day three. Whoever weighed that salt should check the bench notes for the flask volume.

Set 3 · two trials, 27 May to 11 June · 10 and 13 days · round plates

Four doses, two trials

Inconclusive

Why this set exists. 1 mM was toxic and 0.5 mM did nothing, so the working dose had to be somewhere between. Two trials were run at the same time to double the sample before committing to a concentration.

Conducting preliminary testing using solely trehalose protectant helped us quantify growth capacity when protectant is added. Each plate still had all 8 to 9 of their seedlings germinated, so we decided to fine tune the optimal trehalose concentration for rescue effectiveness evaluation. With experimental set 3, we tested out 4 different trehalose dosages, including 0.125 mM, 0.25 mM, 0.5 mM all under 100 mM NaCl. Experimental groups 1 and 2 revealed that 1 mM was too toxic and 0.5 mM was insufficient against strong salt levels, so we considered manipulating the concentration in between the two to reach a balance. As a pre-test, we were able to identify the early effects of salinity stress on the Arabidopsis and key milestones that can be observed, such as stunted root growth or minimized leaf area. Since our protocol was not yet standardized at the time, data collected from early experimental sets only include image documentation (done in a light box with both a black and white background and surrounding lights turned off).

The two trials point opposite ways. In trial 2, salt alone kept 8 of 9 seedlings growing and every trehalose plate kept fewer.

Set 4 · 18 to 30 June · 12 days · square plates, vertical

Vertical plates, three readouts

Readouts disagree

Why this set exists. Set 3 showed that counting germinated seedlings cannot see a salt effect that lands on the root. This set keeps the conditions of set 3 and rebuilds the measurement.

Beginning experimental set #4, this set continues the same testing conditions as set #3 (100 mM NaCl against 0.25, 0.5, 1.0 mM trehalose) but we shifted the culturing platform to vertical planting, and set the overall growth timeline to 2 days stratification at 4 °C, 4 days growth, then transfer the 4 days old seedlings onto newly made salinity plates. The goal of using vertical planting was to primarily better observe gravitational root length growth for ImageJ analysis. Additionally, for data collection, we observed root length, fresh weight, and chlorophyll content to quantify growth parameters.

Overall, all exogenous trehalose concentrations have demonstrated obvious rescue effectiveness under the subjected 100 mM NaCl stress in relation to the control 100 mM NaCl. As shown from the end results pictures, on the second row, all groups treated with trehalose protectant have longer root length and more lateral root growth compared to the control 100 mM NaCl. While among the different treatment groups, the leaf colors vary from slight purple to bright green, indicating the imposed ionic stress and toxicity or trehalose rescue effectiveness correspondingly.

Referring to chlorophyll data on Fig.4, the results aligned with a previous prediction that had not been validated before: while too much NaCl was damaging to plant growth, too much trehalose could be as well. As shown, the lowest chlorophyll content occurred in the 1.0 mM trehalose control and the 100 mM NaCl control groups. In fact, the pure 1.0 mM trehalose treatment even yielded lower chlorophyll levels than the 100 mM NaCl salinity group. Furthermore, among the plants subjected to 100 mM NaCl salt stress, the 0.5 mM trehalose treatment demonstrated higher chlorophyll content rather than a higher 1.0 mM dose. This highlights the necessity for fine-tuning protectant concentration, whereas 0.25 mM dose is slightly inadequate for maximizing rescue effectiveness.

Physical observations based on the second row of Fig.3, reveals that leaves in the 0.25 mM and 1.0 mM trehalose groups partially turned purple, indicating anthocyanin accumulation driven by osmotic stress, meanwhile also validating the physiological effects of a severe 100 mM NaCl stress level. In contrast, leaves from the 0.5 mM trehalose group predominantly maintained a bright or dark green appearance with minimal purple leaves discoloration. The physiological differences demonstrate the necessity of fine-tuning concentration and relatively outstanding rescue effectiveness of 0.5 mM trehalose dose.

To strengthen the protectant necessity, the groups undergoing 100 mM NaCl stress and trehalose treatments at the same time sustained the greatest amount of chlorophyll, even surpassing the groups with trehalose added.

Root length favours trehalose; fresh weight does not, and the chlorophyll chart is divided by fresh weight (fix 6).

Finding the top of the range

Two trials

Why this set exists. Two questions at once: how much salt is too much, and whether a much higher trehalose dose does what the small ones could not. The dose pair came off a published study [2], and the set was also meant to give the dry lab a wider spread of points to model.

While conducting experimental set 5, we realized around 2 to 3 days in that some of the plants had turned white due to chlorosis, which accelerated the breakdown of chlorophyll, leading to a relatively lower chlorophyll content; more severe chlorosis causes total depletion of the pigment in plant leaves. Even in cases where 5 mM of trehalose and 10 mM of trehalose were used, all of the plates had at least one seedling that had shriveled up and turned white. We decided to redo the experiment in trial 2 with slight changes to our contained aseptic technique. The control groups without trehalose could reach up to 82.476 mm in root length, yet the 150 mM groups were limited at around 4 to 5 mm and the 250 mM groups stunted within 1 mm.

There was minimal contamination with trial 2 and the results presented were similar. Among the groups treated with high salinity stress, 250 mM NaCl is revealed to be way too toxic and damaging to the plant as most plant leaves turned white, indicating severe chlorosis and that a high trehalose dose yet couldn’t rescue the plants. In terms of root length, the plants under 250 mM NaCl stress obtained little to none root length growth within a 1 mm range, as shown in the primary root length plot below.

In addition, the chlorophyll content for all of the control agar plates, regardless of trehalose concentration, had around the same chlorophyll content, signalling a maximum efficiency that the trehalose can reach. Set 6 · 29 July to 3 August · 6 days · square plates, vertical

The salt ladder

Closed

Why this set exists. Set 5 established that 250 mM kills before a protectant can act. This set drops it, narrows the intervals under 150 mM, holds trehalose at one dose, and asks the simplest version of the question: across four salt levels, what does 5 mM trehalose change.

The calibration set. Mean root length falls 74.95, 34.77, 11.00, 1.36 mm at 0, 75, 100 and 150 mM, nine seedlings a group.

The specific 5 mM trehalose concentration was isolated due to experimental results from our previous set, set 5, demonstrating that 10 mM was too toxic and 5 mM was performing the best to benefit plant growth. Furthermore, the previous set demonstrated that extreme salt stress such as 250 mM NaCl was too high for the plants to sustain, so we eliminated the highest NaCl concentration and chose smaller intervals under 150 mM for this set. However, we quickly realized that our protectant concentration of 5 mM was visibly not sufficient for 150 mM of NaCl because the leaves of both 100 mM NaCl trials turned purple or brown after 3 days of treatment. Overall we selected 3 testing NaCl concentrations with a relatively smaller interval (75, 100 and 150 mM NaCl).

Contrary to our initial hypothesis, the application of 5 mM trehalose did not confer a protective effect under mild salt stress (75 mM NaCl), instead resulting in reduced chlorophyll content and 5 cm shorter average root lengths compared to the untreated control, as we believed the 5 mM trehalose dose should have worked more effectively under a lower stress level than a high stress level. Conversely, trehalose supplementation proved effective under higher stress conditions (100 mM NaCl). This outcome suggests that 75 mM NaCl represents a mild or sub-threshold stress level where endogenous regulatory mechanisms inside the plants are still sufficient to maintain cellular homeostasis without exogenous protectants. Under these conditions, the addition of trehalose may have slightly disrupted osmotic balance. In contrast, 100 mM NaCl likely exceeds the threshold of intrinsic plant tolerance, which shifts the system into a moderate-to-severe stress state where the plants’ internal regulatory mechanisms are running out of their sustainable range, and exogenous trehalose becomes critical for mitigating the cellular damage, thereby sustaining significantly better growth compared to its untreated control. It is also observed that the root length of the 100 mM control was clearly much lower than that of 75 mM, barely growing even after 6 to 7 days of protection and NaCl treatments. The shift of physiological effects of plants under the 75 mM to 100 mM NaCl condition reveals the relatively strong growth inhibition 100 mM NaCl poses on plants, whereas 75 mM did not stunt the plant growth as much as 100 mM NaCl did.

Regarding the control groups, baseline chlorophyll levels remain comparable between the control and the 5 mM trehalose group, while there is a minor decrease in root length growth of the 5 mM trehalose group, indicating that the protectant alone has a negligible effect on unstressed plants.

Importantly, the application of 5 mM trehalose significantly enhanced plant salt tolerance under 100 mM NaCl stress. This is evidenced by an increase in chlorophyll content and a substantial increase in root length, relative to the untreated control, in the two figures below.

Once the salinity level reaches up to 150 mM NaCl, the high stress concentration severely damaged the plants, as evidenced by physically observed chlorosis and significantly stunted growth shown by data analysis, where 5 mM trehalose did not play much of a role in rescuing the plants, only demonstrating a minor increase in root length within 1 cm, nonetheless showing a decrease in chlorophyll content, indicating the insufficient amount and dose.

Dry lab collaborations

The dry lab work focuses on two questions: how much protectant is needed, and how early does it need to be applied. We have not been able to determine either value yet, but our plant experiments give us a starting point.

Effective concentration prediction

Our original plan was to build a dose-response curve using protectant concentration and a rescue index, which compares the treated plant's response with an unstressed control under the same salt concentration. We could then estimate the concentration at which additional protectant produces little further improvement and use it as the bioreactor's production target.

Trehalose was originally our test case because we had six experimental sets. However, root length did not show a consistent relationship with trehalose concentration. The effective range appeared to fall between a concentration with little effect and one that was toxic, and two trials using the same design in set 3 gave opposite results. We therefore could not fit a reliable curve, and trehalose was removed from our circuit design in August.

In set 7, we also tested treatment timing by applying the protectant before, during, or after salt stress. However, the trials gave inconsistent rankings between the three treatments, so there is not yet enough data to fit a timing model.

Data quality also limits the modelling. Each agar group was represented by only one plate, and three experimental sets had problems with the absorbance wavelength used for chlorophyll measurements. Fitting a curve with this data would give a quantitative-looking result without enough evidence to support it.

For now, our most useful modelling result is the salt calibration. Using nine seedlings per group, mean root length decreased from 74.95 mm at 0 mM salt to 34.77 mm at 75 mM, 11.00 mm at 100 mM, and 1.36 mm at 150 mM. Plants at 75 mM retained 46% of the control root length, while 150 mM reduced root growth by roughly 55-fold. This defines a useful stress range for future experiments, but does not yet tell us how much protectant is required.

The model that uses these results →

Modelling the plant stress index

We measured three different indicators of plant health: root length, fresh weight, and chlorophyll. Each captures a different part of the plant's response to stress, but using all three separately makes it difficult to use them in a single model. We therefore developed a stress index that combines them into one value for each group.

For each measurement, the stressed or treated plants are compared with the unstressed control from the same experiment. The resulting values are then combined into a single score. A value of 1.0 represents the matched unstressed control, while a value closer to 0 represents a severely damaged plant.

The weighting of the three measurements needs to be decided before looking at the results. Otherwise, the weights could be adjusted to make the final data look better. We also calculate the index from the original measurements rather than from already-normalized values. This is important because normalizing chlorophyll to fresh weight can introduce the measurement issue described earlier on this page.

Because our current data are not sufficient for a reliable dose-response fit, we also considered several alternatives:

  • Use the measured salt-response data directly. If additional replicates are not available, we can report the salt calibration as measured points instead of fitting a curve.
  • Use published dose-response relationships as a starting point. If data for a similar protectant are available, we can use the published curve shape and fit only the scale to our own data. Any borrowed parameters would be clearly identified.
  • Compare treatment timing instead of fitting a curve. The pre-, co-, and post-treatment groups can still be ranked even if there are not enough points for a full model.
  • Combine the three plant measurements. The stress index gives us a way to use root length, fresh weight, and chlorophyll together rather than choosing only one measurement.

Hydroponics with Prof. Cheng

27 March

We visited Prof. Cheng's lab with no plants and no established protocol. She showed us how her group grows Arabidopsis on agar and in hydroponics, and gave us seeds to take back to our lab.

Her hydroponic setup was much simpler than we expected. It used plastic containers with foam boards that held the seedlings above the medium. April to July

Building our own system

We started making our own hydroponic boxes based on the setup we had seen at NTU. Our first prototypes used different combinations of containers, float plates, filter paper, and seedlings.

The first four prototypes had problems with contamination. Depending on the prototype, between 1 and 7 out of 10 seeds germinated before contamination became a problem. We kept changing the setup to identify where the contamination was coming from and to find a way to keep the seedlings in place.

Prototype 4 was the first design that allowed us to keep seedlings growing for several days without an immediate failure. On 7 June, we transferred seedlings from agar into the box. The setup stayed clean for the first six days, although contamination eventually became a recurring problem across our hydroponic experiments.

We also learned that maintaining the medium was more complicated than simply replacing water when it evaporated. On 17 June, Dr. Kyle pointed out that topping up the evaporated medium would increase the concentration of the substances that remained behind, including salts and sugars. From then on, we treated evaporation as a variable instead of simply adding water back to the box.

On 20 June, Prof. Chen Wen-liang suggested moving toward a recirculating system. We had also considered an automatically filled reservoir, but neither design was built during this round of experiments.

11 August

Looking at a larger system

We visited 源鮮智慧農場 to see how hydroponics was handled on a commercial scale. Our setup at the time held about 250 mL, so seeing rows of larger growing systems gave us a different perspective on how the floating structure could be supported.

28 August

Returning to Prof. Cheng

In August, we returned to Prof. Cheng's lab with two of our hydroponic boxes and an update on our progress. We went through the suggestions she had given us in March and discussed which ones we had been able to implement. Four of the seven had been completed; the ROS staining experiment had not, and we told her that directly.

We also visited two organizations working with agricultural biotechnology and hydroponic farming.

At CH Biotech (正瀚生技) on 9 July, researchers introduced us to their work and their plant biostimulant products. Their regulatory specialist also explained some of the requirements involved in bringing a plant biostimulant to market. We were given 富肽2號, which became the protectant used in experiment set 7. We tested it at three different application timings across several trials. Because it is a finished product, we do not know its exact active component, so we refer to it as a protectant instead of attributing its effects to a specific compound.

At 源鮮智慧農場 on 11 August, we visited a commercial hydroponic farm and discussed our own hydroponic setup with the farm director. Seeing the scale of their system helped us rethink the structure of our 250 mL laboratory setup. We began modifying our hydroponic hardware design after the visit; more details are included in section 3.3.

Classrooms

Our outreach activities also gave us opportunities to teach younger students about the plant biology behind our project.

At DingXi Elementary School on 25 June and 2 July, we introduced students to photosynthesis, genes, and synthetic biology. We also demonstrated chlorophyll extraction using leaves and acetone. The experiment gave students a way to see plant biology directly instead of only through slides.

At Fushing in June and again on 14 August, we introduced students to synthetic biology, DNA, engineered mutants, and iGEM. These activities helped us explain the same concepts we use in our own experiments at a level that younger students could understand.

The public

Our 5 September public forum event brought several parts of our plant work together in one place. We prepared a booth, presentation, and soil trays to introduce our project and experiments to visitors. It also gave us a chance to see how well we could explain the project outside of the laboratory.

Future work

Active peptide identification

The CH Bio peptide is a mixture. If it rescues a plant, we do not know which component did the rescuing.

The route is ordinary deconvolution: fractionate the preparation, run the fractions through the same three-timing assay at 75 and 100 mM, and keep whichever fraction carries the effect. It needs an assay whose noise is smaller than the difference between fractions, which is a stricter requirement than anything the screen has met so far, and it is why this sits under future work and not in progress.

A commercial mixture is also the wrong endpoint for a project whose output is a bacterium expressing one thing. Identifying the active component is what turns a product that works into a sequence that can be built.

High-throughput screening

The current assay costs about 24 days per protectant: four of stratification, ten in the chamber, three of staggered treatment and seven of observation, plus the harvest. Four protectants at three timings and two salt levels is more plates than four people can transfer by hand without the transfer itself becoming the variable.

Three changes would take it from a bench protocol to a pipeline, and all three are in reach of a team next year:

  • Round plates and rosette imaging: forty plants per plate, scored from a single photograph, instead of ten roots traced by hand in ImageJ. The move to horizontal plates in August was the first step; automated rosette segmentation is the second.
  • Treatment in the medium, not on the plant: set 8's transfer protocol removes the application step that broke two trials. It scales in a way that pipetting and spraying do not.
  • A fixed imaging rig per plate format: the vertical holder already exists. The same idea for round plates, with controlled white balance, would let colour be a readout instead of a caution.

What we would hand a team picking this up: the protocol, the ladder, the failure list, and a standing instruction to run the reference compound alongside every screen. Our six sets of trehalose took two months, and writing them up should save the next team from repeating them.

Who advised this work

Four people between them set the readouts, the exclusion rules, the plant age, the salt schedule and the shape of the hydroponic box. Almost every correction on this page is traceable to one of them.

We also learned the plate and hydroponic protocols in person, at Prof. Cheng's laboratory at NTU and at the Verslues lab at Academia Sinica, before running any of this ourselves.
