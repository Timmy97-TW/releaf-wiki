Plants

Abstract

Salinity and heat cut crop productivity, so ReLEAF built an assay that asks whether a protectant helps Arabidopsis through those stresses, and when it has to arrive.

We built the screen from nothing in five months, on agar, in hydroponics and in soil. A salt ladder sets the stress for every run: root length falls 74.95 to 1.36 mm across 0 to 150 mM NaCl, nine seedlings a group, and three timings test when a protectant has to arrive. The CH Bio peptide gave our clearest rescue at 100 mM. Our own candidates have not shown an effect yet. Two lessons changed every run after them: seedlings enter hydroponics from agar, and a bacterial prep is blotted before it touches a plant.

Overview and design

We grow Arabidopsis thaliana three ways — on agar plates, in hydroponic boxes and in pots of soil — and put our plant protectants through each of them. The three systems trade control for realism: agar holds every variable still, soil holds none of them still, and hydroponics sits between the two.

All three follow the same six steps, whatever the plants are growing in. What changes between them is the vessel, how the protectant is applied and which readouts survive the move.

Agar

Why agar

Agar trials were conducted to fine-tune protectant treatments and NaCl stress within a controlled laboratory environment, ensuring uniform nutrient availability and precise stress exposure. This setup enabled the evaluation and screening of protectant efficacy, helping identify top performing protectant candidates while establishing clear physiological baselines prior to testing in complex soil systems. Furthermore, agar media provide essential nutrients and an accelerated timeframe for observing early physiological changes in Arabidopsis seedlings, facilitating rapid and high-throughput testing across subsequent experimental iterations.

Heat stress

A total of 3 agar and 2 soil experimental sets were conducted under 5 days of 37 °C-induced heat stress to evaluate the efficacy of different protectants.

  • ACC deaminase1 pretest + 1 set, 2 trials; soil 1 pretest + 1 set
  • LEA141 set
  • BoPEP41 set, 2 trials

Targeting heat stress, we took inspiration from Zhang et al. [10] for prolonged thermo priming assays protocol designing. Initially, seeds are germinated and grown on Murashige and Skoog (MS) agar plates at 22 °C. Until the seedlings are grown to 10 days old, they’re held at 37 °C for 5 days to induce acquired thermotolerance prior to subsequent higher stress damage, and then returned to 22 °C for 5 days of recovery. Following recovery, seedling survival and physiological damage are assessed and analyzed via multiple quantitative readouts: fresh weight, chlorophyll content, and anthocyanin measurement. Both the agar and soil based thermo priming assays are established based on this foundational framework and methodology, with specific parameters refined to accommodate our experimental setups.

Set design

Among these sets, six sets evaluated the non-reducing disaccharide trehalose, which has been shown to have a protective effect under salinity stress (PMID: 25400644). The remaining two sets used commercially available CH Biotech’s peptide to do the preliminary tests. One additional set used filtered ACC deaminase against salinity stress. Our experimental sets 1–2 were designed to establish the experimental protocols and assess the initial response of plants to NaCl treatment. Therefore, only two NaCl concentrations, 100 and 150 mM, and two trehalose concentrations, 0.5 and 1 mM, were tested. However, we soon realized that 150 mM of NaCl was too strong for the plants and they dried quickly due to the osmotic stress, and while our data predicted the effect of NaCl on the plants, there were no visual differences between protectant-treated and untreated groups.

Continuing with the belief that higher NaCl stress should be fought with higher trehalose content, we conducted experimental set 6, where 0 mM and 5 mM of trehalose was used against 0 mM, 75 mM, 100 mM, and 150 mM of NaCl.

For our seventh experimental trial, we made a few key changes in our plant stress and protection protocol. Firstly, we wanted to verify the purpose and need for an AIoT prediction model and stress sensors in the farming environment, so one of our chosen independent variables was the treatment time. The pre-treatment group would have CH Bio peptide added 1 day before stress strikes, the co-treatment group would have the protectant added on the same day, then the post-treatment group would be treated one day after the NaCl is added. Next, the CH Bio peptide had a recommended dilution level, which was 1/500, for optimal usage, so we did not change the concentration as it would not be used in real fields with a changed concentration level either. Lastly, this group of plants went through 4 days of stratification and 4 days at 22 °C before the pre-treatment group started directly in their plates. Unlike previous experimental sets, we did not want the salt stress and protectant applied on the first day, and as Arabidopsis plants are extremely fragile and vulnerable to transfer, we decided to add the NaCl and CH Bio peptide directly into the agar plate.

Moving on to the first ACCD pre-test, we purified our first round of ACCD and contained it in multiple 15 mL centrifuge tubes for treatment. We had changed the method of treatment to spraying, one up, one left, and one right, yet we followed the same pre-treatment, co-treatment, and post-treatment protocols.

Application

Phase 1 · Trehalose + NaCl combinations

In phase 1 of trehalose testing (including 3 sets of pre-tests and 4 sets of official testings) fine tuned concentrations of trehalose protectants were added directly into the mixture with agar before solidifying, meaning each experimental group contains the corresponding trehalose and salinity concentrations. We selected vertical planting of agar plates to allow clearer and better visions for root length observation and analysis.

On Day 0, sterilized Arabidopsis seeds were vernalized on ½ MS neutral vertical agar plates for 4 days at 4 °C, and moved to the incubator for 4 days germination. On day 8, 4 days old Arabidopsis seedlings were transferred to each of the newly made vertical salinity agar plates containing a corresponding mixture of trehalose with NaCl. During the transferring process, we screen for seedlings with similar root lengths and similar growth levels, and place 12 seedlings per salinity plate with each of their tips of the roots onto a straight baseline drawn by a marker. Thus we could immediately start observing the seedling's root length difference and physiological changes after they’re exposed to different concentrations of NaCl and trehalose, evaluating the rescue effectiveness and growth for each combination over a course of 14 days.

Phase 2 · CH Bio peptide + NaCl

Using commercial CH Bio peptide, the primary objective is to evaluate the efficacy of commercial protectants’ effectiveness and identify its best application timing in response to the exact point where salinity stress hits. Therefore, CH Bio peptide application is categorized into 3 treatment timing groups: pre-treatment, co-treatment, and post-treatment. Different treatment timings are essential connecting to our project goal, “to supply protectants at the optimal timing before stress harms the plants”. These 3 treatment timing groups and the control group (no protectant) were each subjected to three NaCl concentrations (0 mM, 75 mM, and 100 mM), giving a total of 12 experimental conditions.

Looking at the entire experiment timeline, on day 0, Arabidopsis seeds were stratified on ½ MS neutral agar for 4 days at 4 °C, then transferred to a growth chamber for 4 days germination. Protectant applications were initiated subsequently starting from Day 8, and administered at 24-hour intervals between each treatment timing. Treatment timings can be considered as another set of days (Day −1: pre-treatment, Day 0: NaCl + co-treatment, Day 1: post-treatment). Thus, the day of salt stress application is Day 0, whereas NaCl (75 mM and 100 mM NaCl) was applied to all corresponding treatment groups, coinciding with the application for the co-treatment group.

On the technical side, we applied the protectant directly onto the surface of the agar by adding 3 mL then removing 2 mL of excess to achieve 1 mL of final protectant added (the surface tension of the agar causes uneven distribution of protectant, therefore we added a larger volume of protectant to completely submerge the surface first, then suck out the excess protectant last). Furthermore, to prevent varying factors of growth between groups with and without protectants added (as liquids can affect plants’ absorption and growth), we added the equal volume of sterile water (1 mL) to every other group to simulate equal liquid effects on plants.

Phase 3 · ACC deaminase + NaCl

Secreted by our engineered B. subtilis, ACC deaminase, LEA14 and BoPEP4 were used as the protectant against salinity stress in Phase 3. Before applying, the protectant was purified through a 0.22 µm filter. The main difference in this phase’s protectant application is that we utilized a spraying method. We used a sterilized spraying nozzle to dispense 3 sprays of ACCD onto the surface of plants, administered by spraying in 3 equal areas of individual sections (one top left, one top right, and one bottom) through a distance of 10 cm between the nozzle and agar plate.

The overall experimental flow still follows the timeline from phase 2 of CH Bio peptide testing, while we extended the germination time from 4 days to 7 days (this suggestion was provided by Prof. Cheng to use stronger plants for stress testing).

Heat stress · CH Bio peptide (1 experiment), ACC deaminase (2)

Considering that vertical plates cannot hold larger amounts of liquid protectant on the surface like horizontal plates can, we used a spray nozzle that dispensed 1.67 mL for every 3 sprays which we administered by spraying one up, one left, and one right; all sprays were administered above the line with root tips attached. If there were more than 3 sprays, the protectant would be carried downwards by the weight, and we decided to not spray one at the bottom since it would flow downwards regardless.

Hydroponics

Why hydroponics

Abiotic stress, such as salinity stress and heat stress, negatively affects crop productivity. To test the effectiveness of our self-produced protectants under both types of stress, we chose to use a hydroponic system that allows for precise control of environmental factors. Compared with soil-based systems, hydroponics reduces the number of uncontrolled variables, such as differences in soil composition, microbial communities, and water distribution. Therefore, we can establish and maintain consistent experimental conditions by precisely controlling environmental parameters, including pH, electrical conductivity, temperature, and dissolved oxygen. In addition, nutrients and stress treatments can be delivered more uniformly to all plants through the nutrient solution, improving experimental consistency. Because treatments are added directly to the root environment, the hydroponic cultivation system allows us to rapidly observe plant responses to different stress conditions and evaluate the protective effects of our target protectants.

Where our ideas come from

We are inspired by Matthew Gilliham’s hydroponics culture system (Conn et al., 2013) [7]. First, Arabidopsis seeds were sown on 0.6% agar containing black microcentrifuge-tube lids, each with a 3 mm diameter hole punched using a leather punch. Then, the lids containing seeds were placed on a microcentrifuge tube rack with the agar side facing down. Each well was filled with 2.1 mL of ½ MS medium, allowing the agar to remain in contact with the medium throughout stratification and germination. The seeds were stratified at 4 °C in the dark for at least 48 hours, and then transferred to a growth chamber maintained at 22 °C under a 16-hour light/8-hour dark photoperiod. After germination, 4-day-old seedlings were directly transferred onto the floating board or holder and left to grow in the hydroponic box. The tank was kept dark to reduce algal growth and continuously aerated with air stones to maintain sufficient dissolved oxygen. The nutrient solution was regularly replaced every 2–3 days, and the plants were then used for experimental treatments and physiological measurements.

After several rounds of trial and error, we moved to the box shape we kept. We realized a medium-sized box would be ideal because it doesn't occupy too much space. We can reuse it multiple times after cleaning and autoclaving. Its size is enough to plant the amount of plants we want and it is transparent for us to observe the root system. We can punch at least 5 to 13 holes in one board. The contamination of this box is also less often than the previous two that we tested.

This is our improved hydroponic box prototype, version 4.1. We replaced the foam board with a 3D-printed board and added a carrying handle on each side. The foam board became softened after it stayed in the water for a long time. To make the quality of the hydroponic system more consistent, we decided to use a 3D printed board, so that it is far simpler to maintain the shape of the board and prevent broken boards in the middle of changing medium.

Stress design

To evaluate whether our protectants could reduce abiotic stress in plants, we developed multiple experimental platforms, not only agar systems. We needed a platform in which stress could be easily applied for different durations and the concentration of protectants could be precisely controlled. As a result, we can measure the fresh weight or root length of these treated plants, providing simple and accessible ways to quantify the results. In the beginning, we reviewed related papers to figure out how they established stress protocols on Arabidopsis. Here we focused on heat and salinity stress.

Salinity stress

First, we followed Cortés-Jiménez et al. [11]. This experiment tested six NaCl concentrations, 0, 50, 100, 150, 200 and 250 mM, rescued by Azospirillum brasilense, the most studied plant growth promoting bacteria (PGPB). According to the results, plant stems under 0–100 mM NaCl did better than under 150–250 mM, and best at 50 and 100 mM NaCl. This means that these two salinity concentrations are moderate enough for the plant to be rescued by the bacteria. According to the pretests from agar, Arabidopsis applied with 100 mM NaCl expresses a strong stress response but doesn’t die at this threshold and could be rescued. Therefore, we decided to test 100 mM NaCl in our salinity test. Next, we decided to use CH Bio peptide to evaluate the stress response of plants under 100 mM NaCl. We used CH Bio peptide since it is a commercial product that helps plants resist abiotic stress.

Heat stress

One of our main reference papers on heat stress is Auverlot et al. [4], which describes how the different stages of a plant may have different heat tolerance. This helped us make a quick decision about which developmental stage is most suitable for each condition, and also about the growth medium composition, the timing of stress application, and the days of recovery time. This information was particularly important for us because this was our first time cultivating Arabidopsis in the hydroponic platform and also with the heat treatment. For salinity experiments, we therefore designed the system around three variables: plant developmental stage → timing of protectant application → timing and intensity of stress. Overall, integrating all the information will help us accelerate our experiments and increase the success of protectant testing in the hydroponic system.

Soil

Why soil

Soil trials were done to simulate real field conditions and allow protectants to be validated under complex conditions after testing in controlled environments such as agar and hydroponics. Soil introduces physical matrix interactions, including water retention and pore size distribution, which affect how the NaCl and protectant diffuse and reach the plant roots. Additionally, soil contains native microbial flora, allowing us to validate its real world viability. Hydroponics and agar assays are for baseline fine tuning, protocol simulations, and controlled proof of concept, while demonstrating efficacy in real soil reflects smallholder farming realities.

Salt schedule

Salt behaves differently in soil platforms compared to the agar trials. The first attempt built up 25 mM per day toward 100 mM, a gradual ramp proposed by Dr. Verslues [8]. Prof. Cheng's assessment, recorded on 2 September, pointed out that for a month-old plant the accumulation method is too mild, and worse, watering between doses washes the salt back out instead of letting it accumulate.

  1. 100 mMPour 1
  2. 200 mMPour 2
  3. 300 mMPour 3
  4. 300 mMRepeat until visible

The revised schedule pours equal volumes of 100, then 200, then 300 mM, with no watering in between, and then keeps repeating 300 mM until the effect is visible [9]. It has no fixed end date; the plants decide when it stops. A pot takes 30 to 50 mL before a little runs out of the bottom, which is how we know it reached the roots, and the tray under it wants to be 1 to 2 cm deep so it can hold 200 to 400 mL at a time.

We tested three application timings of ACCD protectant, including pre-treatment, co-treatment, and post-treatment, under the NaCl wash of 100 mM, 200 mM, until reaching a final concentration of 300 mM NaCl.

Pretests: trehalose, experimental sets 1–3

Conducting preliminary testing using solely trehalose protectant helped us quantify growth capacity when protectant is added. Each plate still had all 8–9 of their seedlings germinated, so we decided to fine tune the optimal trehalose concentration for rescue effectiveness evaluation. With experimental set 3, we tested out 4 different trehalose dosages, including 0.125 mM, 0.25 mM and 0.5 mM, all under 100 mM NaCl. Experimental groups 1 and 2 revealed that 1 mM was too toxic and 0.5 mM was insufficient against strong salt levels, so we considered manipulating the concentration in between the two to reach a balance. As a pre-test, we were able to identify the early effects of salinity stress on Arabidopsis and key milestones that can be observed, such as stunted root growth or minimized leaf area. Since our protocol was not yet standardized at the time, data collected from early experimental sets only include image documentation (done in a light box with both a black and white background and surrounding lights turned off).

This set maintains similar testing conditions to set 3, utilizing 100 mM NaCl as the standardized stress level, while evaluating a slightly higher range of trehalose doses (0.25, 0.5 and 1.0 mM compared to 0.125, 0.25 and 0.5 mM in set 3). It aimed to assess the rescue efficacy of higher trehalose concentration against fixed 100 mM NaCl salt stress, and to allow direct comparison with the lower doses from the previous set. We introduced major changes in the experimental set-up: the cultivation platform shifted from horizontal to vertical planting, to better observe gravitational root length growth and to facilitate precise root quantification using ImageJ software; the overall growth timeline was adjusted to 2 days stratification at 4 °C, then 4 days growth, then transfer of the 4-day-old seedlings onto newly made salinity plates. 3 major primary parameters were evaluated to quantify overall growth: root length, fresh weight, and chlorophyll content.

Overall, all exogenous trehalose concentrations have demonstrated obvious rescue effectiveness under the subjected 100 mM NaCl stress in relation to the 100 mM NaCl group with 0 mM trehalose, while among the different treatment groups, the leaf colors vary from slight purple to bright green, indicating the imposed ionic stress and toxicity or trehalose rescue effectiveness correspondingly.

Referring to the relative chlorophyll content, the results aligned with a previous prediction that had not been validated before: while too much NaCl was damaging to plant growth, too much trehalose could be as well. As shown, the lowest chlorophyll content occurred in the 1.0 mM trehalose control and the 100 mM NaCl control groups. In fact, the 1.0 mM trehalose group without stress even yielded lower chlorophyll levels than the 100 mM NaCl salinity group. Furthermore, among the plants subjected to 100 mM NaCl salt stress, the 0.5 mM trehalose treatment demonstrated higher chlorophyll content than the higher 1.0 mM dose. This highlights the necessity for fine-tuning protectant concentration, whereas the 0.25 mM dose is slightly inadequate for maximizing rescue effectiveness.

Physiological observations based on the second row of plates reveal that leaves in the 0.25 mM and 1.0 mM trehalose groups partially turned purple, indicating anthocyanin accumulation driven by osmotic stress, meanwhile also validating the physiological effects of a severe 100 mM NaCl stress level. In contrast, leaves from the 0.5 mM trehalose group predominantly maintained a bright or dark green appearance with minimal purple discoloration. The physiological differences demonstrate the necessity of fine-tuning concentration and the relatively outstanding rescue effectiveness of the 0.5 mM trehalose dose. To strengthen the protectant necessity, the groups undergoing 100 mM NaCl stress and trehalose treatments at the same time sustained the greatest amount of chlorophyll, even surpassing the groups without NaCl treatment.

Summary

  • All exogenous trehalose concentrations at 0.25, 0.5 and 1.0 mM under 100 mM NaCl salinity stress have demonstrated obvious rescue effectiveness.
  • 0.5 mM trehalose under 100 mM NaCl exhibited the highest chlorophyll content; 1.0 mM trehalose under 100 mM NaCl exhibited the longest root elongation.
Set 5 · 4 to 12 July · trials 1 and 2 · vertical square plates

Testing with significantly higher NaCl stress levels and trehalose dosages

While conducting experimental set 5, we realized around 2–3 days in that some of the plants had turned white due to chlorosis, which accelerated the breakdown of chlorophyll, leading to a relatively lower chlorophyll content; more severe chlorosis causes total depletion of the pigment in plant leaves. Even in cases where 5 mM and 10 mM of trehalose were used, all of the plates had at least one seedling that had shriveled up and turned white. We decided to redo the experiment in trial 2 with slight changes to our aseptic technique. The control groups without trehalose could reach up to 82.476 mm in root length, yet the 150 mM groups were limited at around 4–5 mm and the 250 mM groups stunted within 1 mm.

Under the no-stress 0 mM NaCl environment, the chlorophyll content of each group remained stable across different trehalose dosages, indicating the plants do not exhibit exogenous protectant-driven growth boosts when not subjected to stress, and also revealing that exogenous trehalose does not impose metabolic toxicity.

In contrast, under severe salinity stress (150 mM NaCl), chlorophyll content exhibited a significant dose-dependent recovery. Specifically, treatments with 5 mM (p<0.01) and 10 mM (p<0.0001) trehalose yielded statistically significant improvements relative to the untreated stressed control, effectively mitigating salt-induced chlorophyll pigment degradation. This upward trend highlights trehalose’s consistent protective efficacy and proportional correlation in rescuing, shown from the effectiveness of increasing trehalose dose against a high salinity stress.

While 150 mM NaCl serves as an optimal severity level for assessing high trehalose dose rescue, 250 mM NaCl is revealed to be too toxic for the seedlings to sustain, exceeding the physiological tolerance of the seedlings, resulting in severe damage such as physiological chlorosis, stunted root growth, and loss of chlorophyll pigments. Specifically, the seedlings obtained little to no primary root length growth, within a 1 mm range. Furthermore, based on the visuals, the seedling groups (5 mM, 10 mM trehalose) are extensively damaged with severe chlorosis on each of the leaves, and might soon die or only be sustaining their lives based on the nutrients provided by the ½ MS agar.

Summary

  • Exogenous trehalose demonstrated significant dose-dependent rescue under stressed 150 mM NaCl groups, meaning an upward trajectory of chlorophyll content as trehalose dosage increases.
  • 250 mM NaCl is revealed to be too toxic for plants to sustain even with protectants received.
Set 6 · 29 July to 3 August · 6 days · vertical square plates

Evaluate the optimized trehalose dose against smaller NaCl concentration intervals

The previous set demonstrated that extreme salt stress such as 250 mM NaCl was too high for the plants to sustain, so we eliminated the highest NaCl concentration and chose smaller intervals under 150 mM for this set. However, plant images revealed a protectant concentration of 5 mM was not sufficient for 150 mM of NaCl because the leaves of both 100 mM NaCl trials turned purple/brown after 3 days of treatment. Overall we selected 3 testing NaCl concentrations with a relatively smaller interval, which are 75, 100, and 150 mM NaCl.

The application of 5 mM trehalose significantly enhanced plant salt tolerance under 100 mM NaCl stress. This is evidenced by an approximate 8.7 times increase in chlorophyll content and a substantial 3 cm increase in root length relative to the untreated control (all highlighted with red outlines). The statistical significance, **** p<0.0001, further validates the result. Crucially, each analysis conducted shows correlation between growth parameters and physiological performance, as also reflected in the plates: the 5 mM trehalose-treated group consistently exhibited superior growth parameters compared to the untreated stressed group.

Regarding the control groups, baseline chlorophyll levels remain comparable between the control and the 5 mM trehalose group, while there is a minor decrease in root length growth of the 5 mM trehalose group, indicating that the protectant alone has a negligible effect on non-stressed plants. Once salinity reaches 150 mM NaCl, the high stress concentration severely damaged the plants, as evidenced by physically observed chlorosis and significantly stunted growth shown by data analysis, where 5 mM trehalose did not play much role in rescuing the plants, only demonstrating a minor, within 1 cm, increase in root length, and nonetheless showing a decrease in chlorophyll content, indicating its insufficient dose.

What we found unexpected in this experiment

Contrary to our initial hypothesis, the application of 5 mM trehalose did not result in a protective effect under mild salt stress, 75 mM NaCl, relative to the untreated stress control; instead, the trehalose-treated group exhibited reduced chlorophyll content. We originally anticipated that the 5 mM trehalose dose would work more effectively under a lower stress level of 75 mM, but instead, the trehalose supplementation only proved effective under 100 mM NaCl, a higher salt threshold. This outcome suggests that 75 mM NaCl is a mild salt stress, where the plants’ endogenous regulatory systems are still sufficient to maintain homeostasis without exogenous protectants. In contrast, 100 mM NaCl likely exceeds the threshold of plant tolerance, hence triggering necessary protective mechanisms, and more effectively utilized exogenous trehalose to mitigate the cellular damage. Thereby the 100 mM NaCl groups treated with 5 mM trehalose sustained significantly better growth compared to their untreated control.

It is also observed that the root length of the 100 mM control was clearly much lower than that of 75 mM, barely growing even after 6–7 days of protection and NaCl treatments. The shift in physiological effects of plants from 75 mM to 100 mM NaCl reveals the relatively strong growth inhibition 100 mM NaCl poses on plants, whereas 75 mM did not stunt plant growth as much as 100 mM NaCl did.

Summary

  • The previous set showed that 250 mM NaCl is too toxic for plants to sustain, therefore this set applied smaller intervals of NaCl within 150 mM for salinity stress.
  • 5 mM trehalose significantly enhanced plant salt tolerance under 100 mM NaCl induced stress.
  • 5 mM trehalose is revealed to be negligible and less effective against 75 mM NaCl, while insufficient to rescue against 150 mM NaCl.

Hydroponics · CH Bio peptide pre-test · 13-day-old plants · 7 days of salt stress

CH Bio peptide at 1:500 against 100 mM NaCl, by treatment timing

In order to determine suitable experimental conditions, we used CH Bio peptide, a commercial protectant against drought stress, to conduct preliminary tests under both salinity and heat stress at a dilution factor of 1:500. The objective of these pre-tests was to evaluate whether this product exhibits similar protective effects under these two stress conditions.

To evaluate the salt sensitivity of Arabidopsis, 13-day-old Arabidopsis were treated with 100 mM NaCl for 7 days. Peptide treatments were also performed using three different regimens, pre-treatment, co-treatment, and post-treatment, in separate hydroponic boxes. The control group was also treated with 100 mM NaCl, but not treated with CH Bio peptide. In this test, the co-treatment group grew the best after salinity treatment. We found out that 100 mM of NaCl would cause significant stress on Arabidopsis but won’t cause death, which helped us to determine the appropriate NaCl concentration for further experiments.

The co-treatment group showed higher rescue effectiveness based on the observation of rosette leaf area and also chlorophyll content. After 7 days of salinity stress, most leaves in the co-treatment group remained green, whereas leaves in the other treatment groups turned yellow or white. Moreover, the co-treatment group had the highest fresh weight and chlorophyll contents, showing that the co-treatment group grew the best.

Hydroponics · CH Bio peptide pre-test · 12-day-old plants · 7 days of heat stress

CH Bio peptide against heat stress, by treatment timing

Our second set of pre-tests is heat stress testing, where 12-day-old Arabidopsis were treated at 37 °C for 7 days. Except for the control group, CH Bio peptide was applied with a duration of 1 day at 3 different time periods: 1 day before heat treatment (pre-treatment), simultaneously with heat treatment (co-treatment), and 1 day after heat was applied (post-treatment). No obvious difference was shown between the treatment groups based on observation of plant size and color.

Hydroponics · buffer control · six dilutions of the medium

Rule out the B. subtilis medium itself, and set the hydroponic pH

Before applying target protectants to plants, the B. subtilis-only control is pretested to exclude the buffer effect on the plant. Therefore, different dilution factors of B. subtilis-containing TB medium were prepared as follows: 0, 1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 and 1/16.

In addition, the pH of the 0.1× MS medium is originally 5.7, which is suitable for plant growth. However, the optimal pH for the activity of 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate deaminase (ACCD) is 8–8.5, which differs from the original pH. Therefore, we decided to set up two different pH conditions on plants. After research, we found out that ACCD still has 75% of its activity level at pH 7, so we decided to compare the growth of plants at two different pHs: pH 5.7 and pH 7.

Under both pH values, Arabidopsis applied with B. subtilis-containing medium all showed a significant stress response. The stress was most severe at the 1 and 1/2 dilution factors, where leaves turned yellow and the plants were small. Groups with 1/4, 1/8, and 1/16 showed significant accumulation of anthocyanin (a stress response), which can be seen by the darker green color of leaves. The only group with no stress response is the group without B. subtilis-containing TB medium, which shows a higher fresh weight and chlorophyll content than all the other treatment groups. Therefore, we concluded that concentrations greater than 1/16 can cause stress to plants, and concentrations lower than 1/16 will be used in further experiments.

Dry lab collaborations

Our collaboration with the dry lab began with two practical questions: how much protectant would be required to improve plant performance, and how early would it need to be applied? Our plates hand the model three things.

  • 74.95 → 1.36 mmMean root length from 0 to 150 mM NaCl, set 6: the salt calibration
  • 1.67 mLPer three sprays on a 78.5 cm² plate, set 9: the dose the model scales to a hectare
  • 3 runs, 2 answersPre-, co- or post-treatment: when the protectant must arrive

How much protectant

Our original approach was to construct a dose-response curve using protectant concentration and a rescue index. Trehalose initially appeared suitable because we had conducted six experimental sets, but more measurements did not necessarily produce a more reliable relationship. In Set 3, two trials using the same design produced opposing trends, and because each condition was represented by a single plate, the seedlings on that plate could not be treated as independent replicates.

So we removed trehalose from the circuit design. A model should represent what the experiments can support, not force uncertain data into a quantitative relationship. The dose the model now carries, 1.67 mL per three sprays, is a dose that worked rather than the smallest that works; one plate with neat, 1:3, 1:10 and 1:30 arms would find the minimum.

When it must arrive

Since July we have run the same design: the protectant goes on one day before the salt, with it, or one day after. That design exists because the dry lab asked whether a farm needs a prediction at all. If pre-treatment wins, ReLEAF needs a forecast; if co-treatment wins, it needs a full tank and a fast response, which is a different machine.

Three runs have reported and they disagree. On agar at 75 mM, scored as fresh weight, pre-treatment wins. In soil at 300 mM, scored as chlorophyll per gram, co-treatment wins, and the commercial peptide at 100 mM also put co-treatment first. The soil run cannot settle it: read as total chlorophyll, the same tubes put co-treatment last.

Scoring a plant

Root length tracks growth inhibition, fresh weight tracks accumulated biomass, and chlorophyll tracks the photosynthetic tissue. We explored a stress index that compares each with the unstressed control from the same experiment, so that a value near 1 is the matched control and a value approaching 0 is severe impairment.

It also made data quality the central concern. Normalizing chlorophyll content by fresh weight can make severely stressed plants appear to have higher chlorophyll values because their biomass is lower, so we keep the original measurements beside the index.

Future work

Active peptide identification

CH Bio peptide is a mixture, so a rescue does not say which component did it. Fractionating the preparation and running the fractions through the same three-timing assay would turn a product that works into a sequence we can build.

High-throughput screening

One protectant takes about 24 days on the current assay. Three changes would make it a pipeline:

  • Round plates scored from a single photograph, forty plants at a time.
  • A fixed imaging rig for each plate format, with controlled white balance.
