Plants

Abstract

Salinity and heat cut crop productivity, so ReLEAF built an assay that asks whether a protectant helps Arabidopsis through those stresses, and when it has to arrive.

We built the screen from nothing in five months, on agar, in hydroponics and in soil. A salt ladder sets the stress for every run: root length falls 74.95 to 1.36 mm across 0 to 150 mM NaCl, and three timings, a day before the stress, with it, and a day after, ask when a protectant has to arrive. Trehalose held chlorophyll at 1.0 mM under 100 mM NaCl and at 5 and 10 mM under 150 mM; the CH Bio peptide gave our clearest rescue at 100 mM, where post-treatment recovered the most chlorophyll. Our own protectants have their first signals: on agar, ACC deaminase held chlorophyll under 37 °C thermo priming in both trials, which rank the timings differently, post-treatment leading the second at 14% above the heat-stressed control; in water, ACC deaminase at 1/80 or stronger improved heat resistance and BoPEP4 at 1/40 carried plants green through 100 mM NaCl. Timing reads one way under salt and the other under heat, which is the measurement a stress forecast has to stand on.

Overview and design

We cultivated Arabidopsis thaliana using three experimental systems, agar plates, hydroponic boxes and soil pots, to evaluate the effects of our bioprotectants under different growing conditions. Each system offered a different balance between experimental control and environmental realism: agar plates provided highly controlled conditions, soil more closely represented natural growing environments, and hydroponics offered an intermediate approach.

All three systems followed the same six-step experimental framework, with adjustments to the cultivation setup, bioprotectant application methods, and measurable plant responses according to the characteristics of each system.

Stratify

Four days of stratification at 4 °C synchronizes germination, so that the phenotype we read later belongs to the stressor and not to seedlings that started at different times. Stratification suppresses the master dormancy gene DELAY OF GERMINATION 1 (DOG1) and its downstream regulators, shifting the hormonal balance from abscisic acid towards gibberellins and weakening the seed coat enough for the radicle to emerge [15].

Agar

Why agar

Agar trials were conducted to fine-tune protectant treatments and NaCl stress within a controlled laboratory environment, ensuring uniform nutrient availability and precise stress exposure. This setup enabled the evaluation and screening of protectant efficacy, helping identify top performing protectant candidates while establishing clear physiological baselines prior to testing in complex soil systems. Furthermore, agar media provide essential nutrients and an accelerated timeframe for observing early physiological changes in Arabidopsis seedlings, facilitating rapid and high-throughput testing across subsequent experimental iterations.

Targeting heat stress, we took inspiration from Auverlot et al. [4] for prolonged thermo priming assays protocol designing. Initially, seeds are germinated and grown on Murashige and Skoog (MS) agar plates at 22 °C. Until the seedlings are grown to 10 days old, they’re held at 37 °C for 5 days to induce acquired thermotolerance prior to subsequent higher stress damage, and then returned to 22 °C for 5 days of recovery. Following recovery, seedling survival and physiological damage are assessed and analyzed via multiple quantitative readouts: fresh weight, chlorophyll content, and anthocyanin measurement. Both the agar and soil based thermo priming assays are established based on this foundational framework and methodology, with specific parameters refined to accommodate our experimental setups.

Set design

Initial screening

At the beginning of the plant trials we built the protocol and looked at how the plants answered NaCl and a first range of trehalose concentrations: trehalose at 0.125, 0.5 and 1 mM, NaCl at 50, 100 and 150 mM. We soon found 150 mM NaCl too strong, the plants drying out quickly under the osmotic stress. Trehalose at 1 mM was toxic, and both 1 and 0.5 mM held growth back. From then on the salinity sets ran at 75 and 100 mM NaCl, with trehalose at 0, 5 and 10 mM.

Treatment timing

From experimental set 7 onwards, every set tests protectant effectiveness by the timing of its application. Each one carries a control, a pre-treatment, a co-treatment and a post-treatment group, with the protectant going on one day before the heat or salt stress and one day after it. This is the part of the screen that speaks to the rest of ReLEAF: a protectant that has to reach the plant before the stress surge is the argument for the bioreactor, and for the stress sensors and the AIoT forecast that tell it when to run.

Application

Phase 1 · Trehalose + NaCl combinations

In phase 1 of trehalose testing (3 sets of pre-tests and 4 sets of official experiments) fine tuned concentrations of trehalose were added directly into the mixture with agar before autoclaving, meaning each experimental group holds its own trehalose and salinity concentration without any further contact. We selected vertical planting of agar plates to allow clearer and better visions for root length observation and analysis.

On Day 0, sterilized Arabidopsis seeds were vernalized on ½ MS neutral vertical agar plates for 4 days at 4 °C, and moved to the incubator for 4 days germination. On day 8, 4 days old Arabidopsis seedlings were transferred to each of the newly made vertical salinity agar plates containing a corresponding mixture of trehalose with NaCl. During the transferring process, we screen for seedlings with similar root lengths and similar growth levels, and place 12 seedlings per salinity plate with each of their tips of the roots onto a straight baseline drawn by a marker. Thus we could immediately start observing the seedling's root length difference and physiological changes after they’re exposed to different concentrations of NaCl and trehalose, evaluating the rescue effectiveness and growth for each combination over a course of 14 days.

Phase 2 · CH Bio peptide + NaCl

Using commercial CH Bio peptide, the primary objective is to evaluate the efficacy of commercial protectants’ effectiveness and identify its best application timing in response to the exact point where salinity stress hits. Therefore, CH Bio peptide application is categorized into 3 treatment timing groups: pre-treatment, co-treatment, and post-treatment. Different treatment timings are essential connecting to our project goal, “to supply protectants at the optimal timing before stress harms the plants”. These 3 treatment timing groups and the control group (no protectant) were each subjected to three NaCl concentrations (0 mM, 75 mM, and 100 mM), giving a total of 12 experimental conditions.

Looking at the entire experiment timeline, on day 0, Arabidopsis seeds were stratified on ½ MS neutral agar for 4 days at 4 °C, then transferred to a growth chamber for 4 days germination. Protectant applications were initiated subsequently starting from Day 8, and administered at 24-hour intervals between each treatment timing. Treatment timings can be considered as another set of days (Day −1: pre-treatment, Day 0: NaCl + co-treatment, Day 1: post-treatment). Thus, the day of salt stress application is Day 0, whereas NaCl (75 mM and 100 mM NaCl) was applied to all corresponding treatment groups, coinciding with the application for the co-treatment group.

On the technical side, we applied the protectant directly onto the surface of the agar by adding 3 mL then removing 2 mL of excess to achieve 1 mL of final protectant added (the surface tension of the agar causes uneven distribution of protectant, therefore we added a larger volume of protectant to completely submerge the surface first, then suck out the excess protectant last). Furthermore, to prevent varying factors of growth between groups with and without protectants added (as liquids can affect plants’ absorption and growth), we added the equal volume of sterile water (1 mL) to every other group to simulate equal liquid effects on plants.

Phase 3 · Bioreactor-produced protectants + NaCl

Secreted by our engineered B. subtilis, ACC deaminase, LEA14 and BoPEP4 were used as the protectant against salinity stress in Phase 3. Before applying, the protectant was filtered through a 0.22 µm filter. The main difference in this phase’s protectant application is that we utilized a spraying method. We used a sterilized spraying nozzle to dispense 3 sprays of ACCD onto the surface of plants, administered by spraying in 3 equal areas of individual sections (one top left, one top right, and one bottom) through a distance of 10 cm between the nozzle and agar plate.

The overall experimental flow still follows the timeline from phase 2 of CH Bio peptide testing, while we extended the germination time from 4 days to 7 days (this suggestion was provided by Prof. Cheng to use stronger plants for stress testing).

Heat stress · phase 1 · CH Bio peptide at 37 °C

At the beginning of the experiment we did not use a heat shock, because a sudden shock is not what a field sees. Instead we thermo-primed for 3 days at 37 °C, which stands in for the slow change of temperature over a day. Vertical plates cannot hold as much liquid protectant on the surface as horizontal plates can, so we sprayed: a nozzle that dispenses 0.536 mL for every 3 sprays, which keeps the dose each plant receives standardized across plant trials. The three sprays go one up, one left and one right, all above the line the root tips sit on; more than three, and the weight carries the protectant down the plate.

Heat stress · phase 2 · ACC deaminase at 37 °C

Protectants usually go in through the roots, but ACC deaminase is a large enzyme and is taken up through roots with difficulty. With the vertical plates again unable to hold liquid, we sprayed three times for a total of 0.536 mL. Contamination obstructed the data collection in the first trial, so we moved the clean seedlings onto new plates and carried on with the same method; mold still grew extensively on the finished plates. Our instructors suggested an unsterilized spray nozzle, so for the last heat trial we cleaned the nozzles by spraying sterilized water, then 75% ethanol, then sterilized water again.

Hydroponics

Why hydroponics

Abiotic stress, such as salinity stress and heat stress, negatively affects crop productivity. To test the effectiveness of our self-produced protectants under both types of stress, we chose to use a hydroponic system that allows for precise control of environmental factors. Compared with soil-based systems, hydroponics reduces the number of uncontrolled variables, such as differences in soil composition, microbial communities, and water distribution. Therefore, we can establish and maintain consistent experimental conditions by precisely controlling environmental parameters, including pH, electrical conductivity, temperature, and dissolved oxygen. In addition, nutrients and stress treatments can be delivered more uniformly to all plants through the nutrient solution, improving experimental consistency. Because treatments are added directly to the root environment, the hydroponic cultivation system allows us to rapidly observe plant responses to different stress conditions and evaluate the protective effects of our target protectants.

Where our ideas come from

We are inspired by Matthew Gilliham’s hydroponics culture system (Conn et al., 2013) [7]. First, Arabidopsis seeds were sown on 0.6% agar containing black microcentrifuge-tube lids, each with a 3 mm diameter hole punched using a leather punch. Then, the lids containing seeds were placed on a microcentrifuge tube rack with the agar side facing down. Each well was filled with 2.1 mL of ½ MS medium, allowing the agar to remain in contact with the medium throughout stratification and germination. The seeds were stratified at 4 °C in the dark for at least 48 hours, and then transferred to a growth chamber maintained at 22 °C under a 16-hour light/8-hour dark photoperiod. After germination, 4-day-old seedlings were directly transferred onto the floating board or holder and left to grow in the hydroponic box. The tank was kept dark to reduce algal growth and continuously aerated with air stones to maintain sufficient dissolved oxygen. The nutrient solution was regularly replaced every 2–3 days, and the plants were then used for experimental treatments and physiological measurements.

Stress design

To evaluate whether our protectants could reduce abiotic stress in plants, we developed multiple experimental platforms, not only agar systems. We needed a platform in which stress could be easily applied for different durations and the concentration of protectants could be precisely controlled. As a result, we can measure the fresh weight or root length of these treated plants, providing simple and accessible ways to quantify the results. In the beginning, we reviewed related papers to figure out how they established stress protocols on Arabidopsis. Here we focused on heat and salinity stress.

Salinity stress

First, we followed Cortés-Jiménez et al. [11]. This experiment tested six NaCl concentrations, 0, 50, 100, 150, 200 and 250 mM, rescued by Azospirillum brasilense, the most studied plant growth promoting bacteria (PGPB). According to the results, plant stems under 0–100 mM NaCl did better than under 150–250 mM, and best at 50 and 100 mM NaCl. This means that these two salinity concentrations are moderate enough for the plant to be rescued by the bacteria. According to the pretests from agar, Arabidopsis applied with 100 mM NaCl expresses a strong stress response but doesn’t die at this threshold and could be rescued. Therefore, we decided to test 100 mM NaCl in our salinity test. Next, we decided to use CH Bio peptide to evaluate the stress response of plants under 100 mM NaCl. We used CH Bio peptide since it is a commercial product that helps plants resist abiotic stress.

Heat stress

One of our main reference papers on heat stress is Auverlot et al. [4], which describes how the different stages of a plant may have different heat tolerance. This helped us make a quick decision about which developmental stage is most suitable for each condition, and also about the growth medium composition, the timing of stress application, and the days of recovery time. This information was particularly important for us because this was our first time cultivating Arabidopsis in the hydroponic platform and also with the heat treatment. For salinity experiments, we therefore designed the system around three variables: plant developmental stage → timing of protectant application → timing and intensity of stress. Overall, integrating all the information will help us accelerate our experiments and increase the success of protectant testing in the hydroponic system.

Soil

Why soil

Soil trials were done to simulate real field conditions and allow protectants to be validated under complex conditions after testing in controlled environments such as agar and hydroponics. Soil introduces physical matrix interactions, including water retention and pore size distribution, which affect how the NaCl and protectant diffuse and reach the plant roots. Additionally, soil contains native microbial flora, allowing us to validate its real world viability. Hydroponics and agar assays are for baseline fine tuning, protocol simulations, and controlled proof of concept, while demonstrating efficacy in real soil reflects smallholder farming realities.

Two stages of plant growth

Arabidopsis runs through its life in six to eight weeks, in four stages: germination and seedling establishment, stages 0.1 to 0.7, days 0 to 7; rosette growth, stages 1.0 to 1.12, weeks 2 to 4; floral transition and bolting, stages 5.0 to 6.5, weeks 4 to 5; and reproductive senescence, stages 8.0 to 9.0, from week 6 [1]. Our first salinity assays used 1-month-old plants, at the late rosette to early bolting junction. There the plant is already moving its resources towards floral meristem development instead of ordinary vegetative upkeep [18], and the lower leaves begin to senesce, which puts baseline variation into the assay and makes the difference between a stressed and an unstressed plant harder to read. Following Prof. Cheng’s advice we moved the testing window to 2-week-old plants, stages 1.02 to 1.04: active vegetative growth, with no floral transition or senescence in the way.

Salt schedule

Salt behaves differently in soil platforms compared to the agar trials. The first attempt built up 25 mM per day toward 100 mM, a gradual ramp proposed by Dr. Verslues [8]. Prof. Cheng's assessment, recorded on 2 September, pointed out that for a month-old plant the accumulation method is too mild, and worse, watering between doses washes the salt back out instead of letting it accumulate.

  1. 100 mMPour 1
  2. 200 mMPour 2
  3. 300 mMPour 3
  4. 300 mMRepeat until visible

The revised schedule pours equal volumes of 100, then 200, then 300 mM, with no watering in between, and then keeps repeating 300 mM until the effect is visible [9]. It has no fixed end date; the plants decide when it stops. A pot takes 30 to 50 mL before a little runs out of the bottom, which is how we know it reached the roots, and the tray under it wants to be 1 to 2 cm deep so it can hold 200 to 400 mL at a time.

We tested three application timings of ACCD protectant, including pre-treatment, co-treatment, and post-treatment, under the NaCl wash of 100 mM, 200 mM, until reaching a final concentration of 300 mM NaCl.

Heat schedule

To test what ACC deaminase does for a plant under heat, we took the thermo-priming frame of our agar work into soil, building on Auverlot et al. [4]. On agar, 10-day-old seedlings went to 37 °C for 5 days to induce acquired thermotolerance, then recovered for 5 days at 22 °C, and we scored survival, fresh weight, chlorophyll retention and anthocyanin accumulation. Soil-grown plants needed both the incubation and the treatment schedule adapted: two-week-old plants go into a 37 °C incubator for 5 days of heat stress, then recover for 5 days in the growth chamber at 22 to 25 °C.

In a field, high salinity and extreme heat do not arrive on a schedule; they come as fluctuating events. Testing the same three timings here, pre-, co- and post-treatment, is how we ask at which phase of a stress a farmer could still reach for a microbial inoculant and get something back.

Pretests: trehalose, experimental sets 1–3

Conducting preliminary testing using solely trehalose protectant helped us quantify growth capacity when protectant is added. Each plate still had all 8–9 of their seedlings germinated, so we decided to fine tune the optimal trehalose concentration for rescue effectiveness evaluation. With experimental set 3, we tested out 4 different trehalose dosages, including 0.125 mM, 0.25 mM and 0.5 mM, all under 100 mM NaCl. Experimental groups 1 and 2 revealed that 1 mM was too toxic and 0.5 mM was insufficient against strong salt levels, so we considered manipulating the concentration in between the two to reach a balance. As a pre-test, we were able to identify the early effects of salinity stress on Arabidopsis and key milestones that can be observed, such as stunted root growth or minimized leaf area. Since our protocol was not yet standardized at the time, data collected from early experimental sets only include image documentation (done in a light box with both a black and white background and surrounding lights turned off).

On those observations we moved to standardized experiments that carry quantitative measurements of primary root length, fresh weight and chlorophyll content.

What we found unexpected in this experiment

Contrary to our initial hypothesis, the application of 5 mM trehalose did not result in a protective effect under mild salt stress, 75 mM NaCl, relative to the untreated stress control; instead, the trehalose-treated group exhibited reduced chlorophyll content. We originally anticipated that the 5 mM trehalose dose would work more effectively under a lower stress level of 75 mM, but instead, the trehalose supplementation only proved effective under 100 mM NaCl, a higher salt threshold. This outcome suggests that 75 mM NaCl is a mild salt stress, where the plants’ endogenous regulatory systems are still sufficient to maintain homeostasis without exogenous protectants. In contrast, 100 mM NaCl likely exceeds the threshold of plant tolerance, hence triggering necessary protective mechanisms, and more effectively utilized exogenous trehalose to mitigate the cellular damage. Thereby the 100 mM NaCl groups treated with 5 mM trehalose sustained significantly better growth compared to their untreated control.

It is also observed that the root length of the 100 mM control was clearly much lower than that of 75 mM, barely growing even after 6–7 days of protection and NaCl treatments. The shift in physiological effects of plants from 75 mM to 100 mM NaCl reveals the relatively strong growth inhibition 100 mM NaCl poses on plants, whereas 75 mM did not stunt plant growth as much as 100 mM NaCl did.

Hydroponics · buffer control · six dilutions of the medium

B. subtilis TB medium alone, by dilution and pH

Before applying target protectants to plants, the B. subtilis-only control is pretested to exclude the buffer effect on the plant. Therefore, different dilution factors of B. subtilis-containing TB medium were prepared as follows: 0, 1, 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 and 1/16.

In addition, the pH of the 0.1× MS medium is originally 5.7, which is suitable for plant growth. However, the optimal pH for the activity of 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate deaminase (ACCD) is 7.5 [19], which differs from the original pH. Therefore, we decided to set up two different pH conditions on plants. ACCD still holds about 75% of its activity at pH 7 [19], so we compared the growth of plants at two different pHs: pH 5.7 and pH 7.

Under both pH values, Arabidopsis applied with B. subtilis-containing medium all showed a significant stress response. The stress was most severe at the 1 and 1/2 dilution factors, where leaves turned yellow and the plants were small. Groups with 1/4, 1/8, and 1/16 showed significant accumulation of anthocyanin (a stress response), which can be seen by the darker green color of leaves. The only group with no stress response is the group without B. subtilis-containing TB medium, which shows a higher fresh weight and chlorophyll content than all the other treatment groups. Therefore, we concluded that concentrations greater than 1/16 can cause stress to plants, and concentrations lower than 1/16 will be used in further experiments.

Establish the appropriate stress levels and treatment method

We evaluated the two published methods of tuning salinity levels against each other, the sub-irrigation accumulation method from Kim et al. [8] and the top-down wash from Cheng et al. [9], on 2-week-old Arabidopsis. Seeds were stratified in 1 mL of sterile water for 4 days and dispersed into the soil pots with a micropipette, then selected for even rosette development. The accumulation method gave sequential sub-irrigation doses of 100 mM NaCl by tray capillary action, with plain water in between. The wash method gave sequential surface drenches of 100, 200 and 300 mM NaCl, 30 to 50 mL a pot, with no plain water in between, until the pot was saturated and the root zone had equilibrated. Phenotype, fresh weight and chlorophyll were taken at the end of the stress cycle.

The accumulation method did not impose enough osmotic stress. Its pots carried lush rosettes and an average fresh weight of about 0.42 g, because the plain water between doses flushed the accumulated ions out of the active root zone instead of letting them build up. The top-down drench came out at about 0.15 g, a reduction of roughly 64%, which is the salt holding back cell expansion and tissue water. It produces clear, reproducible growth inhibition and salt stress symptoms without immediate lethal necrosis, so it is the schedule for high-concentration soil screening even though its baseline biomass is lower. Both protocols gave comparable volumetric chlorophyll near 1.0 mg/L: under the milder accumulation stress pigment density stays high because chloroplast development is uninhibited, and under the wash the smaller rosette concentrates what pigment is left into less tissue.

What this fixes for ACC deaminase

The choice of salinity schedule decides whether an ACC deaminase effect can be seen at all. Salt-stressed plants accumulate 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid, the precursor of ethylene; ethylene has its own part in normal development and in the stress response, but too much of it under stress inhibits root elongation and growth. Orozco-Mosqueda et al. [24] review the evidence that plant-growth-promoting bacteria carrying ACC deaminase break that precursor down to ammonia and α-ketobutyrate, which lowers the excess and lets the plant keep growing in saline soil, and that biomass and growth traits are the right readouts for it. A treatment that barely inhibits growth leaves no room to tell protection from ordinary growth; one that destroys the tissue leaves no viable growth for a rescue to show up in. The lower mean fresh weight of the top-down drench puts it between the two, which is what the ACC deaminase runs need.

Summary

  • The top-down wash cuts fresh weight to about 0.15 g against 0.42 g for the accumulation method, a reduction of roughly 64%.
  • Volumetric chlorophyll is comparable under both, near 1.0 mg/L.
  • The wash is the schedule the soil salinity trials use.

Execution of salinity trials: ACCD salinity

With the wash schedule settled, we tested three application timings of ACC deaminase, pre-treatment, co-treatment and post-treatment, under the NaCl wash of 100 mM, then 200 mM, until reaching a final concentration of 300 mM NaCl.

By eye, pre- and co-treatment did not hold off the bleaching; post-treatment did, a little. The two chlorophyll readouts rank the timings differently. Normalized to fresh weight, co-treatment comes out highest, 0.92 against 0.60 mg g−1 for the control, and it also carries the largest fresh weight of the four, 0.19 g. On the unnormalized extract the order reverses: post-treatment at 1.13 mg/L, the control at 1.00, pre-treatment at 0.67 and co-treatment last at 0.30. Post-treatment is the only timing above the control, by about 13%.

The schedule explains part of that. At 100 mM NaCl the stress symptoms were not obvious enough to read a rescue against; they appeared at 300 mM, which is why that level became the soil baseline. Post-treatment went on one day after the 300 mM wash, so it is a delayed co-treatment given once the stress had taken hold, while the co-treatment groups were dosed while the salt was still mild. What the run suggests is that ACC deaminase is worth giving at the point the stress is established rather than before it, and one soil set is not enough to settle it.

Summary

  • Post-treatment ACC deaminase holds the most green rosettes of the three timings under 300 mM NaCl.
  • On unnormalized chlorophyll, post-treatment is the only timing above the untreated control, at 1.13 against 1.00 mg/L.
  • 300 mM NaCl is the soil stress level that makes a rescue readable; 100 mM is not.

How much protectant

Our original approach was to construct a dose-response curve using protectant concentration and a rescue index. Trehalose initially appeared suitable because we had conducted six experimental sets, but more measurements did not necessarily produce a more reliable relationship. In Set 3, two trials using the same design produced opposing trends, and because each condition was represented by a single plate, the seedlings on that plate could not be treated as independent replicates.

So we removed trehalose from the circuit design. A model should represent what the experiments can support, not force uncertain data into a quantitative relationship. The dose the model now carries, 1.67 mL per three sprays, is a dose that produced a rescue, so it stands as an upper bound on what a field would need.

The data we hold do not support a reliable dose-response fit, so we weighed four ways of using the plant results as they are.

  • Report the salt calibration as measured points, with no curve through them.
  • Take the shape of a published dose-response curve for a similar protectant and fit only its scale to our data, with every borrowed parameter named.
  • Rank the pre-, co- and post-treatment groups against each other, which the data can carry even where a full model cannot.
  • Combine root length, fresh weight and chlorophyll in the stress index, instead of choosing one measurement.

When it must arrive

Since July we have run the same design: the protectant goes on one day before the salt, with it, or one day after. That design exists because the dry lab asked whether a farm needs a prediction at all. If pre-treatment wins, ReLEAF needs a forecast; if co-treatment wins, it needs a full tank and a fast response, which is a different machine.

Four runs have reported and they do not agree. On agar at 75 mM, scored as fresh weight, pre-treatment wins. In soil at 300 mM, scored as chlorophyll per gram, co-treatment wins, and the commercial peptide at 100 mM also put co-treatment first. Under 37 °C thermo priming on agar, the second ACC deaminase trial puts post-treatment first, 14% above the heat-stressed control. The soil run cannot settle its own case: read as total chlorophyll, the same tubes put co-treatment last and post-treatment first, 13% above the untreated control.

Read together, the salt runs favor an early arrival and the heat run a late one, which is what the ethylene account behind ACC deaminase predicts: ACC accumulates as a salt stress builds, and again when a heat episode lifts. The forecast horizon is therefore a per-stress number in the model, not one constant for the whole farm.

Scoring a plant

Root length tracks growth inhibition, fresh weight tracks accumulated biomass, and chlorophyll tracks the photosynthetic tissue. We explored a stress index that compares each with the unstressed control from the same experiment, so that a value near 1 is the matched control and a value approaching 0 is severe impairment.

It also made data quality the central concern. Normalizing chlorophyll content by fresh weight can make severely stressed plants appear to have higher chlorophyll values because their biomass is lower, so we keep the original measurements beside the index.

The updated model

The model has grown past predicting a protectant concentration. It ties historical weather and crop-yield data to a stress forecast, turns the demand that forecast implies into a response from the light-controlled production system, and uses the plant-treatment data to estimate what an application would take and where a field-scale constraint would bite.

Two of our measurements carry most of that weight: the response to salt, and the dose used in the treatment trials. In set 6, mean root length fell from 74.95 mm with no added salt to 34.77 mm at 75 mM, 11.00 mm at 100 mM and 1.36 mm at 150 mM. That is the empirical basis for telling moderate stress apart from conditions in which growth has all but stopped, and it is why stress calibration has to come before protectant screening: too severe a treatment leaves no room for an improvement to show.

Timing is the second link. ReLEAF is built to anticipate stress, so the model has to account for the time between starting production and applying the protectant, and our trials compare pre-, co- and post-treatment instead of concentration alone. The updated dataset suggests ACCD-containing supernatant before salt exposure may help; the evidence is preliminary, and it does not establish an optimal timing across replicated experiments.

The model also takes an initial DNPH-based activity estimate into its production and field-application figures. That measurement gave a signal, its estimated activity sat below the quantification limit of the assay, and it was not replicated, so what follows from it is a conditional projection and not a validated system capacity.

Between the bench and the model, the objective changed: from finding a curve that described the data we had, to naming the measurements a reliable model needs. As it stands the model is calibrated on two anchors, the salt ladder and the applied dose, and what it reports against them is a projection with both anchors named.

Future work

The screen as it stands fixes the stress levels, the plant ages and the three application timings, and reports four protectants through them. Three measurements would turn what it reports into numbers a field could be designed around, and none of the three needs a method we have not already run.

Effective dose

Every protectant on this page went on at a single working dose, so a rescue sets an upper bound on what a plant needs and never locates a minimum. One plate carrying neat, 1:3, 1:10 and 1:30 arms of ACC deaminase, with the B. subtilis TB medium matched at each dilution, gives a half-maximal rescue concentration and settles the confound the hydroponic salinity run carries, where raising the enzyme raised its medium with it. That concentration is what the model needs in place of the 1.67 mL per three sprays it scales to a hectare today.

Independent replicates

A condition held on one plate is one plate: the seedlings on it share a medium, a position in the growth chamber and any contamination that arrives, so they are sub-samples of a single unit. Three plates per condition, with the plate as the unit of analysis, is what would put the significance brackets on this page on the footing their stars imply, and it costs plate space and nothing else.

Timing across stresses

Pre-treatment leads the agar salinity sets, while post-treatment leads the second heat trial by 14% of the chlorophyll the heat-stressed control held, and the first heat trial ranks them the other way. Running the three timings again on one stress at a time, replicated and on uncontaminated plates, is what separates a real difference between salt and heat from the scatter of two single trials. The answer sets the forecast horizon the bioreactor runs against, so it is the measurement the rest of ReLEAF leans on hardest.

What the three platforms amount to is a working screen with its protocol written down: the stress levels and the plant ages that give a readable phenotype, the application methods each format allows, and the readouts to take on the harvest day. Another team can run a protectant of their own through it and compare the result with the four on this page.
