Plants

Abstract

Salinity and heat stress reduce crop productivity, so we built a plant assay that tests whether a protectant helps Arabidopsis thaliana withstand these stresses, and when it has to be applied.

We grew Arabidopsis on agar, in hydroponics and in soil, and applied each protectant one day before the stress (pre-treatment), together with it (co-treatment) or one day after it (post-treatment). Salinity screening set the stress level: primary root length fell from 74.95 to 1.36 mm between 0 and 150 mM NaCl, and 100 mM became our working concentration. Under 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM trehalose raised chlorophyll content about 8.7-fold, and post-treatment with the commercial CH Bio peptide retained the most chlorophyll. Our own protectants gave first signals: pre-treatment ACC deaminase held about 70% more chlorophyll than the untreated control under 100 mM NaCl, while post-treatment led under 37 °C heat (14% above the heat-stressed control) and in soil at 300 mM NaCl (13% above the control). In hydroponics, BoPEP4 at 1/40 kept seedlings green under 100 mM NaCl. Each condition was one plate, so these results are preliminary; they suggest that the best application timing depends on the stress.

Overview and design

We cultivated Arabidopsis thaliana using three experimental systems, agar plates, hydroponic boxes and soil pots, to evaluate the effects of our bioprotectants under different growing conditions. Each system offered a different balance between experimental control and environmental realism: agar plates provided highly controlled conditions, soil more closely represented natural growing environments, and hydroponics offered an intermediate approach.

All three systems followed the same six-step experimental framework, with adjustments to the cultivation setup, bioprotectant application methods, and measurable plant responses according to the characteristics of each system.

Stratify

To synchronize the germination of Arabidopsis thaliana seedlings and isolate the phenotypic effects of the stressor from baseline developmental variations, we did 4 days of stratification at 4 °C, which ensures homogeneous growth of the plants. Seed stratification suppresses the master dormancy gene DELAY OF GERMINATION 1 (DOG1) and its downstream regulators. This overcomes the genetic brakes holding back germination, shifting the hormonal balance from abscisic acid (ABA) to gibberellins (GA) and causing the enzymatic weakening of the seed coat, which allows the radicle to emerge [12].

Grow

We moved seedlings from the 4 °C refrigerator to grow in the 22 °C, 60% RH growth chamber (the day of transfer is counted as day 0 for salinity agar). Newly germinated Arabidopsis seedlings have fragile cotyledons and underdeveloped root systems that cannot immediately withstand harsh stress conditions. Pre-growing them for a set time under a controlled environment allows the greening of cotyledons, the establishment of true leaves, and the elongation of the primary root system.

Agar

Why agar

Agar trials were conducted to fine-tune protectant treatments and NaCl stress within a controlled laboratory environment, ensuring uniform nutrient availability and precise stress exposure. This setup enabled the evaluation and screening of protectant efficacy, helping identify top performing protectant candidates while establishing clear physiological baselines prior to testing in complex soil systems. Furthermore, agar media provide essential nutrients and an accelerated timeframe for observing early physiological changes in Arabidopsis seedlings, facilitating rapid and high-throughput testing across subsequent experimental iterations.

Targeting heat stress, we took inspiration from Auverlot et al. [3] for prolonged thermo priming assays protocol designing. Initially, seeds are germinated and grown on Murashige and Skoog (MS) agar plates at 22 °C. Once the seedlings are 10 days old, they’re held at 37 °C for 5 days to induce acquired thermotolerance prior to subsequent higher stress damage, and then returned to 22 °C for 5 days of recovery. Following recovery, seedling survival and physiological damage are assessed and analyzed via multiple quantitative readouts: fresh weight, chlorophyll content, and anthocyanin measurement. Both the agar and soil based thermo priming assays are established based on this foundational framework and methodology, with specific parameters refined to accommodate our experimental setups.

Set design

Initial screening

At the beginning of the plant trials, we established the experimental protocol and assessed the initial response of the plants to NaCl and to different trehalose concentrations: trehalose at 0.125, 0.5 and 1 mM, and NaCl at 50, 100 and 150 mM. We soon found that 150 mM NaCl was too strong, as the plants died quickly under osmotic stress. We also noticed that trehalose at both 0.5 and 1 mM suppressed plant growth. This is supported by research: feeding low amounts of trehalose blocks, or feedback-inhibits, the plant’s endogenous trehalose-6-phosphate phosphatases (TPPs). This triggers hyper-accumulation of trehalose-6-phosphate (T6P), which signals a false sugar abundance, disrupts starch allocation and induces developmental arrest [22]. At higher concentrations, the plant significantly upregulates trehalase, the enzyme that breaks trehalose down into glucose. This detoxifies the excess trehalose and prevents the toxic accumulation of T6P, and the released glucose acts as a metabolizable carbon source that rescues the plant from growth arrest. Concurrently, the intact fraction functions as an osmoprotectant, mitigating salt stress. Going forward, we set our plant experiments at 75 and 100 mM NaCl, with trehalose at 0, 5 and 10 mM.

Treatment timing

From experimental set 7 onwards, we tested protectant effectiveness by application timing. Each experimental set includes a control, a pre-treatment, a co-treatment and a post-treatment group; the pre-treatment and post-treatment groups receive the protectant one day before and one day after the heat or salinity stress, respectively. With this application schedule we can test whether a protectant has to be applied before the stress surge, which is the role of our bioreactor, and whether a stress forecast from an AIoT prediction model and field stress sensors is needed.

Application

Phase 1 · Trehalose + NaCl combinations

In phase 1 of trehalose testing (3 sets of pre-tests and 4 sets of official experiments) fine tuned concentrations of trehalose were added directly into the mixture with agar before autoclaving, meaning each experimental group holds its own trehalose and salinity concentration without any further contact. We selected vertical planting of agar plates to allow clearer and better visions for root length observation and analysis.

On Day 0, sterilized Arabidopsis seeds were stratified on ½ MS neutral vertical agar plates for 4 days at 4 °C, and moved to the incubator for 4 days germination. On day 8, 4 days old Arabidopsis seedlings were transferred to each of the newly made vertical salinity agar plates containing a corresponding mixture of trehalose with NaCl. During the transferring process, we screen for seedlings with similar root lengths and similar growth levels, and place 12 seedlings per salinity plate with each of their tips of the roots onto a straight baseline drawn by a marker. Thus we could immediately start observing the seedling's root length difference and physiological changes after they’re exposed to different concentrations of NaCl and trehalose, evaluating the rescue effectiveness and growth for each combination over a course of 14 days.

Phase 2 · CH Bio peptide + NaCl

Using commercial CH Bio peptide, the primary objective is to evaluate the efficacy of commercial protectants’ effectiveness and identify its best application timing in response to the exact point where salinity stress hits. Therefore, CH Bio peptide application is categorized into 3 treatment timing groups: pre-treatment, co-treatment, and post-treatment. Different treatment timings are essential connecting to our project goal, “to supply protectants at the optimal timing before stress harms the plants”. These 3 treatment timing groups and the control group (no protectant) were each subjected to three NaCl concentrations (0 mM, 75 mM, and 100 mM), giving a total of 12 experimental conditions.

Looking at the entire experiment timeline, on day 0, Arabidopsis seeds were stratified on ½ MS neutral agar for 4 days at 4 °C, then transferred to a growth chamber for 4 days germination. Protectant applications were initiated subsequently starting from Day 8, and administered at 24-hour intervals between each treatment timing. Treatment timings can be considered as another set of days (Day −1: pre-treatment, Day 0: NaCl + co-treatment, Day 1: post-treatment). Thus, the day of salt stress application is Day 0, whereas NaCl (75 mM and 100 mM NaCl) was applied to all corresponding treatment groups, coinciding with the application for the co-treatment group.

On the technical side, we applied the protectant directly onto the surface of the agar by adding 3 mL and then removing 2 mL of excess, to leave 1 mL of protectant. The surface tension of the agar causes uneven distribution of a small volume, so we first added a larger volume to completely submerge the surface and then removed the excess. Furthermore, to prevent sample variation, we added an equal volume of sterile water (1 mL) to every other group to simulate equal liquid effects on the plants.

Phase 3 · Bioreactor-produced protectants + NaCl

Secreted by our engineered B. subtilis, ACC deaminase, LEA14 and BoPEP4 were used as the protectant against salinity stress in Phase 3. Before applying, the protectant was filtered through a 0.22 µm filter. The main difference in this phase’s protectant application is that we utilized a spraying method. We used a sterilized spraying nozzle to dispense 3 sprays of ACC deaminase (ACCD) onto the surface of plants, administered by spraying in 3 equal areas of individual sections (one top left, one top right, and one bottom) through a distance of 10 cm between the nozzle and agar plate.

The overall experimental flow still follows the timeline from phase 2 of CH Bio peptide testing, while we extended the germination time from 4 days to 7 days (this suggestion was provided by Prof. Cheng to use stronger plants for stress testing).

Heat stress · phase 1 · CH Bio peptide at 37 °C

We did not use heat shock, as it is rarely observed in actual field conditions. Instead, thermo-priming for 3 days was used to simulate a slow change of temperature. Vertical plates cannot hold as much liquid protectant on the surface as horizontal plates can, so we used a spray nozzle that dispensed 0.536 mL for every 3 sprays. This way, each plant receives a controlled amount of protectant, kept standardized across plant trials. We allowed a 5-day recovery period before applying the post-treatment protectant.

Heat stress · phase 2 · ACC deaminase at 37 °C

While protectants are usually administered through the roots, ACC deaminase is a larger enzyme and is absorbed through roots with difficulty, so we used a spray bottle, three sprays each time. During our first trial we observed contamination that obstructed our data collection, so we transferred only the clean seedlings from the contaminated plates onto new agar plates and continued with the same method. However, mold still grew extensively on the finished plates. Because the contamination might have come from an unsterilized spray nozzle, we cleaned the nozzles by spraying sterilized water, then 75% ethanol, then sterilized water again.

Hydroponics

Why hydroponics

Abiotic stress, such as salinity stress and heat stress, negatively affects crop productivity. To test the effectiveness of our self-produced protectants under both types of stress, we chose to use a hydroponic system that allows for precise control of environmental factors. Compared with soil-based systems, hydroponics reduces the number of uncontrolled variables, such as differences in soil composition, microbial communities, and water distribution. Therefore, we can establish and maintain consistent experimental conditions by precisely controlling environmental parameters, including pH, electrical conductivity, temperature, and dissolved oxygen. In addition, nutrients and stress treatments can be delivered more uniformly to all plants through the nutrient solution, improving experimental consistency. Because treatments are added directly to the root environment, the hydroponic cultivation system allows us to rapidly observe plant responses to different stress conditions and evaluate the protective effects of our target protectants.

Where our ideas come from

We are inspired by Matthew Gilliham’s hydroponics culture system (Conn et al., 2013) [5]. First, Arabidopsis seeds were sown on 0.6% agar containing black microcentrifuge-tube lids, each with a 3 mm diameter hole punched using a leather punch. Then, the lids containing seeds were placed on a microcentrifuge tube rack with the agar side facing down. Each well was filled with 2.1 mL of ½ MS medium, allowing the agar to remain in contact with the medium throughout stratification and germination. The seeds were stratified at 4 °C in the dark for at least 48 hours, and then transferred to a growth chamber maintained at 22 °C under a 16-hour light/8-hour dark photoperiod. After germination, 4-day-old seedlings were directly transferred onto the floating board or holder and left to grow in the hydroponic box. The tank was kept dark to reduce algal growth and continuously aerated with air stones to maintain sufficient dissolved oxygen. The nutrient solution was regularly replaced every 2–3 days, and the plants were then used for experimental treatments and physiological measurements.

Stress design

To evaluate whether our protectants could reduce abiotic stress in plants, we developed multiple experimental platforms, not only agar systems. We needed a platform in which stress could be easily applied for different durations and the concentration of protectants could be precisely controlled. As a result, we can measure the fresh weight or root length of these treated plants, providing simple and accessible ways to quantify the results. In the beginning, we reviewed related papers to figure out how they established stress protocols on Arabidopsis. Here we focused on heat and salinity stress.

Salinity stress

First, we followed Cortés-Jiménez et al. [8]. This experiment tested six NaCl concentrations, 0, 50, 100, 150, 200 and 250 mM, rescued by Azospirillum brasilense, the most studied plant growth promoting bacteria (PGPB). According to the results, plant stems under 0–100 mM NaCl did better than under 150–250 mM, and best at 50 and 100 mM NaCl. This means that these two salinity concentrations are moderate enough for the plant to be rescued by the bacteria. According to the pretests from agar, Arabidopsis applied with 100 mM NaCl expresses a strong stress response but doesn’t die at this threshold and could be rescued. Therefore, we decided to test 100 mM NaCl in our salinity test. Next, we decided to use CH Bio peptide to evaluate the stress response of plants under 100 mM NaCl. We used CH Bio peptide since it is a commercial product that helps plants resist abiotic stress.

Heat stress

One of our main reference papers on heat stress is Lee et al. [2], which describes how different stages of a plant may have different heat tolerance. This helped us make a quick decision about which developmental stage is most suitable for each condition, as well as the growth medium composition, the timing of stress application, and the days of recovery. This information was particularly important for us because this was our first time cultivating Arabidopsis on the hydroponic platform and our first heat treatment. We therefore designed the system around three variables: plant developmental stage → timing of protectant application → timing and intensity of stress. Integrating all of this information helped us accelerate our experiments and increase the success of protectant testing in the hydroponic system.

Soil

Why soil

Soil trials were done to simulate real field conditions and allow protectants to be validated under complex conditions after testing in controlled environments such as agar and hydroponics. Soil introduces physical matrix interactions, including water retention and pore size distribution, which affect how the NaCl and protectant diffuse and reach the plant roots. Additionally, soil contains native microbial flora, allowing us to validate its real world viability. Hydroponics and agar assays are for baseline fine tuning, protocol simulations, and controlled proof of concept, while demonstrating efficacy in real soil reflects smallholder farming realities.

Two stages of plant growth

After determining the planting protocol, we optimized the plant growth stage by aligning our experiment timeline with the life cycle of Arabidopsis thaliana, because choosing the correct developmental stage is vital for capturing active metabolic and phenotypic responses. Arabidopsis progresses through a rapid life cycle of approximately 6 to 8 weeks, structured into distinct principal growth stages [1]: germination and seedling establishment (stages 0.1–0.7, days 0–7), when the radicle emerges through the seed coat, the cotyledons expand and the first true leaf appears; the rosette growth phase (stages 1.0–1.12, weeks 2–4), with exponential leaf expansion and primary root elongation; the floral transition and bolting (stages 5.0–6.5, weeks 4–5), driven by floral regulators such as LEAFY (LFY) and APETALA1 (AP1); and reproductive senescence (stages 8.0–9.0, weeks 6–8 and later), when resources are reallocated, chlorophyll breaks down and the siliques mature.

We initially conducted salinity assays on 1-month-old plants, at the late rosette to early bolting phase. At this developmental junction, Arabidopsis shifts its resource allocation toward floral meristem development rather than general vegetative homeostasis [15], and the lower leaves begin early senescence, which introduces baseline physiological variability into our assay data. The difference between stressed and unstressed plants may therefore be less obvious, which affects our conclusions on protectant efficacy. Following suggestions from Prof. Cheng, we moved our testing window to 2-week-old plants (stages 1.02–1.04). At 14 days after germination, Arabidopsis is in an active vegetative growth phase, and the absence of floral transition and senescence gives uniform tissue and reproducible experimental responses.

Salt schedule

Salt behaves differently in soil than on agar, so the salinity concentrations and protectant doses had to be re-evaluated to maximize the observable differences in rescue effectiveness. Evaluating the protective efficacy of ACC deaminase requires a soil salinity model that induces measurable stress without causing immediate, lethal tissue necrosis. We evaluated two established soil salinization protocols on 2-week-old Arabidopsis thaliana in growth chambers: a top-down wash method [7], with direct surface application of 100, 200 and 300 mM NaCl until it washes through, and a sub-irrigation accumulation method [6], with successive doses of 100 mM NaCl in the bottom trays, drawn upward into the root zone by capillary action. In earlier trials, a gradual ramp of 25 mM NaCl per day up to 100 mM, adapted from Dr. Verslues’ suggestions, produced minimal phenotypic damage and did not separate the control from the ACC deaminase groups. As Prof. Cheng noted, sub-irrigation accumulation was also insufficiently stressful, because plain-water irrigation every 2 days flushed the accumulated ions out of the active root zone.

To resolve these problems, we developed a sequential top-drenching protocol for evaluating the three protectant timings (pre-treatment, co-treatment and post-treatment). Plants receive equal volumes (30 to 50 mL per pot) of 100, then 200, and finally 300 mM NaCl, with no freshwater irrigation in between to prevent salt leaching. Runoff at the base of each pot confirms that the root system is fully saturated, and each tray reservoir is kept 1 to 2 cm deep, holding 200 to 400 mL, so that the root zone stays in equilibrium with the salt solution. Instead of following a fixed calendar, the protocol repeats 300 mM NaCl until clear phenotypic markers appear.

Heat schedule

To evaluate the thermal protective capacity of ACC deaminase, we adapted established thermo-priming frameworks to our soil platform, building on the heat stress assays of Auverlot et al. [3] and following the same method as our agar system. In the agar trials, 10-day-old seedlings were exposed to 37 °C for 5 days to induce acquired thermotolerance and sustained heat stress, followed by 5 days of recovery at 22 °C before survival, fresh weight, chlorophyll retention and anthocyanin accumulation were measured. Translating this framework to soil required adapting both the incubation parameters and the treatment schedule: two-week-old plants were placed in a 37 °C incubator for 5 days of heat stress, followed by 5 days of recovery in a 22 to 25 °C growth chamber.

In farming environments, abiotic stresses such as high salinity and extreme heat rarely occur predictably; they arrive as dynamic, fluctuating events. By testing three intervention timings, we simulate how farmers might apply microbial inoculants across different phases of the stress.

  • Pre-treatment simulates applying bio-inoculants or seed coatings before an anticipated stress, for instance when a farmer sees a heatwave in the seasonal forecast. It allows beneficial bacteria to colonize the rhizosphere and prime the plant’s defenses before cellular damage occurs.
  • Co-treatment simulates application at the moment a stressor arrives, such as sudden soil salinization after flooding or fertilizer runoff. It tests whether the protectant can intercept stress signals immediately: supplied together with NaCl or heat, ACC deaminase cleaves the ethylene precursor ACC as the stress develops, limiting root growth arrest and photosynthetic decline.
  • Post-treatment simulates a rescue application after an extreme event, such as spraying crops after a heatwave or salt-water intrusion, and tests the restorative capacity of the protectant. In our thermo-priming assays, post-treatment was applied one day after the plants were moved from the 37 °C incubator to the 22 °C growth chamber.

Soil · 6 September · 2-week-old plants

Establish the appropriate stress levels and treatment method

We directly compared the sub-irrigation accumulation method [6] with the top-down wash (drench) method [7] on 2-week-old Arabidopsis thaliana. Seeds were stratified in 1 mL of sterile water for 4 days and dispersed evenly into soil pots with a micropipette, followed by selection for uniform rosette development. The accumulation method applied sequential sub-irrigation doses of 100 mM NaCl by tray capillary action, with plain-water irrigation between doses. The wash method applied sequential surface drenches of 100, 200 and 300 mM NaCl (30 to 50 mL per pot) without plain water until the pots were fully saturated and the root zone had equilibrated. Phenotype, fresh weight and chlorophyll were measured at the end of the stress cycle.

The sub-irrigation accumulation method did not induce sufficient osmotic stress, giving lush rosettes and a high average fresh weight of approximately 0.42 g, because the plain water between doses flushed accumulated ions out of the active root zone. In contrast, Prof. Cheng’s top-down drench gave a fresh weight of 0.15 g, a reduction of approximately 64%, which shows that top-down drenching impairs cell expansion and tissue water accumulation. The drench protocol produced clear, reproducible growth inhibition and salt stress symptoms without immediate lethal necrosis, which makes it the better method for high-concentration soil screening despite the lower baseline biomass. Both protocols gave comparable volumetric chlorophyll concentrations near 1.0 mg/L: under the milder accumulation method, pigment density stays high because chloroplast development is uninhibited, while under the wash method the smaller rosettes concentrate the remaining pigment.

Implications for ACC deaminase testing

The choice of salinity treatment is particularly important for evaluating protection by ACC deaminase. Under salinity stress, plants accumulate 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylic acid (ACC), the precursor of the plant hormone ethylene. Although ethylene has important roles in normal development and stress responses, excessive stress-induced ethylene can inhibit root elongation and plant growth. Orozco-Mosqueda et al. [21] reviewed evidence that plant-growth-promoting bacteria with ACC deaminase degrade ACC into ammonia and α-ketobutyrate, reducing excess ethylene and helping plants maintain growth under saline conditions, which supports using biomass and growth traits to study ACC deaminase. A salinity treatment that causes little growth inhibition makes it hard to distinguish a protective effect from normal growth, while one that causes extensive tissue damage leaves too little viable growth to measure a rescue. The lower mean fresh weight under the top-down drench therefore makes it a promising method for ACC deaminase screening, provided the plants remain viable and the response is reproducible across biological replicates.

Soil · 12 September · 2-week-old plants

Execution of salinity trials: ACCD salinity

Following optimization of the salinity application method, we compared three ACC deaminase timings, pre-treatment, co-treatment and post-treatment, under a NaCl wash of 100, then 200, and finally 300 mM NaCl.

In this preliminary soil assay, pre-treatment and co-treatment did not visibly prevent leaf bleaching, while post-treatment showed a limited effect on chlorosis. Without fresh-weight normalization, the measured extract concentrations were about 1.00 mg/L for the untreated control, 0.67 for pre-treatment, 0.30 for co-treatment and 1.13 for post-treatment. Post-treatment, applied one day after 300 mM NaCl, gave the highest unnormalized chlorophyll concentration, about 13% above the untreated control, suggesting that ACC deaminase applied later may still influence chlorophyll after severe salinity stress has begun. Further experiments are needed to confirm this response.

Under our conditions, 100 mM NaCl was not enough to induce clear visible stress, whereas symptoms became apparent at 300 mM, so 300 mM became our soil stress baseline. Because post-treatment was applied one day after the first 300 mM NaCl exposure, it can be seen as a delayed co-treatment given once salinity stress had developed, while the co-treatment groups were dosed while the stress was still mild. The detectability of a protective effect therefore depends on the severity of the stress: under insufficient stress, protected and unprotected plants are hard to tell apart, and under severe stress the tissue damage may exceed the protection the treatment provides.

Dry lab collaborations

Our collaboration with the dry lab began with two practical questions: how much protectant would be required to improve plant performance, and how early would it need to be applied? Our plant data enter the Math Model at three points.

How much protectant

Our original approach was to build a dose-response curve from protectant concentration and a rescue index. Trehalose first seemed suitable because we had run six sets, but in set 3 two trials of the same design gave opposing trends, and each condition was a single plate, so its seedlings were not independent replicates. We removed trehalose from the circuit design: a model should represent what the experiments can support, not force uncertain data into a quantitative relationship.

The model now scales the dose we sprayed. Three sprays delivered 1.67 mL onto a 10 cm plate (78.5 cm²), the same rate as 2,126 L per hectare; with a modelled rice canopy correction and our secreted activity of 7.25 mU mL−1, this becomes 37.6 kU of protectant per hectare per application. It is the dose that worked, not the smallest that works, because our sets varied salt level and timing but not the applied amount.

When it must arrive

A weather forecast reaches about three days ahead, but ten 20-litre reactors need 5.4 days to make one hectare’s application. The reactor therefore has to produce through the season, and the forecast decides when the stock is used; if the protectant must arrive a day before the stress, production has to start 6.4 days ahead.

Our timing runs do not agree. On agar at 75 mM NaCl, pre-treatment gave the highest fresh weight (0.164 g against 0.144 g for co-treatment). In soil at 300 mM, co-treatment led on chlorophyll per gram, but its tubes held 22 to 199 mg of tissue and chlorophyll per gram tracked tissue weight (r = −0.85); read as total chlorophyll, co-treatment came last. No run bounds how much yield the protectant recovers, so the model carries efficacy as an axis instead of a fixed value.

Scoring a plant

Root length tracks growth inhibition, fresh weight tracks accumulated biomass, and chlorophyll reflects the condition of the photosynthetic tissue. We explored a stress index that compares each measurement with the unstressed control from the same experiment, so that a value near 1 is the matched control and a value approaching 0 is severe impairment.

The index also made data quality a central concern. Normalizing chlorophyll by fresh weight can make severely stressed plants appear to have more chlorophyll because their biomass is lower, so we keep the original measurements beside the index.

The updated model

The updated model goes beyond a protectant concentration. It fits a stress index to 25 years of Taiwan’s county rice harvests, which carries 32% less county error than a no-weather trend in the second crop, sets the lamp level for the light-controlled production system, and turns our plate dose into equipment: 81 litres of vessel and about US$3,800 of capital per hectare.

Two of its inputs come from our plates: the salt calibration of set 6 (74.95 mm without salt, 34.77 mm at 75 mM, 11.00 mm at 100 mM and 1.36 mm at 150 mM), which separates moderate stress from arrested growth, and the applied dose. The secreted activity rests on one DNPH assay below the quantification limit, and two readings put the apparent half-time of the protectant at 0.7 to 1.5 hours, so the production and application figures are conditional projections, not validated capacities.

Together, the plant and dry lab work changed our objective from fitting a curve to our existing data to identifying the measurements a reliable model needs: independent replicates, a consistent treatment protocol, appropriate controls, and a dose-response series for ACC deaminase.
