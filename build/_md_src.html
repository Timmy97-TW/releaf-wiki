Molecular Dynamics

The contact everything is measured against. Asn23, the last residue of BoPEP4, buried at the closed end of the PEPR1 groove where its free carboxylate reaches Arg487. Seven of the nine runs are, in the end, a question about whether that one bond holds.

Docking scores a pose. Molecular dynamics asks whether the pose survives being shaken. Nine all-atom trajectories were run on the PEPR1 ectodomain, eight of them carrying a peptide. Each chapter below is one question the set answers, and each links to the full report for the runs it rests on.

Introduction

Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulation is a fundamental computational methodology that uses statistical mechanics to evaluate the physical properties, structural stability, and mechanism of action of engineered proteins and peptides. By integrating Newtonian equations of motion, MD tracks the velocities, positions, and interactions between molecules over time. We applied this to our project to understand the translation and secretion efficiency of our protectants by analyzing the interactions between different receptors.

With HADDOCK providing a structural framework for how our protectants bind to specific molecules, Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulations offer a glimpse into whether these predicted bound poses remain stable over time. Rather than treating a single docking pose as a definitive representation of the binding interaction, MD subjects the complex to a simulated dynamic environment, allowing the protein and protectant to move and interact as they would under molecular conditions. While MD runs can answer crucial hypotheses, they can also serve as trigger points for unique, project-altering strategies.

How the nine runs were set upForce field, water model, time step, what was held identical

Every system was built on the PEPR1 ectodomain, residues 29 to 738, taken from PDB 5GR8 and solvated in explicit OPC water with AMBER ff19SB [1][2], about 127,600 atoms per system. Hydrogen mass repartitioning allows a 4 fs time step. Each run is 30 ns of production with one random seed, 1,500 saved frames, 270 ns and 25.9 GPU-hours over the set.

Four things change across the nine runs and nothing else does: which ligand is in the groove, which residues of it are present, whether the C-terminus is free, and whether three histidines are protonated. The numbers on every run come from one analysis codebase applied to all nine trajectories, so a column means the same thing from row to row, which is what makes the cross-run reading in the last chapter possible at all. The protocol in full, with the equilibration schedule, is on MD Simulations.

The runs

Six chapters. The first is the reference every other run is read against; the last is what only the whole set can say.

Run 1 of 9

Wild type

Full-length BoPEP4, residues 1 to 23, with a free C-terminus, docked in the PEPR1 groove. Nothing is being tested here. This is the run every other number on the page is read against, and it is also where two earlier claims were checked and one of them was withdrawn.

Wild-type

The wild-type run served as a comparison and analysis benchmark for the other mutations. We found that the eight N-terminal residues had an average fluctuation above 2 Å. This was consistent with the alanine scan results, which found that mutations to the first six residues had the least penalty in the peptide-receptor docking, indicating a lower contribution to peptide-receptor interaction. All residues past Pro10 had over 75% contact with the receptor, except for Pro19. All residues past Arg11 had under 1 Å fluctuation, except for Lys18. Our conserved window 15-23, defined by aligning sequences from the PEP receptor family, had near 100% contact with mostly sub-Ångström movements. This evidence supports that the N- and C-termini have different behaviours and roles as the N-terminus has greater conformational freedom, while the conserved C-terminus region remains stabilized in contact with the receptor. The conserved residues also form the primary region of the peptide that interacts with the binding pocket, highlighting their importance.

The run also supported our decision to use truncations instead of point mutations. Arg9 has a fluctuation of 1.32 Å and a mean closest approach of 2.79 Å and 100% contact, which explains the sharp jump in the HADDOCK score when removing Arg9. Although Ile2 and Ile4 have contact with the receptor, they have a fluctuation of over 1.5 Å and switch between different locations on the PEPR1 instead of maintaining contact. Adding these residues also necessitates preserving the surrounding disordered residues, which have only about 25% contact with the receptor. The improved binding from adding these residues was not worth the extra sequence length, so we did not pursue the Δ1 and Δ1-3 designs and instead opted for the truncation designs with residues 7 and 9.

Reassessing Arg18 contact network

A second correction emerged from the same analysis. Although smaller in scope, it affects the rationale behind one of our engineering decisions and is therefore important to document. The literature assigns two receptor contacts to Arg18 in AtPEP1: one with Asp348 and another with Phe371. BoPEP4 contains lysine at the corresponding position, and our initial docking poses suggested that Lys18 could already reproduce both interactions at approximately crystal-like distances. This observation contributed to our rationale for K18R, which we initially described as restoring an interaction that was already largely established.

The MD trajectory does not support that interpretation based on distance and occupancy. Asp348 is clearly the primary receptor partner of Lys18, with the next-closest peptide residue remaining 7.87 Å away. However, the Lys18-Asp348 interaction is substantially less stable than our static docking pose suggested. The contact is present in 53.7% of frames and spans distances from 2.55 to 9.37 Å. By comparison, the Asn23-Arg487 interaction is maintained at 100% occupancy within a 0.54 Å distance window. Thus, while Lys18 does interact with Asp348, this interaction behaves as a transient contact rather than a persistent anchor.

The Phe371 assignment requires a clearer correction. In the MD trajectory, Phe371 most frequently contacts Gly20, which remains within 3.69 Å for 84.4% of frames. Lys18, by contrast, has an average distance of 5.12 Å from Phe371 and approaches within 4 Å in only 18.1% of frames. The residue we had assigned to the Phe371 contact in the static model is therefore not the residue that predominantly maintains that interaction during the simulation.

These results do not invalidate the chemical rationale for K18R. Lysine has a shorter side chain than arginine and cannot reproduce the bidentate interaction expected from the longer guanidinium group of arginine. What the MD analysis removes is the supporting claim that the native contact was already largely established before substitution. The K18R design therefore remains plausible, but its rationale is narrower: the substitution is intended to introduce an arginine side chain capable of forming the proposed bidentate interaction, rather than simply completing an interaction that is already present in BoPEP4.

Whether K18R actually establishes this bidentate geometry remains untested and requires a dedicated simulation. Interestingly, the effect is context-dependent: in the nine-residue construct, the Lys18-Asp348 interaction increases in occupancy from 53.7% to 90.1%. This indicates that the interaction itself is sensitive to the surrounding peptide sequence. Shortening the peptide appears to stabilize the Lys18-Asp348 contact, providing additional support for the importance of considering the full peptide context when interpreting individual docking interactions.

Overall, the MD analysis refines the K18R design because the substitution remains justified by the distinct interaction geometry available to arginine. However, we no longer characterize it as simply restoring a contact that is already nearly formed in the wild-type BoPEP4 sequence.

Runs 3, 4 and 5

Truncation ladder

Three cuts from the docking ladder, taken into simulation: 7-23, 9-23 and 15-23. The construct that was ordered is the one that came back worst, and the explanation is a salt bridge that was on nobody's list.

Unidentified salt bridge

We modeled the 9-23 construct as part of the truncation series, and it proved to be the least stable untagged construct. Clamp integrity was 77.1%, the bidentate salt bridge was present in 46.9% of frames, and the Asn23-Arg487 interaction remained within the defined distance in 81.1% of frames. The conserved window formed 68.5 contacts compared with 96.5 in wild type, while backbone RMSD reached 2.28 Å, the highest among the BoPEP4 trajectories. Although 9-23 passed the acceptance gate, every contact-based measure was the lowest among the untagged constructs simulated at pH 7. Moreover, the bidentate salt bridge did not dissociate as a single event but instead underwent repeated transient dissociation, comprising 94 separate break events that collectively accounted for 22.9% of the trajectory, none exceeding 0.68 ns in duration. Contacts involving Asp348 and Phe371 also transferred repeatedly between peptide residues. Together these observations indicate that the interface remained engaged but was substantially more dynamic than in wild type.

We initially attributed this instability to the new N terminus created by removing residues 1-8, which makes Arg9 terminal and introduces a positively charged α-ammonium group. To test this explanation, we modeled 7-23, shifting the free terminus to Lys7 while retaining Arg9 as an internal residue. The stability of the interface was substantially restored: clamp integrity increased from 77.1% to 98.5%, Asn23-Arg487 occupancy from 81.1% to 99.9%, and bidentate salt-bridge occupancy from 46.9% to 87.7%, while the shared 9-23 window, which comprises the same fifteen residues in both constructs, gained 23 contacts, increasing from 128.7 to 151.3. However, the trajectories did not support the proposed α-ammonium interaction as the cause. The Lys7 α-amino group in 7-23 was 7.11 Å from its nearest receptor carboxylate and within 4 Å in 0.0% of frames; similarly, the Arg9 terminus in 9-23 was within 4 Å in only 18.5% of frames. Full-length BoPEP4 and the AtPEP1 benchmark showed similarly weak terminal interactions.

Instead, the truncation ladder revealed a previously unlisted N-terminal anchor: the Lys7 side chain formed a salt bridge with Asp129 at 2.81 Å in 98.7% of frames, even when Lys7 was the terminal residue. Lys8 contributed to this interaction network in only 4.1% of frames, while Arg9 maintained its interaction with Asp179 in 100% of frames. Thus, BoPEP4 contains two N-terminal salt bridges absent from the published hotspot list, and the instability of 9-23 is better explained by removal of the Lys7-Asp129 anchor while retaining residues 9-14 than by formation of a new terminal salt bridge. This also explains why the truncation ladder is not simply a case of shorter peptides being less stable: full-length, 7-23, and 15-23 showed clamp integrity of 100.0%, 98.5%, and 99.9%, respectively, whereas only the intermediate 9-23 construct dropped to 77.1%. A direct test of this interpretation would therefore be a K7A full-length mutant, which should disrupt the same anchor without altering the peptide termini.

The 7-23 construct was built to test a hypothesis that failed. What it gave instead was the one peptide in the set that can be compared with the crystal structure residue for residue.

7-23 as a benchmark comparison

The 7-23 construct also removes an important ambiguity in comparison with the AtPEP1 crystal structure, which spans residues 7-23 and therefore has the same terminal configuration as BoPEP4 7-23. Across the two peptides, 15 of 17 positions showed closely matching contact occupancies, while the main sequence-level difference among the published hotspots was Arg18 in AtPEP1 versus Lys18 in BoPEP4, providing the structural basis for the proposed K18R substitution. The N-terminal comparison also revealed a positional difference, in that each peptide anchors this end through a single residue, but at positions one apart. AtPEP1 employs Gln8-Tyr153 at 3.41 Å with 95.8% occupancy, with Lys7 contributing only 19.0%, whereas BoPEP4 employs Lys7-Asp129 at 2.81 Å with 98.7% occupancy, with Lys8 contributing only 4.1%. Because both peptides now carry a free terminus at position 7, this difference can be attributed to differences in the interaction network rather than to the confounding effect of one peptide presenting an internal residue where the other presents a chain end. The same comparison applies directly to Lys7, which reaches 99.9% occupancy in BoPEP4 compared with 23.5% in AtPEP1. Although 7-23 closely reproduced the full-length binding architecture, it was not identical, with the Asn465 hydrogen bond occurring in 62.9% rather than 99.8% of frames and the bidentate salt bridge in 87.7% rather than 100%.

Negative control

The 15-23 construct was intended to provide a negative control by removing the upstream sequence required for stable receptor engagement; however, it remained highly organized rather than dissociating. Clamp integrity was 99.9%, bidentate salt-bridge occupancy was 97.2%, and backbone RMSD was 1.81 Å, while the remaining nine residues formed 89.6 contacts compared with 96.5 contacts made by those same residues in wild type, retaining approximately 93% of the original contact network despite removal of 14 residues. Because peptide dissociation from an LRR ectodomain can occur on microsecond-to-millisecond timescales, substantially beyond the 30 ns equilibrium trajectories used here, failure to observe dissociation cannot establish that the peptide would remain bound indefinitely; nevertheless, the trajectory demonstrates that the conserved C-terminal region can independently maintain the binding register for the duration of the simulation.

Therefore, the truncation series supports a model in which the conserved C-terminal region functions as the core structural anchor, while the upstream sequence contributes additional interactions that modulate overall stability. The 9-23 construct is specifically destabilized because it removes the Lys7-Asp129 anchor while retaining an upstream segment that still participates in the binding interface, whereas the 15-23 construct removes that segment entirely and remains organized through the conserved C-terminal interactions. Rather than indicating a simple dependence of binding stability on peptide length, the ladder therefore identifies a specific N-terminal interaction network and provides a testable prediction for the role of Lys7.

Run 6 of 9

The histidine tag

The construct that was already ordered, simulated to check an assumption nobody had checked. It passed every acceptance criterion the project had written, and it should not have.

Why Asn23

In 5GR8, AtPEP1's C-terminal Asn23 makes four contacts with PEPR1: a salt bridge from its free carboxylate to Arg487, hydrogen bonds to Asn465 and Asp441, and a van der Waals contact with Ile443. BoPEP4 shares that residue, and our threaded model placed it in the same pocket. The run was pre-registered to test whether it stays, with a tolerance of ±1.5 Å on each crystal distance set before the trajectory started.

This finding is important, as it defines an amino acid with four contacts that future runs should look out for to better display docking, acting as a benchmark for binding strength. Furthermore, Tang et al. (2015) showed that removing Asn23 nearly abolished PEPR1 binding, making the terminal Asn particularly important. The identification of four contacts allows us to see for ourselves how much the binding of a peptide is disturbed through mutations by observing the clamp integrity of the four contacts.

The expectation going in, in the team’s own words, was this:

Asn23 anchors the complex mainly through its free C-terminal carboxylate, and that group exists only at the end of a chain. Appending any residue consumes it into a neutral peptide bond, so the anionic charge Arg487 pairs with disappears. This follows from the chemistry rather than from the length or flexibility of the tag. The contact is held at 100% occupancy inside a 0.54 Å window, which is the behaviour of a specificity determinant with no geometric slack to absorb a perturbation. Asn23 sits buried at the end of the groove against Arg487, Asn465, Asp441 and Ile443, with no channel for extra residues to thread out through, making the tag a steric problem alongside an electrostatic one.

Half of that survived the run. The electrostatic half did; the steric half did not, and the cards below are where the team says so.

Asn23-Arg487 salt bridge

Our expression constructs place a 6×His tag on Asn23, which lies in the middle of our proposed PEPR1 binding model. Since the behaviour of Asn23 is so important for its interaction with the receptor, we first investigated whether adding the tag disturbed its local binding interaction. In the crystal structure, Asn23 has four interactions with PEPR1: a salt bridge between its terminal carboxylate and Arg487, hydrogen bonds with Asn465 and Asp441, and a van der Waals interaction with Ile443. Tang et al. had previously shown that deleting Asn23 reduced PEPR1 binding almost to zero, confirming its importance. We thus expected the His tag to perturb this interaction and set a pre-registered tolerance of ±1.5 Å for each of the distances in the crystal structure.

Interestingly, the tagged construct appeared to pass our criteria: the distance between Asn23 and Arg487 was under 4 Å 99.8% of the time in the 1,500 frame trajectory and the clamp was maintained 99.7% of the frames. We also checked that the RMSD criteria were passed: we found an RMSD of 1.12 Å for the conserved window backbone, which was the lowest RMSD of any of our runs and even lower than the 1.17 Å RMSD of the AtPEP1 crystal control. By our criteria alone, there was no difference between the tagged and wild-type constructs.

This was, however, an artefact of our original analysis, not preservation of the native interaction. Adding six histidines to Asn23 changes it from a terminal to an internal residue. Its terminal carboxylate is now used in the peptide bond with His24 and the tagged construct only has one carbonyl oxygen compared to the two oxygens of the wild-type carboxylate. Our original criteria had measured the distance of either oxygen to Arg487, without considering the chemical environment. Thus, in the tagged trajectory it was able to count the approach of a neutral amide carbonyl as equivalent to the charged carboxylate of wild-type.

We thus repeated the analysis with atom-resolved and angle-aware criteria that specifically identified the native salt bridge geometry. The runs were now very different. With the definition of a bidentate salt bridge as the peptide having both oxygens interacting with the Arg487 guanidinium group simultaneously under angle-aware criteria, the salt bridge was present in 100.0% of wild-type frames and 0.0% of tagged frames. Using a distance-only definition of the same interaction, the tagged construct has only 2.4% occupancy. This difference indicates how much the results depend on the definition of our criterion. Similarly, the runs have 99.8% and 0% frames with two or more hydrogen bonds to Arg487. The peptide carboxylate was in the first solvation shell of the Arg487 guanidinium group 100% of the time in wild-type frames compared to 0% of the tagged frames. The closest the carboxylate came in the tagged trajectory was 11.65 Å and the distance between the charge centres increased from 3.98 Å to 20.07 Å. The AtPEP1 control also showed the same behaviour as the wild-type in these measures. Importantly, Arg487 did not form an equivalent salt bridge with another receptor carboxylate. The negative charge of the peptide was effectively removed from the interaction site instead of being replaced.

The tagged peptide still had good structural ordering. The six histidines assumed conformations against the receptor surface and made roughly 50 more heavy atom contacts. These non-native contacts can compensate for the loss of the native salt bridge in geometric and contact-based metrics such as RMSD. In other words, a new surface adsorption interaction can maintain overall structural integrity without replacing the chemical interaction that was native to binding.

An independent apo control also helped to confirm that the tag affected the local receptor environment. In seven out of eight runs, Phe510 was the closest receptor neighbour to Arg487. In the tagged run, this changed to Ile463 72.1% of the time, and the Arg487 site was the least dense of the eight trajectories. This indicates that the tag does not simply replace the native peptide in the binding environment, but causes a local change to the pocket.

These runs demonstrate the difference between whether the peptide is physically interacting with the receptor and whether it has the native chemistry to do so. The tagged peptide does not get ejected from the binding groove in the 30 ns trajectory and the eight hotspots all have the same closest peptide residue. However, the chemical interaction that holds the peptide in place is greatly changed. Our original concern about the tag had two parts: first, that the pocket would not accommodate the extra residues, and second, that we would lose the Asn23 carboxylate. The simulations supported the second concern but not the proposed steric mechanism. Clearly, the histidines found space on the receptor surface, and the loss of the negatively charged terminal group was seen in our atom-resolved criterion. This also highlights the need for caution when using geometric metrics such as distance and contacts in molecular dynamics, as two interactions with the same geometry do not necessarily make the same chemical and energetic contribution. The neutral amide carbonyl can have a distance trace like the charged carboxylate without having the electrostatic interaction that stabilises the native complex.

Our conclusion is thus more restricted than our original hypothesis. We cannot say that the His tag ablates receptor interaction, and thus a negative experimental assay with the tagged construct could not necessarily be attributed to steric clash with the tag. However, our simulations do show that the tag ablates the native Asn23-Arg487 salt bridge. We cannot, however, characterise the energetic cost of removing this formal negative charge from a residue proven to be so close to essential in binding; 30 ns of preserved geometry is not the same as preserved binding affinity.

What follows for the construct

The design consequence is thus clear: the N-terminus is not only the more flexible end of the peptide, but also the end that our truncation series shows can be removed. Thus, placing a tag on this end gives extra sequence space while not altering the Asn23 binding terminus. Alternatively, synthetic peptides can be made without a tag. Both strategies keep Asn23 a true chain terminus and avoid the ambiguity of the original construct.

One caveat unproved is that our simulations ran the six histidines in their neutral form at pH 7. The plant apoplast is closer to pH 5.5 where histidine would be mostly protonated. Thus, we have not characterised the effect that the protonation state of the tag has on its interactions with PEPR1 and the rest of the binding pocket.

Runs 7 and 8

pH 5.5

The peptide does its work outside the plant cell, where the pH is nearer 5.5 than 7. Two runs test what that changes. On the full-length peptide it recovers a contact; on the truncated one it breaks the anchor.

pH run

Because conventional molecular dynamics does not dynamically simulate pH, our pH 5.5 condition represents a fixed protonation state intended to approximate the apoplast rather than a constant-pH simulation. Histidine protonation was assigned using model pKa values, with His14 and His22 on BoPEP4 and His227 on PEPR1 carrying positive charges, while all other histidines remained neutral and all Asp and Glu residues remained deprotonated throughout the 30 ns trajectory. No other simulation parameter was changed relative to the wild-type pH 7 run, making the three protonation changes the sole variable in this comparison.

Under this condition, the interaction between peptide Glu12 and receptor His227 was substantially strengthened, with the pair separated by 2.75 ± 0.11 Å in 99.9% of frames compared with 5.33 Å and only 0.8% occupancy at pH 7. This interaction is already present in the AtPEP1 benchmark at neutral pH, where the corresponding pair occurs at 3.36 Å with 76.9% occupancy, indicating that protonation allows BoPEP4 to recover a contact present in its structural benchmark rather than introducing an entirely new interaction. Importantly, this improvement did not compromise the primary C-terminal anchor: clamp integrity remained at 100.0%, the bidentate salt bridge at 99.9%, and the conserved window formed 95.7 contacts with a backbone RMSD of 1.34 Å, the lowest value observed among the untagged constructs that satisfied their acceptance criteria.

This result therefore changes the interpretation of the original sequence difference at Glu12-His227. The lower contact occupancy observed at pH 7 had initially appeared to reflect a sequence-level deficiency in BoPEP4, but the pH-controlled comparison indicates that it was instead a consequence of the protonation state used in the simulation. Because the apoplast is the environment in which the engineered protectant is expected to function, pH 5.5 is therefore a more physiologically relevant default for full-length BoPEP4, with pH 7 retained as the control. At the same time, the trajectory does not support the proposed His14 pH-switch mechanism: His14 remained approximately 6.1 Å from the nearest receptor carboxylate and formed a sub-4 Å contact in 0.0% of frames at either condition. The pH-responsive interaction identified here is instead the Glu12-His227 pair.

Protonation helped the full-length peptide. The obvious next question was whether it could rescue the construct that had come back worst.

Truncation crossed with protonation

Because pH 5.5 substantially improved the full-length BoPEP4 interaction network while 9-23 was the least stable untagged construct, we next asked whether protonation could restore the instability observed in that truncation. We therefore repeated the 9-23 simulation from the same starting coordinates while protonating the same three histidines and changing no other simulation parameters.

Contrary to the full-length result, protonation further destabilized the 9-23 construct. Asn23-Arg487 remained within 4 Å in only 23.6% of frames, well below the 60% acceptance threshold, while clamp integrity fell to 19.3% and bidentate salt-bridge occupancy to 6.9%, making this the only BoPEP4 trajectory that failed its pre-registered acceptance criteria. The underlying mechanism was a new intramolecular interaction between protonated His22 and the C-terminal carboxylate: this contact occurred at 2.84 Å in 96.9% of frames in 9-23 at pH 5.5, compared with 17.4% at pH 7. For comparison, the corresponding interaction occurred in only 1.1% of full-length frames at pH 7 and increased to 64.7% at pH 5.5 while the full-length clamp remained intact.

The combined effect of truncation and protonation is therefore important. Neither condition alone was sufficient to produce the strong self-contact observed in the failed trajectory: truncation increased occupancy from 1.1% to 17.4% at neutral pH, while protonation increased full-length occupancy to 64.7%; only their combination produced the 96.9% occupancy that coincided with collapse of the C-terminal anchor. At the same time, the stabilizing Arg9-Asp179 interaction remained highly occupied in full-length trajectories but decreased to 75.0% in 9-23 at pH 5.5, leaving the shortened peptide sufficiently unconstrained for its C terminus to fold back toward His22. Thus, protonation should not be treated as a universal stabilizing condition across constructs; instead, its effect depends on the interaction network remaining in each peptide.

Two additional observations support this interpretation. First, the newly exposed Arg9 α-amino group remained 6.90 Å from its nearest receptor carboxylate and formed a sub-4 Å contact in only 5.8% of frames, providing further evidence against the proposed terminal salt bridge. Second, although protonation restored the Glu12-His227 interaction to 99.9% occupancy in full length, the same pair reached only 26.4% in 9-23, indicating that protonation alone cannot stabilize an interaction when the upstream peptide sequence required to position it has been removed.

The trajectory also provides a methodological warning because several conventional structural metrics would have classified this failed construct as improved. Relative to 9-23 at pH 7, backbone RMSD decreased from 2.28 to 1.33 Å, conserved-window contacts increased from 68.5 to 92.2, and the Asp348 and Phe371 interactions returned to their wild-type-associated peptide residues. Nevertheless, the atom-resolved acceptance criteria showed that the principal Asn23-Arg487 anchor had largely collapsed. Consequently, RMSD and aggregate contact counts cannot substitute for monitoring the specific interactions that define the binding model.

Run 9 of 9

The apo receptor

The receptor with no peptide at all. It is the run that says how much of the motion in the other eight would have happened anyway.

Apo receptor

To determine whether peptide binding itself produced measurable conformational changes in PEPR1, we also simulated the receptor without peptide using chain A from the wild-type complex and otherwise identical conditions. Contrary to the expectation that the unbound receptor would become substantially more flexible or that its binding groove would partially collapse, the apo receptor remained structurally comparable to the peptide-bound trajectories.

The apo receptor had the lowest mean Cα RMSD of all nine simulations at 1.57 Å, compared with 1.58-2.16 Å across the peptide-bound runs, while its groove span was 35.91 Å compared with 35.65-36.42 Å for the bound structures. Its per-residue flexibility profile also correlated strongly with the wild-type profile at r = 0.94-0.97. Moreover, the 54 interface residues shifted by only +0.125 Å relative to wild type, compared with +0.156 Å for the remaining 656 residues, with the largest differences occurring at residues 41-55, which constitute the N-terminal cap of the ectodomain, and with residue 738, the final residue, being the most rigid in the structure. Both observations reflect chain-end effects distant from the binding groove. Thus, within the 30 ns simulation window, we found no evidence for a substantial peptide-induced conformational change in the receptor.

This provides both a structural and methodological control. The pre-formed groove supports representing the binding surface using a mean receptor structure rather than assuming that ligand binding creates the pocket. More importantly, the apo trajectory establishes a noise floor against which receptor-side differences can be interpreted: the seven peptide-bound runs differed from one another by 0.59 Å in receptor Cα RMSD, while the apo trajectory was only 0.01 Å below the steadiest bound trajectory. Because changing the peptide produced more receptor-side variation than removing it entirely, we do not interpret small receptor RMSD or flexibility differences as evidence that BoPEP4 stabilizes PEPR1. Instead, our comparisons remain centered on peptide-side contact geometry.

Across the set

One result needs every run to exist: the number a construct would normally be ranked on does not track the bond the ranking is standing in for.

Aggregate metrics and ranking constructs

The combined trajectories further demonstrate why RMSD and aggregate structural measures are insufficient for ranking these constructs. Across the eight peptide-containing runs, backbone RMSD over the conserved window correlated only weakly with the bidentate Arg487 salt bridge, with Pearson and Spearman coefficients of +0.13 and +0.29, respectively, while correlations with clamp integrity were −0.03 and −0.07. In other words, overall structural stability was largely independent of whether the key binding interaction remained chemically intact.

The individual trajectories illustrate this more clearly: the tagged construct and 9-23 at pH 5.5 had the two lowest RMSDs, at 1.12 Å and 1.33 Å, yet their bidentate salt bridges were occupied in only 2.4% and 6.9% of frames, whereas 15-23 had a higher RMSD of 1.81 Å but retained the salt bridge in 97.2% of frames. Contact count performed relatively better, with a Spearman correlation of +0.71 across the seven untagged runs, but this comparison is based on only seven trajectories and is strongly influenced by individual constructs. Together with the earlier docking-score analysis, these results support using named atom-pair criteria rather than aggregate scores to determine whether a construct retains the intended binding chemistry.

Limits

  • Thirty nanoseconds is not a binding measurement. It is three to six orders of magnitude short of a dissociation event, so nothing on this page is a binding affinity, a leaving time or a free energy. The 15-23 control says so in the students' own words: failing to see a peptide leave in 30 ns cannot establish that it would stay.
  • One seed per system. A difference between two runs may be a difference between two relaxation paths rather than between two constructs.
  • The acceptance gate was written before the tagged construct existed and, on that construct, measures atoms it does not have. That is the finding of the tag chapter and it applies backwards to every number the gate produced.
  • None of it has been checked at the bench. No binding assay, no thermal shift, no plant.
What the set changed

The tag moved to the N-terminus, pH became a parameter rather than an assumption, the K18R rationale narrowed, and a salt bridge that was on no published hotspot list, Lys7 to Asp129, became the explanation for why the construct in hand is the unstable one. Each of those is a design decision, and each is argued in the chapter above it.
