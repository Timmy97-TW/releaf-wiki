What this model cannot tell you

The limits are part of the model. A well-scored, stereochemically clean complex says the sequence is capable of the molecular recognition step: the one thing this kind of modelling is able to answer. It does not say the peptide is taken up, survives the apoplast, or that binding fires the downstream immune output. Pattern-triggered immunity is read out in the cell, not in the coordinates, so the redesign loop can only ever optimise for predicted binding, never for function.

Established here

Shape complementarity, plausible interface chemistry, a ΔG estimate, and reproduction of known anchor contacts.

Not established

Ca2+ influx, ROS burst, MAPK activation, PEPR1–BAK1 phosphotransfer, growth inhibition.

Only in the wet lab

Binding assay (ITC / MST / SPR), receptor co-immunoprecipitation, and a functional defence-marker readout.

1Introduction

1.1 Background

Computational tools give real insight into protein engineering and act as decision-making engines for wet lab work, but the process is easy to oversimplify: convenient analysis of inputs and outputs in place of thorough analysis, and no methodology tailored to the project it serves.

Their outputs depend heavily on the quality of inputs, parameters and underlying assumptions, and that dependence is worse for short, disordered peptides such as Brassica oleracea Plant Elicitor Peptide 4 (BoPEP4), one of our three protectants alongside Late Embryogenesis Abundant protein 14 (LEA14) and 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate deaminase (ACC deaminase). A workflow that holds up has to account for how and why these tools generate their predictions, which is more than running them.

Our docking methodology predicts and evaluates the ternary signalling complex of the receptor-like kinase PEPR1, the co-receptor kinase BAK1, and the Brassica-derived peptide ligand BoPEP4. We designed the workflow on the principles of integrative structural biology established by Alexandre Bonvin at Utrecht University, whose lab produced most of the software below.

How to read this page

Every stage is written twice. The opening text is the method itself: what the step is for, what it takes in, and what it can and cannot tell you. The panels under it are what we got when we ran it on each protectant.

2.1 Choosing the protectants

We started from the stress we needed to fix, not from a molecule we liked, and we searched gene families instead of single proteins. A family is a set of sequences that arose by duplication, so its members usually share a mechanism even when their triggers differ. Four criteria cut the shortlist: does the mechanism match the stress we are targeting, can we assay it in the wet lab we have, can we engineer it, and is it novel enough to be worth the risk. A candidate that fails one is written down as considered and excluded. The exclusions set the edges of the design space, so they belong in the record as much as the three we kept.

The PEP family, and why BoPEP4 over AtPEP1

The BoPROPEP family tree identified candidate PEP peptides including BoPEP4, AtPEP1 and AtPEP3, and let us place BoPEP4 in relation to Arabidopsis instead of taking the peptide directly from the plant. PEP-family peptides are perceived by the same pair of receptor kinases, PEPR1 and PEPR2, so receptor recognition is conserved across the family: a reasonable first signal for which candidates behave similarly, before any experiment.

We did not assume the best-characterised peptide would be the best target. AtPEP1 is known mainly for biotic stress, while the project needed measurable abiotic stress, which weather data and sensors detect well. Drought is prominent in Taiwan and is directly signalled by BoPEP4. Abiotic focus also gives direct plant phenotypes such as root length and biomass, where biotic assays would add a second organism and more confounders.

Finally, BoPEP4's variable N-terminal region alongside its conserved C-terminal motif (SSG-x2-G-x2-N) allows targeted N-terminal edits while preserving the conserved core, and its relative obscurity means the sequence variants are genuinely uncharacterised.

The LEA family, and why LEA14

LEA proteins are associated with abiotic stress and can show substantial intrinsic disorder: some lack a single stable fold and adopt different conformations depending on environment or partner. We therefore separated ordered or globular members from intrinsically disordered ones, because mutation methods designed for folded proteins cannot be read the same way for disordered peptides, and the family contains both.

We chose the ordered, globular LEA14, with demonstrated protection against drought, salinity and osmotic stress. Its defined structure gives a stable scaffold for the protein-protein interactions involved in molecular stabilisation. Unlike BoPEP4, it does not depend on recognising one dedicated receptor; its stabilisation function can involve many cellular proteins, so a larger structured protein suits the question better. A disordered LEA would instead act through broader, less specific molecular-shield mechanisms.

It also gives a measurable phenotype for wet lab testing, and at over 150 residues it poses a fundamentally different design problem from a short receptor-binding peptide.

ACC deaminase

Suggested by Stanford bioengineering assistant professor Jenn Brophy in interview. It fills a real gap: both LEA14 and BoPEP4 fail against heat stress, one of Taiwan's largest abiotic stresses.

The enzyme is largely specific to ACC, the direct precursor of the plant hormone ethylene. Acting on that one signalling molecule lets it reduce stress-induced ethylene accumulation, and so the growth inhibition that follows, without interfering with other cellular chemistry. It also functions both inside plant cells and outside the roots, which widens the application routes.

Considered and set aside: trehalose, proline, CAP peptides

Trehalose was set aside despite its established role in osmotic tolerance: it is already heavily engineered in synthetic biology and offers little differentiation. Proline was excluded because excessive accumulation is associated with cytotoxic rather than purely protective effects, a risk for a system producing protectant continuously. CAP-derived peptides were deprioritised on timeline, since synthesis and validation would not fit the development window.

2.2 Literature: understanding the mechanism

The reading supplies the parameters every later step needs, and it tells us whether a design is new. We used three kinds of source: sequence databases for the canonical sequence and the domain boundaries, structural databases for coordinates and for the residue range that was actually resolved, and primary functional papers for the assays and the effect sizes. With only the first two you can build a design, but you cannot say what it means.

Tang et al. 2015

Resolved the AtPEP1-receptor structure and showed the peptide binding in an extended conformation. Because of that, our docking inputs treat BoPEP4 as a set of possible conformational states instead of one rigid structure, and that structure is the reference the receptor-binding interface is read against.

Yu et al.

Covers broader strategies for designing and engineering plant-derived peptides. It supported truncation and residue substitution as exploratory axes, and we used it to identify which modifications would produce interpretable changes, since not every mutation is equally informative.

Pearce et al. 2008

An alanine scan of AtPEP1(9–23) and the basis for several of our experimental design concepts. It motivated systematic peptide modification to establish which residues contribute to activity, and it later settled a design question directly: [A18] is fully active in the alkalinisation assay at 2.5 nM, ten times the EC50.

Truncation series, as a method

A truncation series progressively removes portions of a sequence, so comparing variants of different lengths shows whether a region is necessary for the expected interaction. Truncation was therefore both an engineering strategy and a mechanistic probe.

Alanine scanning, as a method

Replaces selected residues with alanine, keeping a simple side chain while removing the original chemistry. The purpose is diagnostic: a substitution that degrades the measured interaction marks a load-bearing position, and one that does not marks a degenerate position available for other modification. With truncation it is the conceptual foundation of the mutation-design workflow.

2.3 Interviews

An interview is only useful to us later if the record keeps three things apart: what the expert stated, what they recommended, and what we decided afterwards. They collapse into one line easily, and then nobody can tell whose judgement it was. The questions have to be narrow too. A question with no edges comes back with an answer we cannot act on.

Dr. Jenn Brophy, Stanford Bioengineering

Her interview shaped decisions beyond the choice of ACC deaminase. Asked how to apply stress-sensor and protectant-synthesis logic so plants tolerate heat and salinity, she recommended an OR-gate architecture, and advised working backward from application needs: select a logic gate that is activated under the conditions you care about rather than starting from circuit design. She pointed to two- and three-input logic gates in E. coli as a resource.

Her ACC deaminase recommendation was scoped to drought and heat. She made no direct claim about salinity, so we researched the OR-gate further to see whether it extends to both heat and salinity.

2.4 Protectant families: sequence alignment

Comparing sequences is the cheapest first pass at conservation, variability and possible engineering sites, and it costs nothing to run before any structure exists. What it gives is candidate positions, with no ranking among them. Comparison on its own cannot tell a position that varies because it does not matter from one where two peptides solve the same problem in different ways.

PEP sequence alignment and BoPEP4

Multiple AtPEP sequences were aligned to find shared residues and conserved patterns, then compared with BoPEP4. That gives both halves of the engineering brief at once: conserved residues are features to preserve, and the positions where they differ are candidates for mutation and truncation. The AtPEP1–PEPR1 structure provides the context for reading those conserved residues, especially in the C-terminal region that makes concentrated receptor contacts.

Both peptides are 23 residues and align without gaps, so every position has a direct counterpart and numbering transfers one to one. Without that one-to-one correspondence, AtPEP1 structural data could not be read onto BoPEP4 at all.

Ten positions are identical: 7, 9, 12, 15, 16, 17, 19, 20, 22 and 23, all in the C-terminal region. Identity is 10 of 23 across the full peptide, 10 of 17 within residues 7–23, and zero across residues 1–6. That boundary matters because an independent method draws it in the same place: 5GR8 resolves AtPEP1 only from residue 7, and our contact analysis found both PEPR1 and PEPR2 engage residues 7–23 while the first six make no receptor contact.

BoPEP4 also conforms to the conserved motif SSG-x2-G-x2-N at positions 15–23: Ser15, Ser16, Gly17, Gly20 and Asn23 are all present. The two positions differing inside that window, 18 and 21, both fall in the motif's degenerate positions. The peptide satisfies the family signature and differs only where the family permits variation.

The N-terminal divergence is chemical as well as positional. AtPEP1 opens with ATKVKA, charged; BoPEP4 opens with GILIGS, uncharged and hydrophobic. The whole segment differs in character, not at scattered positions.

Across the three engineering axes the alignment fixed a starting point for each. N-terminal truncation is supported by two independent lines, zero conservation and zero receptor contact across 1–6. Point substitution has a defined candidate set inside 7–23, with positions 18 and 8 differing from AtPEP1. The conserved repeat analysis has a defined unit at 15–23. Which of those candidates survived is reported in Sprints and Results.

Why we did not run a family alignment for LEA14

Sequence conservation alone was less informative for the questions being asked. The LEA family contains substantial structural diversity, including both intrinsically disordered and folded members, so similar sequence patterns do not necessarily correspond to equivalent structural or functional roles.

LEA14 was instead analysed as a specific folded protein: its experimentally characterised structure, residue environment, interaction surfaces, and the vulnerabilities created by our delivery route. That let us connect each proposed mutation to a defined structural hypothesis. The two protectants therefore needed different forms of sequence analysis.

2.5 Engineering axes

Our substitutions split in two, and which kind one is decides how we justify it. An interface substitution changes a contact, and we take the new residue from a homologue whose structure is solved, so it is a residue already seen to work in that position. A liability substitution removes a predicted failure mode: one chemical feature that one process condition is expected to attack.

BoPEP4 axes
Truncations

The uncharged six-residue GILIGS segment carries zero conservation and zero receptor contact, making the N-terminus the only end that can be trimmed. The C-terminus is off limits: Asn23's free carboxylate is the anchor, and deleting the last residue is documented to compromise binding. Fewer residues to synthesise and export also implies faster secretion, so shortening is a manufacturability gain if binding holds. The wet lab took this up as a testable claim and is measuring secretion for truncated constructs against wild type.

Point substitutions

The axis came from a simple idea: BoPEP4 residues not in contact with PEPR1 could be swapped for the residue AtPEP1 carries at the same position, restoring residues present in the literature-backed 5GR8 structure. Alignment identified positions 18 and 8 as the candidate set inside the contact region.

Conserved repeats

BLAST scans suggested the conserved 15–23 region drives receptor binding, motivating a tandem repeat analysis. The countervailing risk is that lengthening the peptide makes it less active-site specific, filling other parts of the receptor.

What each axis returned, including the designs we withdrew and why, is in Sprints and Results.

LEA14 axes
Point substitution

These address liabilities identified in the sequence, not contacts at an interface. LEA14 contains a single cysteine, Cys58, at the start of β3 with no partner. In the reducing plant cytosol an unpaired thiol is inert, but our route sends the protein through the oxidising extracellular compartment where it can form intermolecular disulfides and dimerise. C58S substitutes a serine of almost identical size that cannot. The test is a gel run with and without reducing agent, so the hypothesis fails visibly if a paired band persists.

The sequence also contains exactly one Asp-Pro bond, at 78–79, the most acid-labile peptide bond type. D78E removes it while preserving the negative charge. This is insurance against a process condition, not a gain in activity, and was ranked as such.

A third group targets the flexible N-terminal extension at 2–19. Helical wheel analysis gives that segment a hydrophobic moment near 0.37, periodic but below the range typical of membrane-binding amphipathic helices. Face-consistent substitutions could raise it into that range and turn a flexible tail into a membrane anchor. We record this as a hypothesis, and built the N-terminal truncation as its matched negative control.

We deliberately did not pursue surface hydrophilicity substitutions. LEA14's GRAVY of +0.056 is unusual for a stress-protective protein and could be lowered, but doing so works against the fold, and the bulk hydration shielding it would serve is the mechanism our feasibility analysis argues is unavailable at any deliverable concentration.

K-segment translocation

LEA14 belongs to the atypical folded LEA_2 group and carries none of the repeat motifs characterising the disordered groups. The K-segment is the best-characterised membrane-protection module in the family and occurs in dehydrins, not here. The design fuses one or two K-segments, or Group 3 LEA 11-mer repeats, onto the folded core, giving a stable scaffold with a disordered arm.

The precedent is documented: the mitochondrial LEA protein LEAM is disordered in solution and folds into an amphipathic helix that binds and stabilises membranes as the cell dries. If the motif carries that behaviour instead of the whole protein, grafting it should carry the behaviour with it. Two constraints bound the graft: added mass reduces the fraction crossing the cell wall, and every fusion junction is a new site to audit for degron compliance and protease susceptibility.

Truncations

The published solution structure defines residues 24–145. The first 23 have no fixed conformation in the ensemble and are where a protease attacks first. Removing them does three things at once: removes the most exposed proteolytic target, lowers mass and hydrodynamic radius, and leaves the folded β-sandwich core intact. The same construct doubles as the negative control for the amphipathicity hypothesis above, so the two read against each other. The test is a trypsin challenge comparing how quickly full-length and truncated bands disappear.

Zone 02 on the map →3.1 Peptide structural input

BoPEP4 structural input

We based the input on two sources: 5GR8, the crystal structure of PEPR1 with AtPEP1, and AlphaFold. 5GR8 confirmed the extended conformation of AtPEP1 along the inner surface of the superhelical PEPR1 leucine-rich repeat (LRR), supporting the inference that BoPEP4, from the same PEP family, adopts a comparable extended structure when bound. Structures were generated with AlphaFold 3, cross-checked against 5GR8, and used as docking inputs, with 5GR8 itself as the control.

Run on the free peptide, AlphaFold gives us nothing we can dock: fraction_disordered = 1.0, pTM 0.18–0.35, and five seeds returning five different secondary structures. For a peptide that only orders when it binds, that is the expected answer and not a failed run. The route we took instead is threading onto the AtPEP1 backbone of 5GR8 chain J, which the gapless alignment allows: the backbone transfers unchanged and we rebuild only the side chains at the positions that differ.

3.2 Receptor and co-receptor structures

Two receptors and one co-receptor make up the BoPEP4 signalling complex. PEPR1, an LRR receptor-like kinase acting as an innate immunity pattern recognition receptor, has its extracellular domain in 5GR8 chain A, which we used as the docking receptor input.

Given the similarity between PEPR1 and PEPR2 we considered PEPR2 as an alternative. PEPR2 is more specific toward AtPEP ligands where PEPR1 shows broader recognition. We generated a PEPR2 structure with both AlphaFold 3 and SWISS-MODEL homology modelling against PEPR1, but selected PEPR1 as primary on the strength of its experimental structure, using PEPR2 as a complementary model.

BAK1 came from 4MN8 chain B, the FLS2–BAK1 complex bound to flg22. BAK1 is a shared LRR co-receptor for multiple plant immune receptors including the PEPRs, and its conserved ectodomain provides the framework for receptor–co-receptor association. 4MN8 does not contain PEPR1, but the resolved BAK1 ectodomain transfers on that conserved architecture.

3.3 Receptor restraint mapping

Restraints mark the residues expected to participate in an interaction, constraining the search toward interfaces that are physically and biologically plausible. Given the limited and heterogeneous evidence for the molecules in this complex, we cross-validated across several tools before writing anything into a restraint file.

MUSCLE alignment and WHISCY

To supply WHISCY with evolutionary information for PEPR1 we collected homologues by BLAST and aligned 352 sequences with MUSCLE. WHISCY weights each surface residue by conservation, on the reasoning that conserved solvent-exposed patches are more likely to be functional interfaces. We ran it both with and without the alignment so the effect of the evolutionary input could be measured.

Results · what the scan and the predictor returned

41 active residues, 7 supported by both

The primary restraint set is contact-derived from 5GR8: every PEPR1 heavy atom against every AtPEP1 heavy atom at 4.5 Å, across both crystallographic copies, unioned. That gave 41 active residues covering most of the concave LRR face. Passive residues were solvent-exposed positions (relative SASA ≥ 0.40) with a Cα within 8 Å of an active residue, giving 9. The passive set is thin because the active interface is unusually extensive, as expected for an LRR wrapping its ligand.

The same scan found the anchor Tang et al. report, without being pointed at it: Arg487 salt-bridges the C-terminal carboxylate of Asn23 at 2.40 Å and 2.59 Å in the two copies. Arg491 makes no hydrogen bond and no salt bridge on our PDBePISA run. It buries 12 Å² and sits about 5 Å away, so we record it as an interface residue and not as a second anchor, and we do not test models against it.

On the peptide side the scan returned 16 of the 17 resolved AtPEP1 residues, and we treated the peptide as fully active, standard for a short linear ligand enclosed in a groove.

Run blind, WHISCY's active tier recovered little of the crystal interface, 1 of 41 and 2 of 41, agreeing with each other on a single residue, so we did not treat it as a reliable independent check. The passive tier behaved differently, returning the same six crystal-contact residues either way. Seven residues (347, 348, 369, 371, 372, 393, 395) appear in the crystal contacts and in both runs; we kept all 41 as active and flagged those seven as the highest-confidence core.

For PEPR2 we transferred the lists by pairwise alignment, giving 39 active and 9 passive. 21 of the 41 PEPR1 active residues are identical in PEPR2. Arg487, the anchor, is conserved as Arg464. These lists are a starting hypothesis for the homology model, not a validated restraint set.

US-align and the BAK1 interface

No structure of PEPR1 bound to BAK1 exists, so the co-receptor interface had to be predicted by structural analogy with FLS2.

Results · the transferred interface

23 residues mapped onto PEPR1

FLS2 and PEPR1 share only 28% sequence identity, too low for sequence-based transfer, but US-align returns TM-score 0.840, well above the 0.5 threshold for the same fold. We read residue equivalences directly from the alignment block, without approximating by nearest neighbour, then mapped FLS2's 25 BAK1-contact residues onto PEPR1, giving 23 predicted BAK1-site residues.

An initial placement rotating the whole BAK1 domain produced a physically impossible intertwined model. A local Kabsch refit restricted to matched interface residues brought interface RMSD from 4.00 Å to 2.09 Å and raised plausible contact pairs from 61 to 95.

Two of the 23, residues 372 and 395, fall inside the seven-residue peptide-interface core, and several sit near the 487–491 region. In the FLS2 system the ligand's C-terminal region and BAK1's footprint are structurally adjacent, so this is what the architecture would predict. We report it as consistent with known biology, not as evidence for it, since a geometric transfer could land generically nearby.

On the BAK1 side the restraints are contact-derived from 4MN8: 24 active and 4 passive, comprising contacts with FLS2 and with flg22, where Val54 and Thr58 are shared. We had expected Koller and Bent (2014) to give a second, conservation-based line of support. On reading it in full we found it reports the opposite: the conserved cluster it identifies did not impair FLS2–BAK1 interaction under mutagenesis, and the paper concludes FLS2 and BRI1 use receptor-specific docking sites. The BAK1 restraints therefore rest on a single line of evidence and were weighted accordingly.

DisVis

Before committing to full runs, DisVis tests whether a restraint set describes a geometrically possible arrangement at all. It scans every rigid-body placement of one partner around the other and reports how many restraints can be satisfied simultaneously, with the volume of accessible interaction space. Each line in a DisVis file is independent, with no OR grouping, so a broad active set is not expected to be fully satisfiable; what matters is how much of the set and how much space stay consistent, measured against a case where the answer is known.

Results · the two runs

Prepared, not yet run

Because we had active residue lists and no known pairs, we generated the full combinatorial matrix for each run, as Cα–Cα distances bounded 2.0 to 8.0 Å. Run 1, PEPR1 against AtPEP1 (656 restraints), is the positive control drawn from a solved co-crystal. Run 2, PEPR1 against BAK1 (552 restraints), is the test: its two sides were derived independently and never checked for mutual compatibility.

DisVis has no numbers on it yet. The inputs are prepared, but the web server needs a WeNMR login we do not have, so the satisfiable-restraint count, the accessible interaction space, and the Run 2 against Run 1 comparison are all missing.

Structural superposition gave reference geometry for each binary interface, which is what lets us judge docked models on structure instead of score alone.

Results · the reference poses

fnat 0.974 on the threaded peptide

For the peptide interface we threaded BoPEP4 onto the AtPEP1 backbone from 5GR8 chain J. The gapless alignment means numbering carries over one to one. The threaded model reproduces the native interface with fnat 0.974, so it is the reference pose for BoPEP4 in the groove. That number is mostly a consequence of how the model was built: grafting onto the native backbone recovers the native contacts at conserved positions by definition. It tells us the threading came out undistorted. It does not tell us BoPEP4 binds this way.

Asn23 is held by five contacts and not by one salt bridge: OXT–Arg487 NH1 at 2.40 Å, O–Arg487 NE at 2.80 Å, ND2–Asp441 at 2.71 Å, N–Asp441 at 2.75 Å, and O–Asn465 at 3.14 Å.

For the co-receptor interface we superimposed FLS2 (4MN8 chain A) onto PEPR1 along the LRR β-ladder at 1.59 Å over 74 Cα pairs and carried BAK1 with the transform. As a check, the transformed flg22 lands within 1 to 2 Å of AtPEP1, confirming both ligands occupy the same register on their respective receptors.

HADDOCK score did not reliably track structural accuracy in these runs: one cluster ranked fourth by score returned fnat 0.000. We therefore rescored every cluster against the 5GR8 and 4MN8 reference geometry and selected models by agreement with those references.

3.4 LEA14 structural input

We took the protein from 1XO8, the solution NMR structure of At1g01470, deposited by the Center for Eukaryotic Structural Genomics. Two features of that deposition matter. The entry is annotated UNKNOWN FUNCTION, so nothing in the record says where the protein binds anything. And the construct is the whole protein: the deposition cross-references UniProt O03983 across residues 1 to 151, and the coordinates carry all 151 with no gaps and no expression tag. What we dock is the complete mature protein.

Results · what the 20-model ensemble contains

A rigid core and one mobile tail

Over the first ten models the mean pairwise Cα RMSD across all 151 residues is 3.94 Å, maximum 5.55 Å. Restricted to the folded core, residues 24–145, the same comparison gives 0.66 Å, maximum 0.84 Å. The spread is almost entirely in the N-terminal tail; the β-sandwich core is one conformation reproduced twenty times.

Per-residue fluctuation agrees. Superposing all 20 models on the core, Cα RMSF averages 8.75 Å across residues 1–16 against 0.42 Å across 24–145. The transition is abrupt: Leu16 fluctuates by 3.97 Å and Ala18 by 1.16 Å, a factor of three across two residues. The C-terminal six residues sit at 1.61 Å, mobile without being disordered. The most rigid position is Cys58 at 0.26 Å.

Those figures reproduce the ones already in the LEA14 design documents to the same decimal, which is the cross-check we wanted before building on them. They also set the truncation boundary: a cut at residue 16 removes the mobile segment and leaves the ordered region intact, where a cut at 23 would remove six residues belonging to the folded core. Glu23 is 10.9% solvent exposed on our own calculation, which is to say buried.

Two truncation boundaries, and neither was built. The engineering axis cuts residues 1–23, from the published structure's 24–145; this ensemble puts the boundary at 16. We did not choose between them. Both went onto the September design list and into Level 0 assembly on 26 September, and neither reached a sequence-verified construct; every LEA14 plasmid we hold is full-length.

For docking we prepared two forms: model 1 alone, and the first ten models concatenated as a HADDOCK ensemble input. Chain and segment identifier A, heavy atoms only.

3.5 AtPP2-B11 structural input

No experimental structure of AtPP2-B11 exists, so this one had to be predicted. We folded the full 257-residue sequence of UniProt Q949S5 with AlphaFold 3 on the AlphaFold Server, templates enabled, and the server returned five models from the submitted seed.

Results · confidence and what the five models mean

pTM 0.89, mean pLDDT 90.8

All five models report pTM 0.89 with ranking scores 0.89 to 0.90, no clashes, and a disordered fraction of 0.00 or 0.01. Mean pLDDT on the top model is 90.8: 92.8 over the F-box region, 89.9 over the linker, 90.6 over the PP2 domain. Three stretches fall below pLDDT 70: residues 1–2 at the terminus, and two loops inside the PP2 domain at 179–186 and 196–202.

As an independent check we compared the top model against the AlphaFold Database entry for the same protein, computed separately. The two agree to 1.27 Å Cα RMSD over all 257 residues, and both place the same two loops in their low-confidence regions.

The word ensemble needs qualifying. Mean pairwise Cα RMSD among the five models is 0.94 Å, maximum 1.14 Å, and restricting to the PP2 domain changes nothing at 0.95 Å. The five models are the same conformation five times: they sample the predictor's uncertainty, not the molecule's conformational space. LEA14 is the opposite case: its deposited models carry genuine variety in the tail.

Which part we dock

InterPro places the F-box domain at residues 1–44 and the PP2 lectin-like domain at 92–255, with an unassigned linker between. The F-box is the surface that binds ASK1 inside the SCF complex, so it is not available to LEA14 in the cell. Docking the full chain would let the search place LEA14 on a face that is occupied in vivo, so the input is residues 45–257 with the F-box removed, numbering preserved. That leaves the PP2 lectin domain as the surface under test, which is also the substrate-recognition module by analogy with the solved plant F-box complexes.

Zone 04 on the map →4Assembly: structural docking

4.1 Binary docking

Binary docking reduces the complexity of the full system and characterises one interface at a time. Most of our runs, including the alanine scans and truncation ladders, are built on this workflow because of its relative simplicity.

We docked BoPEP4 against the PEPR1 ectodomain in HADDOCK 2.4. The conformations generated during structure preparation were treated as alternative representations of the flexible peptide, and the active and passive residues from restraint mapping guided the search toward plausible interfaces.

Sampling was 1000 rigid-body, 200 semi-flexible and 200 water-refined models per run, FCC clustering at 0.60, minimum cluster size 4, peptide fully flexible. Confirmation runs, which ask the cost of a single edit instead of where the peptide docks, used 40/10/10 on the same protocol. Settings were constant across every binary run, so scores stay comparable.

Models were clustered by structural similarity and evaluated by HADDOCK score and subsequent structural assessment. We did not treat the highest-scoring structure as correct, and retained structurally plausible clusters for comparison.

4.2 Threaded ternary docking

BoPEP4's activity is not determined solely by PEPR1. BAK1 is the signalling kinase balancing growth and innate immunity, and PEP perception involves receptor–co-receptor association, so we asked whether the predicted BoPEP4–PEPR1 interaction could be accommodated in a PEPR1–BoPEP4–BAK1 complex.

Instead of docking all three components simultaneously, we used the binary result as a structural starting point: a selected PEPR1–BoPEP4 complex was retained as established, and BAK1 was introduced on the interface identified during restraint mapping. This preserved the geometry of the modelled ligand–receptor interaction while searching for a compatible BAK1 orientation. Resulting models were assessed for steric clashes, interface plausibility and consistency with the restraints.

Two caveats. The ternary run inherits its binary input, so a peptide-register error would carry through uncaught, which is why the binary complex was validated against 5GR8 first. And its two restraint sets are not equally certain: peptide-to-receptor is contact-derived from a solved co-crystal, BAK1-to-receptor transferred from 4MN8 by analogy. The output is a structural hypothesis consistent with the published heterodimerisation biochemistry, not a residue-level prediction.

4.3 AI co-folding

HADDOCK searches under restraints we supply; a co-folding predictor builds from sequence with none. The two fail for different reasons, so agreement between them carries information. We ran AlphaFold 3 on the AlphaFold Server, templates enabled, five models per seed, with nothing from the HADDOCK workflow entering the job. The two tracks run in parallel and never seed each other.

We record ipTM, pTM, the ranking score and the chain-pair ipTM matrix with interface PAE, since a single ipTM averages across every interface and a confident pair can hide a failed one. Tracks are compared on interface residue identity, not global RMSD. Solved complexes of the same class are run first under identical settings, and the acceptance threshold is fixed from what they return.

Results · confidence is not activity

ipTM 0.73 for a peptide 4000-fold inactive

Our 30 July audit found AlphaFold 3 returned ipTM 0.73 for a peptide published as more than 4000-fold inactive, against 0.74 for BoPEP4. The metric does not separate a working ligand from a dead one, so we use it only as a structural confidence estimate. Multiple models from one seed express the predictor's uncertainty about a single answer. Treating them as a conformational ensemble over-reads them.

The docking and co-folding runs themselves are in Sprints and Results.

Zone 04 on the map →5Flex: molecular dynamics

Docking returns a pose. It cannot say whether that pose survives once the atoms are allowed to move, and every design decision above rests on poses that have only been scored, not watched. Flex is where that gets tested.

The work is nine all-atom trajectories, including an AtPEP1 benchmark and an apo receptor control alongside the BoPEP4 constructs. The Molecular Dynamics page reports all of it: the systems and their parameters, what each run changed about the design, and the limits of a 30 ns window.

Zone 05 on the map →6Triage: structure scoring

Docking returns hundreds of models and will always return a best one, whether or not any of them are right. Triage decides whether a model is worth carrying forward, and it is deliberately built from methods that fail for unrelated reasons. A model passes only if it passes all five. When one fails, which one fails is the diagnosis that shapes the next round of truncations and substitutions.

  1. Did the search converge?
  2. Is the model actually right?
  3. Is this the size and chemistry of a real interface?
  4. Is the geometry chemically legal?
  5. Does it reproduce contacts we already know exist?

6.1 HADDOCK score and clustering

HADDOCK groups similar models into clusters, each representing one binding mode. We read the funnel shape and the cluster population before the single best-scoring model, because a lone good score with no supporting cluster usually means the search sampled a lucky pose without converging. A well-populated cluster at the bottom of a funnel is the shape we want.

Why this is not fully applicable: the score discrepancy

HADDOCK score is a weighted sum of energy terms in arbitrary units, designed to rank models within a run. In our own validation it did not track structural accuracy: one cluster ranked fourth by score reproduced none of the native contacts. That failure is the single most important reason triage exists as a multi-check process. If the score alone were reliable we would not need the other four. We use it to judge convergence and to rank within a run, never as evidence that a model is correct.

It matters more again across constructs of different length, where a longer chain buries more surface and scores better for that alone. Down a truncation series we compare per unit of interface, never raw score.

6.2 CAPRI

CAPRI is the community standard for grading a predicted complex against a known answer, sorting models into acceptable, medium and high accuracy on contact recovery and ligand placement. CAPRI is the only check that measures correctness directly, and it works only where a reference structure exists. For us that is 5GR8. We grade every protocol change against it, so that any claim about a model with no reference rests on a protocol calibrated on one that has it.

One caution: the grade is an aggregate. A model can land in the medium tier with the single most conserved contact in the system far out of place. The literature check below exists to catch exactly that.

6.3 PDBePISA

PISA reports buried surface area, chemical composition, and whether a contact looks like a genuine assembly or a crystal packing artefact. It answers a question the docking score cannot: is this the right size and character for a complex that exists in solution? A model can score well while burying far too little surface to be real.

The 5GR8 reference list

We ran PISA on the crystal first, so every later number has a reference point. Our docked models are read against those values, which matters for a 23-mer peptide where expectations built on globular interfaces do not transfer.

Results · the reference interface

2,039 Å² and 25 unique interactions

The 5GR8 interface buries 2,039 Å², and that surface is 21% apolar, 41% polar and 38% charged. The bond inventory is 19 hydrogen bonds and 10 salt bridges, which comes to 25 unique interactions once the pairs that appear in both tables are counted once.

One property of this interface decides how we read PISA here: its own hydrophobicity test comes back negative. The peptide side gives a solvation gain of +2.5 kcal·mol−1 with a P-value of 0.585, so the interface is less hydrophobic than a random patch of the same size. The polar network is what holds it together, and that is why we read area and bond inventory here instead of the ΔiG verdict.

6.4 MolProbity

MolProbity validates the stereochemistry of the coordinates themselves, independent of any docking score. It reports a clashscore for atoms in impossible overlapping positions, rotamer outliers for side chains in conformations real residues do not adopt, and backbone geometry. A model can satisfy every restraint and still be chemically illegal, particularly after the side-chain repacking refinement performs, which is why this check sits outside the energy-based scores.

The Ramachandran plot

The plot maps each residue's backbone dihedrals against the regions physically accessible to a polypeptide. Most residues should fall in the favoured regions, and outliers mark strained conformations. For threaded and refined peptide models this is a particularly direct check, because threading imposes a backbone taken from another sequence and this is where the strain from that imposition would show.

6.5 Literature check

The final check asks whether the model reproduces contacts that are already documented. For the PEPR1 interface that is Arg487 engaging the peptide's free C-terminal carboxylate, which we verified directly in the crystal coordinates before using it as a test. A model that fails to reproduce it is wrong regardless of how it scored on everything above, because it has placed the peptide in the wrong register.

We measured the criterion on the crystal before applying it to anything. Arg487 holds the carboxylate at 2.40 Å. Arg491 is often named alongside it, but on our PDBePISA run it makes no hydrogen bond and no salt bridge at all, so we report it as an interface residue and do not score against it.

This check is cheap, it is the hardest to fool, and it is the one that caught register errors the energy scores accepted.

What triage cannot establish

A model passing all five checks shows the sequence is capable of the molecular recognition step. It does not show that the peptide reaches the receptor, survives the apoplast, or that binding produces the downstream immune output. Calcium influx, ROS burst, MAPK activation and receptor complex phosphotransfer are all read out in the cell. The redesign loop optimises for predicted binding; only the wet lab closes the gap to function.

Which checks each sprint passed and which it failed is recorded run by run in Sprints and Results.

7Codon optimisation

7.1 ViennaRNA

We used ViennaRNA on the assumption that reducing mRNA secondary-structure stability around the MF001 ribosome binding site and the start codon improves translation initiation. Performance was assessed by minimum free energy and unpaired-probability predictions, with codon adaptation index values against Bacillus subtilis 168 codon usage. Recoding codons 2 to 31 within the SamyQ signal peptide and the early CDS breaks inhibitory structure in the start+15 and SD core regions, making the start site easier for ribosomes to attach to.

The approach follows Castillo-Hair et al. (2019) on optogenetic control of B. subtilis gene expression. All analyses ran on the RS-inclusive Window A, modelling the pre-recombination transient mRNA state: RS (28 nt) + MF001 RBS (24 nt) + spacer (1 nt) + SamyQ signal peptide (93 nt) + first 90 nt of CDS.

MFE
Minimum free energy (kcal/mol) of the most stable secondary structure.
start+15 / start+30
Mean unpaired probability over the first 15 or 30 nucleotides from the start codon.
SD core
Mean unpaired probability over the 7-nucleotide Shine-Dalgarno core (AAGGAGG).
CAI
Codon adaptation index relative to B. subtilis 168 usage from the Kazusa database.

Phase 1 held the 93-nt SamyQ sequence static and optimised the first 15 codons of each passenger gene independently across 8,000 random synonymous variants, maximising a balance score of start+15 plus SD core. Phase 2 addressed elongation via CAI and appended a C-terminal 6×His tag; this was the June and July design, and the tag moved to the N-terminus for BoPEP4 constructs ordered after 16 August. Full-length optimisation degraded initiation by lowering start+15. Phase 3 departed from standard host adaptation by making SamyQ codons 2 to 31 editable, further reducing structure around the initiation site.

[Figure 2 | Predicted MFE secondary structures of RS-inclusive Window A before and after codon optimisation, four panels: (A) SamyQ-BoPEP4, (B) SamyQ-LEA14, (C) SamyQ-ACCD, (D) SamyQ-AmilCP. Each shows the 5′ end, the MF001 RBS, the start codon, and the mutated bases differing between original and optimised sequences.]

All four cargoes show the same pattern. The upstream RBS baseline barely moves, while the first stretch of the ORF rises in unpaired probability over a short window. Breaking the double-stranded stems at that point opens the start site, and the sequence in front of it never has to unwind.

7.2 SignalP 6.0

SignalP predicts whether a sequence carries a signal peptide, which secretion pathway it targets, and where signal peptidase cleaves. We use it for two purposes.

First, to confirm each signal peptide and mature sequence junction is cleaved where intended, so the released mature protein has the N-terminus we designed. This matters because the mature N-terminal residues carry charge rules affecting secretion efficiency, and one of our substitutions sits in that region.

Second, to check that no cleavage site is cryptically predicted inside the mature sequence, which would cut the protectant itself. A construct that passes structural triage and fails this check is not orderable.

Predictions were run across the whole signal peptide panel instead of one assumed choice, because the best signal peptide differs for each cargo and cannot be predicted from first principles.
