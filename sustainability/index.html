Overview

ReLEAF started as a way to help farmers cope with climate stress. As the project grew, we saw that sustainability had to be built into it. Our project started as a way to help farmers combat climate-related stress, but as we continued to do our project we realized that one of the best ways to combat that is to incorporate various concepts of sustainability into our project. Sustainability is not only limited to environmental sustainability, but also the sustainability of our societal structure, ensuring that the education and knowledge we provide to the next generation is sufficient to sustain current progress and advance, to sustain the rights of all people regardless of demographic. That is what sustainability is to GEMS-Taiwan, and here are some ways that we’ve tried to make our own contributions to the big mission that is sustainability. These are based on the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) agreed upon by the United Nation (UN) to achieve by the year 2030.

2 Zero hunger 4 Quality education 10 Reduced inequalities 12 Responsible consumption and production 15 Life on land

Why we chose the SDGs that we did

Out of all the 17 SDGs available, we chose our SDGs first based on their relation to our project, then we narrowed it down based on which ones we can actually make an impact on and change, whether through hypothetical implementation of our business plan, through current real-life action, or hypothetical usage and results of our bioreactor. Through the first condition, we can whittle the SDGs down to SDGs 2, 3, 4, 7, 10, 12, 13, and 15.

All of these SDGs are ones that we want to impact and might be able to through our project, but ultimately we had to condense it down to only the ones that we can actually do. For example, while Climate Action can be affected through improving the environment through using our bioreactor, it isn’t the main point of our project and neither is what we do rooted in resolving climate change in itself. Therefore, we might have an impact on the SDGs not chosen, which are SDGs 3, 7, and 13, but it’s not significant enough to be considered as an SDG that we can claim we target. After putting all the related SDGs through similar processes of elimination, we ended up with SDGs 2, 4, 10, 12, and 15, all of which are targets that we actively considered and either have or plan to enact in our project.

Our SDGs

SDG 2

Zero hunger

Problem

In 2024, over 295 million individuals experienced acute hunger in 53 different countries, and while part of this can be attributed to issues such as distribution, part of it is also caused by extreme temperatures in recent years that lead to crop loss (FAO 25). This is especially acute for farming families that not only sell their crops, but rely on their farm to feed themselves, forcing them to make compromises between eating and earning money. Early on into the project, we talked with the leader of Ubuntu Social Enterprise Ghana, Gilbert Nkpeniyeng. From that meeting, we learned that it’s true that climate is actively harming their crops and making it harder to farm on generational land.

Solution

Our protectants are designed to help crops to continue growing under stress, which can increase food production. By reducing the adverse effects of stress, the protectants allow plants to devote more energy for crop production. We’ve proven that plants can grow better under stress using protectants like ACCD with our Arabidopsis thaliana, which as a relative to mustard plants we know will at least translate into some other crops. In the long term, our bioreactor can help gradually increase the amount of crops that are produced and reduce the impact of chemical pollution by allowing the land to heal over time on its own. Though we do not intend to directly solve world hunger, we do hope to alleviate not only farmers’ financial pain but also provide more healthy and sustainable food sources to the world.

SDG 4

Quality education

Problem

A lot of people have misconceptions regarding synthetic biology due to not knowing where to look or not having access to the resources to learn about it. For example, many still have pre-existing stigmas on synthetic biology being a kind of zombie-apocalypse level threat to humanity because they don’t have easily accessible resources to teach/tell them otherwise outside of scientific papers that are hard to understand from the get go. This may cause them to openly show distrust or act ignorantly towards new inventions and products due to that very lack of understanding.

Solution

We have an education website where we compiled all of the education resources from this year and previous years, along with basic introductions to synthetic biology. Through this website, people can learn more about synthetic biology and teachers can sign up for us to teach lessons for their classes from the website. Additionally, we’ve done a public forum in collaboration with Green Media on 9/5/2026, where we introduce our project and explain about synthetic biology to the public. Through this, we hope to be able to reach out and tell people that synthetic biology is something that will be used to help and advance society, and encourage people to learn more about synthetic biology on their own.

SDG 10

Reduced inequalities

Problem

Despite agricultural production increasing since 1950, in-sector inequality between agricultural households and industrial farms has only increased (World Inequality Database 25). This makes it so that large farms can afford to constantly advance with technology, grow more crops, and create a loop that can easily generate profit while small scale farmers must be more careful and conservative in terms of technology, which can prevent them from increasing profit.

Solution

We plan to use the profit we obtain from selling devices to industrial farmers or selling data to researchers to subsidise small farmers’ purchases or allow unions or similar organisations to rent bioreactors instead, making the price more manageable and friendly towards them. This way, both sides of the spectrum can use and enjoy the benefits of having more advanced regulation and protectant technology without breaking the bank.

SDG 12

Responsible consumption and production

Problem

Using a lot of bacteria and lab-grade ingredients is not only expensive, but also unsustainable. Each purchase means more resources consumed and more money spent on both our and the farmer’s side, while perfectly good waste material from harvesting crops, or leftover cooking materials are left behind. 7.9 percent of all food harvested is thrown away as waste, which amounts to 3.6 million tonnes per year! (Food Waste in Primary Production in the UK 19)

Solution

Our culture medium turns agricultural and food-processing residues (hay, okara, papaya pulp and peels) into a growth medium for the bioreactor, instead of buying purified lab-grade ingredients. This means that farmers can actually create culture for the bacteria on their own, which can reduce the need to buy new culture mediums and help use part of the waste produced by farms.

SDG 15

Life on land

Problem

Chemical fertilizers and protectants often cause runoff and environmental pollution, which can lead to the destruction of local ecosystems. Issues such as eutrophication, accumulation of nitrate, and heavy metal buildup are all things that arise from the usage of chemical fertilizers and protectants on farms (Savci 12). This is not only detrimental to the environment, but also causes farmland to later become harder to cultivate crops on and make the resulting crops to be unhealthy for the human body.

Solution

While our product cannot reverse the damage done to the environment and soil directly, it can prevent further deterioration of its surrounding ecosystem. The protectants produced already exist in plants prior to exposure to our bioreactor and the safety measures in the bioreactor itself ensure containment unless under extenuating circumstances. The bioreactor is designed to keep the engineered B. subtilis inside with three layers: a genetic kill switch, a hollow-fibre membrane and a sealed housing. Usage of our bioreactor over time can allow for the previous pollution to slowly go away on its own while maintaining and gradually increasing the quality of soil.

Negatives

We can say without hesitation that we want our project to do as much good to the world as possible, but it’s unavoidable that the usage of our product might indirectly have adverse effects on the environment and attitudes of the people that use them. Here, we want to acknowledge those negatives as a part of our product that we hope we can change, but also realize that no matter how good something can be there will always be inherent adverse effects to everything.

Though we want to promote sustainability, and while our project’s method of producing protectants is undeniably more sustainable than chemical protectants, the usage of our product doesn’t promote longevity. Due to the fact that our product always immediately supplies plants with the necessary protectants, it might make it so that the plants never actually grow their own resilience and have to rely on our protectant to grow properly, which can actually be a net negative and set back the progress on plant resilience. This was mentioned by a natural farmer, Ms. Chen, who stated that her plants eventually grew to be as big as they are because she allowed them to grow and survive on their own – essentially survival of the fittest to let her plants thrive. Plants that use our protectants will have a bit of a hard time building resilience on their own, and may even succumb to climate stress more easily once taken off of our protectants. Because of her concern, we plan to release only as much protectant as the crop needs, so it can still build its own resilience.

Long-term impact

We want our work to last beyond this season, for future iGEM teams and for our community. We have already established part of this through our education website, which can provide resources for teachers that wish to inform their students of climate stress and shed light on synthetic biology and will remain permanently up. Additionally, through the business plan we have laid out development plans for up to 6 years, and what goals we want to achieve beyond iGEM. From our geospatial analysis, it can be seen where our project will have the largest impacts and where we are needed the most.

Conclusion

ReLEAF greatly values ensuring that our project and our product will benefit the world the best we can. Based on SDG 2 Zero Hunger, we increase crop yield so that small farmers can obtain increased food and economic security. In regards to SDG 12 Responsible Consumption and Production, we give some agricultural waste a new usage in a mixed bacteria culture made entirely of agricultural waste, which works just as well as LB medium. Another way we impact sustainability is through SDG 15 Life on Land, where our product can reduce chemical protectant usage, which allows the soil and environment to repair itself over time. Outside of physical impacts on sustainability, it’s also equally important to ensure the sustainability of our societal structure. That means equal opportunity to access necessary infrastructure and open access to knowledge. For SDG 4 Quality education, we do this through interacting and teaching about plant stress and synthetic biology at various schools across Taipei, along with a public website containing free teaching materials and educational games. Outside of that we affect SDG 10 Reduced Inequalities via our business model of subsidising small farmers and having different purchase models allows for all farmers to have the same level of access to our technology. Despite what our project tries to achieve, we still negatively affect crops in that they won’t build their own resilience to stress, because our protectants are always there to do the job for them. All in all, our project and product broadly impacts the mentioned SDGs, and will continue to strive to positively impact the world.
