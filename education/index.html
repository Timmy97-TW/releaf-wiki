Preparation

Overview

To make synthetic biology and the iGEM competition accessible, engaging, and inclusive for everyone, we created a dedicated education website.

Through this platform, we share our team's active journey, highlight our mission and vision, showcase our educational philosophy, and provide open-access tools for students and educators around the world.

Mission & Vision

We believe education is an important part because it allows us to share our research and ideas and make complex ideas more accessible to students. Through our promotion, we introduce topics like synthetic biology in a simple and engaging way, even for those with no prior background. We also hope that through our education, students of different ages are able to ignite their interest in this area and inspire them to think critically about real-world problems.

We aim to:

  • Integrate STEM learning with schools in an interactive way
  • Encourage interest in scientific research
  • Build teamwork, problem-solving, and communication skills
  • Help students think about how science impacts society

Education Website

Overview

In the process of developing our education lessons and events, we realized that it was important for people to have the ability to access our lessons and other resources in order to create a sustainable form of education. Driven by this thought, we decided to create a website of our own that could serve as a centralized center where students and educators alike could easily be able to freely access things such as interactive learning modules, ready-to-use lesson plans, etc. By transforming our materials into open-access tools, we aim to extend our reach beyond one-time workshops and empower communities to explore and teach about synthetic biology and our projects independently.

Development

In the process of developing this website, we wanted to focus on building an intuitive, user-friendly environment tailored to a diverse audience ranging from elementary students to high school educators.

To bring the platform to life, we focused on three main goals:

  • Trust & Transparency: Learning environment that provides clear information about who we are as an iGEM team, alongside age-appropriate, verified science content.
  • Clarity: Created clean, easy-to-use menus so users can explore without getting lost.
  • Accessible Resources: Direct download links for educators or students to access lesson plans, slides, and worksheets instantly.

Together, we integrated all these ideas and our thought process into one integrated website that includes the following information/content:

Designing The Lesson

Overview

Designing an effective educational lesson requires moving from big ideas to a smooth, classroom-ready reality. Before stepping into a classroom, our team dedicated significant time to carefully planning, testing, and refining our educational content. We wanted to make sure our synthetic biology concepts were not only accurate and age-appropriate, but also engaging, safe, and easy for teachers. This phase outlines the process we used to transform complex scientific concepts into hands-on learning experiences for students across all age groups.

Design Process

The whole process of designing our lessons revolved around these main four steps, developing lesson plans with varying educational levels, trialing games and experiments we plan to carry out during our lessons/workshops, having a full run-through session of our lesson, and lastly communicating and reaching out to schools. Below are our proceedings in more detail:

1 · Lesson Plan Development one team member per age group, content checked against what schools already teach

Before we started designing, we split the work so each team member focused on a single age group: primary, middle, or high school. This approach ensured every lesson plan received dedicated attention, allowing us to tailor the scientific depth, vocabulary, and teaching methods specifically to that age group’s learning capabilities. Next, we brainstormed the type of activities and content best suited for each group. We focused on keeping core synthetic biology, plant biology, and sustainability concepts consistent and present throughout all age groups while introducing aspects of iGEM and the project we were working on. We then scaled the complexity and depth of these details depending on the target age group. Other than just including things we felt were important, we also took the time to verify whether the content we were planning on teaching were already being taught in schools, ensuring our lessons didn't just repeat classroom material, but instead introduced fresh ideas to inspire students and spark their interest in new areas.

2 · Trialing every experiment, craft and game tested by the team first

Next, after our lesson plans underwent initial review, we put our ideas to the test by trialing the experiments, arts & crafts, and games. We tested each activity firsthand to evaluate timing, gauge student engagement, and troubleshoot potential safety or technical issues. This hands-on trial phase allowed us to fine-tune every component, ensuring that the activities were both fun and seamlessly integrated into the lesson structure.

3 · Contacting Schools to collaborate with direct email, plus a sign-up form on the website

To share our curriculum, we compiled a target list of schools and reached out directly via email to gauge their interest. We also created an easy sign-up sheet using Google Forms, which we included on the “Get Involved” page of our website. This simplified process for interested educators and gave schools a quick, accessible way to partner with us for our workshops.

4 · Run-through Sessions a full practice run about a week before each event

Once school dates were confirmed, we assigned team members to each session. We then divided the slides and tasks so everyone could present topics they knew best. About a week before each event, we held full practice runs to simulate a real classroom setting and keep everyone on track. During these run-throughs, we also tested our games and experiments to fix any issues and ensure everything ran smoothly on the day of the event. Using these sessions helped us check whether the lesson plan contents and time allocation for each section was appropriate for the age group we were teaching.

Execution

In the world we live in nowadays, synthetic biology and other related areas are often associated with negative perceptions due to misconceptions surrounding the topic. As a result heated debates surrounding genetic engineering and biotechnology erupted with some questioning the morals and safety of continuing this practice. Additionally, students have limited opportunities to learn about synthetic biology, climate change, and sustainability through engaging, age-appropriate educational experiences. After recognizing this gap, we sought to provide students of all ages and the public with educational experiences that foster and encourage informed discussions about synthetic biology and its real-world applications. Lastly, throughout our events, sustainability and the development of reusable educational materials have also been a central focus in both the planning and execution of our educational activities.

This whole process was integrated using the DBTL (Design, Build/Prep, Test, Learn) where we designed the lessons and materials, built and prepared the educational activities, tested them through implementation in schools, and learned from survey results and feedback to refine and improve future sessions.

We intentionally planned our event dates to give us enough time to make necessary adjustments between sessions. Grouping these events into trials allowed us to compare them over time and see exactly how our ongoing improvements boosted the students' knowledge, attitudes, and skills.

To achieve the goals set above, we thoughtfully designed events that revolved around age-appropriate and interactive learning experiences that could actively engage students into learning about simplified biological concepts and thinking about the implications of synthetic biology to the world around them. With these thoughts in mind, each of our sessions are structured to introduce key concepts such as climate change, plant biology, and synthetic biology, ensuring that students could build understanding step by step. Our teaching materials, including presentations, games, experiments, and hands-on activities, were developed to encourage participation and curiosity, while also reinforcing core concepts in an accessible way.

However, we realized that learning goes both ways and that in order to bring the best experiences to those around us, we had to continue to evaluate ourselves and make improvements on our end. To evaluate and improve our sessions, we implemented pre- and post-education surveys that allowed us to assess the students' baseline knowledge before the event and the outcome of their learning afterwards. The post-education survey was designed using the K-A-P (Knowledge-Attitude-Practice) framework which included questions of all types including multiple choice, 1-5 scale, and free response questions that allowed us to assess the knowledge they had gained but also if they had any changes in opinions when it came to perceptions, and intended behaviors in their future. All the data collected was used to help better our program for subsequent events.

Lastly, to ensure long-term impact, we promoted sustainable education through our website and survey-based evaluation of the willingness of teachers to continue engaging with our program in the following years. We made our resources such as teaching materials, educational games, and other learning tools more accessible to both teachers and students. To achieve this, we created a website that provides free access to all of our educational content. This includes downloadable lesson plans, slides, worksheets, comics, and games developed in the previous years as well as this year. In addition, the website also includes introductory content on iGEM and synthetic biology to provide users with a clearer understanding of the field and our project context.

Elementary: Little Plant Guardians

Overview

This year, our team visited 3 elementary schools spanning over the months of June and July. During these lessons, we delivered an interactive Synbio workshop titled “Little Plant Guardians: Combating Environmental Stress”. These lessons were made for elementary students ranging from fourth to sixth grade with the goal of raising awareness and sparking interest in topics such as climate change and its implications on plant stress, synthetic biology, and simple ideas behind photosynthesis and how DNA works.

In order to integrate the DBTL process into our lessons we separated the five lessons we held into three different trials: Preliminary trial (June 24th, 2026), Trial 1 (June 25th/26th, 2026), Trial 2 (June 29th, 2026), and Trial 3 (July 14th, 2026). Using this method, we hoped to see improvements in the results of the surveys we designed alongside the lesson plan.

Lesson Plan

Pre-Survey seven multiple-choice questions on Kahoot, before teaching

In order to assess the baseline knowledge students had towards the topic before the lesson and to compare these results to the Post-Survey results, we designed a simple survey hosted on Kahoot which consisted of seven multiple choice questions asking about simple concepts that will be taught in the lesson that day.

Introduction: Questions of the Day climate change and what it means for plants and people

To start off our lesson we posed a few questions that asked students to discuss the implications of climate change and how that would impact plants and the way humans lived. After, we also introduced a few problems being caused by climate change and how that would impact the world around us. Through these questions, the students were able to familiarize themselves with the contents of the lesson as well as to prompt further reflection on related issues.

Vocabulary illustrated slides, meanings inferred from pictures and prefixes

Next, we moved onto simple vocabulary, including words that were related to today's ideas such as biotic, climate change, photosynthesis, genetics, etc. We utilized illustrated slideshows to introduce new vocabulary, encouraging students to infer word meanings through visual cues and prefix analysis, making students feel more engaged in our workshop.

Photosynthesis a short video and a summary slide of reactants and products

After vocabulary, we moved onto learning about the process of photosynthesis and the materials involved in the reactants and products of the process. This concept was introduced through a simple yet captivating video as well as a summary of the process shown in the slideshow. By breaking down the complex system and process of photosynthesis into more accessible concepts, students were able to better grasp the main ideas.

Plant Stress biotic and abiotic stress, and how plants cope

In the following section, we introduced the different types of stress that plants face in two categories: biotic and abiotic, defining biotic stress as damage caused by living organisms like pests and pathogens, and abiotic stress as harm resulting from non-living environmental factors like drought and extreme temperatures. We then analyzed the unique coping mechanisms and physiological changes plants undergo to survive these distinct pressures.

Experiment spinach leaf disks float under light, and heat slows them down

We then moved on to conducting a simple experiment that allowed students to visualize what photosynthesis would look like in real time. The students were thrilled and in awe to watch tiny spinach leaf disks, initially sunk at the bottom of a beaker, slowly float to the surface as they generated oxygen under a light source. To connect back to the core idea of our project, we introduced heat as a variable in the experiment, this was done by replacing the room-temperature water with warmer water. As a result of this, the flotation process slowed down significantly or even stopped, showing the students that excessive heat can damage plant enzymes and hinder photosynthesis. Throughout the experiment, students were given worksheets that encouraged them to record experiment results as well as to discuss the results of the experiment with team members.

The Role of Science ATCG base pairing at the board, then synthetic biology

Later, we moved onto genetics introducing the concept of ATCG base pairing and how DNA passes down from one generation to the next. Students were chosen to come up to the front of the class to write down the correct sequence that corresponded to the example given to them. We then connect genetics to synthetic biology and its ability to help solve real-world problems such as the ones being discussed in the class.

Interactive Card Game an original DNA base-pairing card game, played like Joker

To reinforce these concepts, we created an original card game based on DNA base pairing. Played similarly to ‘Joker,’ the game challenged students to pair the correct ATCG letters together, making the learning process highly interactive and solidifying their memory of how DNA bases pair up.

Arts & Craft: Self-Watering Plant a recycled PET bottle and a rope wick

Near the end of the lesson, students participated in a hands-on arts and crafts activity, transforming recycled PET bottles into self-watering planters using a basic rope wick. This provided a perfect tangible analogy for our overall project on automated protectant release. It allowed students to visualize how a continuous, automated supply system can autonomously protect a plant from environmental pressures without needing constant manual intervention.

Post-Survey and Feedback knowledge, attitude and practice, plus free response

At the end of the lesson, we asked students to fill out a survey to assess the effectiveness and the impact of our workshop. The survey was designed following three main categories: K (knowledge), A (attitude), and P (practice), it also included free response questions where students were free to share things like their favorite part of the lesson as well as any concerns or feedback they have for our program.

Junior High: The Battle Against Climate

Overview

Our team visited 1 junior high school this year spanning over the months of June and July. During our education event, we presented a Synbio workshop titled “The Battle Against Climate: Miniature Projects to Protect Plants”. The lessons were designed for students spanning across grades 7 to 9 covering experiments, debates, and even a card game. The goal of the education event was to tackle topics such as photosynthesis, climate change, and synthetic biology.

Lesson Plan

  • PDF Junior High School Lesson Slides 73 slides, Chinese and English · 8.4 MB

The lesson plan and the printable card game for this level are not online yet.

Pre-education Survey seven multiple-choice questions on Kahoot

In order to assess the students’ baseline knowledge, we created a Kahoot with seven multiple choice questions based off the topic we would be teaching later. By assessing these results, we can compare them with the post-education result to see how much the students learned through our lesson.

Introduction climate change, plant stresses, and how plants defend themselves

To start off our lesson, we begin with introducing climate change and plant stresses. We begin by addressing the different types of stresses there are. For example, we mention high temperatures, droughts, flooding, and pests/diseases. As most students are familiar with the different types of plant stresses, we introduce the two terms biotic and abiotic. After, we introduce ways plants can defend against the varying stresses, for example, closing the stomata or producing stress-hormone responses.

Photosynthesis the chemical equation, and an online game on what plants need

We start off the section by introducing the chemical equation behind photosynthesis and its main idea. Following that, we ask a few questions regarding how stress affects leaves and whether or not photosynthesis actually occurs. We showed them an online game that allows student to apply different amount of vital necessities plants need to clearly showcase the effect of each factor.

Experiment the spinach leaf-disk experiment, with heat as the variable

We then moved into a hands-on experiment designed to let students actually watch photosynthesis happen in real time. Using spinach leaf disks that initially sank to the bottom of a beaker, students observed with excitement as the disks slowly rose to the surface, propelled by the oxygen bubbles they produced under a light source. To tie the experiment back to our project's central theme, we introduced heat as a variable by swapping the room-temperature water for warmer water. This caused the flotation process to slow dramatically or stop altogether, giving students a clear, visual demonstration that excess heat damages plant enzymes and disrupts photosynthesis. Throughout the activity, students used worksheets to record their observations and discuss their findings with teammates.

Synthetic Biology DNA structure and function, real applications, and our project

The next section we did was introduce synthetic biology and how synbio can help relieve plant stress. Because DNA was a fundamental basis of synthetic biology, the lesson plan incorporated basic definitions of the function of the DNA and its structure. To tie it back to our project, we mentioned real world applications of synthetic biology and briefly introduced our project as well.

Card Game spin for a stress, then defend with a Sensor and an Action card

To make the lesson more fun, we also created a card game that enables the students to understand the effects of stress on plants. Each round begins by spinning the wheel to reveal a stress. All players then simultaneously check their hands, laying cards face-up if they hold a valid combo (a Sensor plus an Action card matching the revealed stress, with an optional Boost card). Players who form a valid combo are safe and discard those cards, while players who cannot may play a Functional card to steal or swap cards from a teammate who already succeeded. Anyone still without a valid combo after that receives one disaster token, and the next round begins.

Debate two teams, three speakers each, on synthetic biology in agriculture

Lastly, to end our lesson, we asked the students to participate in a debate. Currently, using synthetic biology on plants is a major debate throughout society, so through this activity students get to understand why exactly synthetic biology is controversial. Students split into two teams, Supporting (in favor of synthetic biology) and Opposing (against it), and are given 8 minutes to prepare their arguments. Each team selects 3 speakers, who take turns presenting in order, discussing for 45 seconds as a team before each turn and then speaking individually for 2 minutes. The first speaker lays out their team's evidence and reasoning, the second rebuts the opposing team's first speaker using their own team's evidence and reasoning, and the third summarizes the round and explains why their side should be favored over the opponent's.

Post-education Survey knowledge, attitude and practice, plus open questions

To wrap up the lesson, students completed a survey designed to gauge the workshop's effectiveness and overall impact. The survey was structured around three core categories, Knowledge (K), Attitude (A), and Practice (P), and also included open-ended questions, giving students the opportunity to share their favorite parts of the lesson along with any concerns or feedback for the program.

Summary

Through our education program, we introduced junior high school students to plant stress, photosynthesis, climate change, and synthetic biology through a combination of interactive lessons, hands-on experiments, games, and debates. The spinach leaf-disk experiment provided students with a visual demonstration of how heat can affect photosynthesis, while the card game and debate encouraged them to apply synthetic biology concepts and consider different perspectives on its use in agriculture. Pre- and post-education surveys allowed us to evaluate changes in students’ knowledge, attitudes, and intended practices. Overall, the program successfully combined scientific learning with active participation, while student feedback highlighted the need to reduce lecture time, expand interactive activities, and provide additional visual support for difficult concepts such as DNA base pairing. These findings will guide us in improving future workshops and making our education program more engaging and accessible.

High School: Decoding the Genetic Code

Overview

During August, our team visited 1 high school to expand our educational initiative. In the lesson, we conducted a Synbio workshop titled “Decoding the Genetic Code: From DNA to the Future of Food Security”. Our lessons were designed to engage students across 10th to 12th grade through hands-on experiment, mutation game, and discussions on topics such as plasmolysis, DNA mutation, and abiotic and biotic stress. Specifically, we tailored the lesson slides and created a lesson plan for students to better understand the concept.

Lesson Plan

  • PDF High School Lesson Slides 47 slides, Chinese and English · 13.6 MB
Pre-education Survey six multiple-choice questions on Kahoot

Before our lesson, we designed a short survey that includes six multiple-choice questions on Kahoot in order to assess the students’ baseline knowledge and provide them with a preview of the topics that would be covered in the lesson. The results were then compared with those of the post-survey to evaluate students’ improvement after the lesson.

Introduction iGEM, and what synthetic biology is used for

To begin our lesson, we start off by introducing the students to the iGEM competition to provide them with a basic understanding of its purpose and the concepts behind synthetic biology. Afterward, we explained the concept of synthetic biology and introduced several of its real-world applications, such as engineering microorganisms to produce biofuels and creating biosensors that detect and remove environmental pollutants.

Plant Stress opened with a guiding question, then biotic and abiotic stress

Next, we started the section with a guiding question: “As the Earth gets warmer and drier, what will happen to the plants?”, which allowed students to establish a connection with the concepts that would be introduced in the following section. We then introduced different types of plant stress, including biotic stress caused by pests and pathogens and abiotic stress caused by factors such as salinity and drought, as well as how these stresses can affect our daily lives. To help students better understand the experiment that would be conducted later, we also explained the defense mechanisms of plants when facing environmental stress.

Experiment: Plasmolysis onion cells in 3 M and 6 M salt solutions

After explaining the effects of stress on plants, we then moved on to the experiment to provide students with a more concrete demonstration of how salinity stress can affect plant cells. In the experiment, the students observed the changes in the pigment within onion cells before and after applying salt solutions. To demonstrate the effects of different levels of salinity stress, we prepared 3 M and 6 M salt solutions for different groups of students. After applying the salt solutions for 15 minutes, the students compared the effects of the different salt concentrations and observed how the pigments within the cells changed in response to the salinity stress.

Photosynthesis the leaf-disk experiment on video, while the onion soaked

While waiting for the onion to fully immerse in the salt solution, we prepared a short video that demonstrates and visualizes photosynthesis. In the video, we prepared two beakers that contained different temperatures of water: room-temperature water and warmer water, and they were placed under the light source. Each beaker contained small spinach leaf disks that initially sank to the bottom. After a certain period of time, the disks were propelled by the oxygen bubbles they produced and slowly rose to the surface. However, the disks in the warmer water didn’t rise as effectively as the disks in the room-temperature water. This experiment demonstrates how heat can damage plant enzymes and disrupt photosynthesis. After the experiment section, the video explained the mechanism of the experiment and asked several questions to help the students better understand the concepts.

Synthetic Biology farmers' challenges, then our stress-responsive bioreactor

After the video, we introduced the students to our team and our project, ReLeaf. In this section, we first discussed some of the challenges faced by farmers. We then explained how our stress-responsive bioreactor works, as well as the synthetic biology mechanisms we designed to activate the system and release plant protectants in response to environmental stress, helping to protect plants grown in both soil and hydroponic systems.

DNA base pairing, transcription, translation, amino acids

After concluding the experiment section, we briefly reviewed several fundamental DNA concepts, including base-pairing rules, transcription, translation, and the role of amino acids in protein synthesis.

DNA Mutation Sighter, Messenger and Builder, then engineered mutations and their trade-offs

To help students better understand the processes of transcription, translation, and mutation in a more engaging and interactive way, we organized a game in which students worked together to pass a genetic sequence down the line. Students were given one of three different roles: the Sighter, Messenger, and Builder. The Sighter transcribed a DNA sequence and passed the transcribed sequence to the next student. The Messenger passed down the sequence to the next person. The Builder translated the sequence received from the student behind them. After the Builder wrote down their answer, each group checked their answers with the answer key. Through this interactive game, students can picture how genetic information is translated and transcribed more easily and understand how changes or errors in the sequence can lead to mutations.

Lastly, we introduced the students to how engineered mutations can enhance plant protectant release in order to maximize extracellular protectant release while maintaining bacterial viability. In this section, we explained how different mutations target different components of the stress-responsive system to enhance protectant release through three distinct mechanisms: increase receptor sensitivity to the stress signal, engineer the export protein, and modify the protectant molecule for improved membrane permeability. We also explained the trade-offs that need to be considered to balance protectant release efficiency with bacterial viability, as overproduction may place additional stress on the bacteria and reduce their overall viability.

Post-education Survey knowledge, attitude and action, plus three open questions

To conclude the lesson, we designed a post-survey consisting of three core categories: Knowledge (K), Attitude (A), and Action (A). The survey allowed us to evaluate the effectiveness of our educational lesson by assessing students’ knowledge, attitudes, and willingness to engage in relevant actions in the future. It also included three open-ended questions, which provided students with an opportunity to share their feedback, thoughts, and concerns regarding the lesson.

At the end of the survey, several students expressed curiosity about how DNA sequences can be modified and indicated that they would like a more detailed explanation of how mutations occur. Beyond suggestions related to the lesson content, students also provided feedback on the presentation and delivery, such as “the speakers’ voices could be louder,” “the font could be bigger,” and “there could be fewer words on the slides.” As we were teaching a class of 28 students within a limited timeframe, we attempted to incorporate and deliver as much knowledge as possible. However, in doing so, we did not sufficiently consider whether the students could effectively absorb and follow all of the information presented. Looking at the results from the Knowledge section of the survey, the plasmolysis question had the highest percentage of correct responses in the post-survey. This suggests that the interactive, hands-on learning experience may have contributed to students’ understanding of the concept.

Summary

Overall, the responses and feedback suggest that the hands-on plasmolysis experiment and interactive games contributed to higher student engagement and stronger knowledge outcomes compared with the more traditional lecture-based components of the lesson. Based on this observation, we recognized the importance of incorporating more interactive elements throughout the lesson to further enhance student engagement and understanding.

Card Game #1: ATCG matching elementary; complementary base pairing, with two Jokers that can never be paired

Goal

Introduce young learners to the foundational concepts of molecular biology by demonstrating how DNA codons operate and teaching them the fundamental rules of complementary base pairing (A with T, and C with G) through an engaging, accessible medium.

How We Designed to Achieve the Goals / How to Play

We designed and made a simple card game called the ATCG DNA Card Game:

  • Base Matching Mechanics: Each card features a 3-letter sequence made up of DNA bases. To form a pair and discard it from your hand, you must pair a card with its exact complementary match:
    • A pairs with T (e.g., AAA matches TTT)
    • C pairs with G (e.g., CAG matches GTC)
  • The Jokers: The two Jokers represent sequences using letters outside the standard ATCG alphabet, making them impossible to pair using normal DNA base-pairing rules.
  • Gameplay Loop: Players draw and trade cards to find matches, try to throw out completed pairs, and avoid getting stuck with the unmatchable non-ATCG Joker cards.

Feedback (from Education)

The response from our educational outreach sessions was overwhelmingly positive, with a significant majority of the participating students highlighting the card game as the absolute standout, favorite part of the entire lesson. Teachers and facilitators noted that the interactive element kept the children actively engaged, helping reinforce complex biological concepts through play much more effectively than standard teaching methods.

Card Game #2: Super Bacteria: Zero-Hour Rescue junior high; sensors, actions and boosts against a Climate Wheel

Goal

We built this game to make our synthetic biology research fun and easy to understand, showing how biological sensors and protective peptides work together to save crops from climate threats. It gives players a hands-on feel for real-world teamwork and problem-solving, turning complex science into a simple story about protecting Taiwan's agriculture.

How We Designed to Achieve the Goals / How to Play

Setup

  • 2 to 5 players.
  • Deal 5 cards and 3 Gene Coins to each player.
  • Place the Climate Wheel and 3 Disaster Tokens in the center.

Card Types

  • Sensor (Blue), Action (Green), Boost (Yellow): Played together to build combos.
  • Functional (Purple): Free special abilities played anytime on your turn.
  • Stress Events (Red): Immediate hazards drawn from the deck.

Player Turn (4 Steps)

  1. Play: Put 1 to 3 cards face-up on the table (cards are shared by everyone).
  2. Combo: Discard 1 Sensor + 1 Action + 1 Boost from the table to complete a defense combo.
  3. Spin: Spin the Climate Wheel.
    • Combo completed: Safe.
    • No combo completed: Add 1 Disaster Token.
    • Double Disaster sector: Requires 2 combos to stay safe.
  4. Draw: Refill hand back to 5 cards. Pass clockwise.

Gene Coins

  • 1 Coin: Discard 1 card and draw a new one.
  • 2 Coins: Copy a card played by a teammate.
  • Gain Coins: Everyone gets +1 coin when any combo is completed.

Feedback (from Education)

After the lesson, students had to fill out a survey where many had mentioned that playing games were their favorite aspect. Additionally, they mentioned how the card game had made them understand better how sensors and peptides responded to climate stress and helped them learn new things.

Online Game #2: Releafamon vs Distressamon a two-player battle game on plant stress and the engineering pathway

Goal

  • We created Releafamon vs Distressamon to teach students how plants respond to environmental stress and how synthetic biology can be used to protect crops. By turning plant biology, climate stress, and the biological engineering process into a Pokémon-inspired battle game, we made these concepts more engaging and accessible to younger and non-specialist audiences.
  • The game allows players to experience the balance between maintaining plant health and developing a biological solution. Players must manage limited resources, conduct research, and complete an engineering pathway while responding to attacks caused by environmental stresses.

How We Designed the Game to Achieve These Goals

  • Biology-Based Mechanics: Releafamon collect Water and CO₂ and use photosynthesis to produce Sugar. These resources are required for survival, research, and biological engineering.
  • Environmental Stress as Enemies: Distressamon represent threats such as salinity, drought, pathogens, and other environmental stresses that damage plants or remove essential resources.
  • Synthetic Biology Pathway: The Releafamon player must complete five engineering stages to develop and deliver a protective biological product.
  • Bioreactor System: The game includes a bioreactor with a 50% success rate, demonstrating that biological production is not always predictable.
  • Research and Upgrades: Players earn Research Points that can be used to strengthen their Releafamon and unlock instructors.
  • Instructor System: Instructors are unlocked through Research Points and damage milestones. Each instructor permanently provides one additional Action Point and heals one specific Releafamon.
  • Strategic Trade-Offs: Players must decide whether to spend their actions gathering resources, attacking, researching, upgrading, or progressing through the engineering pathway.
  • Bilingual Accessibility: The game includes both English and Traditional Chinese, allowing it to reach a wider range of students in Taiwan.
  • Engaging Presentation: Pixel-art characters, animated attacks, sound effects, multiple battle environments, and familiar turn-based mechanics make the scientific content feel like part of the gameplay rather than a separate lecture.

How to Play

  • Two-Player Format: One player controls the Releafamon team, while the other controls the three Distressamon. Players take turns using the same device.
  • Action Points: Each player begins with a limited number of Action Points per turn. Actions can be used to gather resources, attack, switch characters, purchase upgrades, or advance the engineering process.
  • Photosynthesis: The Releafamon player can spend one Water and one CO₂ to produce Sugar. Photosynthesis does not consume an Action Point.
  • Resource Management: Distressamon can drain Water, CO₂, and other resources. If the Releafamon player has zero of any essential resource at the end of the Distressamon turn, the active Releafamon takes three damage.
  • Releafamon Abilities: Releafamon can attack, heal, create shields, collect resources, and purchase stronger versions of their abilities.
  • Distressamon Abilities: Each Distressamon has attacks, stacking debuffs, a one-use Terrain ability, and a powerful Disaster move that unlocks after enough damage has been dealt.
  • Terrain Effects: Terrain lasts for three Distressamon turns and only benefits the Distressamon that created it. It increases damage by 20% and removes one additional resource.
  • Research and Instructors: The Releafamon player earns Research Points and completes damage challenges to unlock instructors. Each instructor permanently adds one Action Point, allowing the player to perform more actions in later turns.
  • Releafamon Victory: The Releafamon player wins by completing all five engineering stages and successfully delivering the final protective product, or by defeating all three Distressamon.
  • Distressamon Victory: The Distressamon player wins immediately if any member of the Releafamon team is defeated.

Educational Value

  • Through gameplay, students learn that protecting crops requires more than simply defeating an environmental threat. They must maintain photosynthesis, manage limited biological resources, conduct research, optimize a bioreactor, and complete an entire engineering process before a protective product can be delivered.
  • The game also demonstrates that synthetic biology involves experimentation, uncertainty, and strategic decision-making. Because every scientific concept is connected to a visible gameplay mechanic, students can learn through experience instead of memorizing isolated definitions.

Feedback and Evaluation

Formal student feedback will be collected during future educational outreach sessions. Students will be asked whether the game improved their understanding of plant stress, photosynthesis, bioreactors, and the synthetic biology engineering process. Their feedback and gameplay performance will also be used to improve the game’s scientific clarity, difficulty, balance, and accessibility.

Long Term Impacts

Overview

Education, in our eyes, isn't only about sharing information and teaching at one specific point of time, instead, it should be something that students are able to carry forward with them and continue to share and find useful over a period of time. Our education program focuses on helping students understand plant stress, climate change, sustainability, and synthetic biology through hands-on activities and interactive learning. However, we also wanted to consider what would happen after our lessons were finished. This is why throughout our education journey, one of our major goals was to create an education that was sustainable.

Mutual Learning and Dialogue

Our Education was implemented in a way where both sides were mutually able to contribute to the conversation rather than just a one way lecture. We employed the use of interactive feedback loops through post-activity survey forms. This feedback allowed us to ascertain the student’s perspectives on climate change and synthetic biology directly guided our lesson adaptations. Moreover, during the lesson itself, rather than only talking about the advantages of synthetic biology, we included debates as part of the lesson itself to understand different perspectives that students might have towards synthetic biology and our product, even if those perspectives were not aligned with ours.

Implementation Process

Our educational rollout followed a deliberate, multi-stage process that began with assessing audience needs and language requirements, leading to bilingual (English and Traditional Chinese) materials. We then tested our workshops and lessons with members of the team, ensuring that instructions were clear, material was engaging, and the content was at the right level for the students. After the lesson, we established a feedback loop using participant feedback to optimize lesson pacing, simplify complex biological concepts, and build a lesson that was impactful.

What is Sustainable Education?

Sustainable education is education that creates lasting learning opportunities and can continue to benefit students and communities over time. It focuses not only on what students learn today, but also on creating resources, knowledge, and learning environments that can be passed on to future learners.

Below are five of the main points we have identified that make up parts of completely sustainable education:

Environmental Sustainability

Education should encourage awareness of environmental challenges and help students understand how their actions and knowledge can contribute to a more sustainable future.

Accessible Education

The education should be easily accessible to students everywhere, including those from different backgrounds with resources that are flexible and can be used in different situations.

Long-Term Learning

The education should be something students can continue building on outside of class time, not just a one time event.

Sustainable Resources

Educational practices and resources should be practical and reusable, allowing schools and teachers to continue using them without relying on one-time programs or outside support.

Preparing for the Future

Education should equip students with knowledge, skills, and awareness that can help them understand and respond to future challenges.

How we Achieved Sustainable Education

Here is how our team achieved sustainable education, using the five points presented above:

Environmental Sustainability

Our education program focuses on helping students understand environmental challenges and their connection to plants, agriculture, and climate change. Through our lessons and activities on plant stress, students explore how factors such as extreme heat, drought, salinity, and disease can affect plants and food production. By introducing these topics at a young age, we hope to build greater awareness of environmental challenges and encourage students to think about how science and technology can contribute to a more sustainable future.

Accessible Education

We wanted our education materials to be accessible to students and teachers beyond the people we were able to reach directly. To achieve this, we created a website that can be freely accessed which includes lesson plans, presentations, worksheets, activities, and other learning materials from this year as well as the past two years. Our materials are also mostly available in both English and Traditional Chinese so that they can be used in different learning environments and reach a wider range of students.

Long-Term Learning

Rather than designing our education program around a single activity or lesson, we aimed to give students knowledge that they can continue building on. Our activities introduce concepts such as climate change, plant stress, DNA, and synthetic biology in a way that connects different ideas together. We also provide resources that allow students and teachers to revisit these topics after our lessons have ended, encouraging learning to continue beyond our direct interactions.

Sustainable Resources

To make our education program last beyond our iGEM season, we created resources that can be reused and adapted by teachers and students. Our lesson plans, slides, worksheets, games, and other materials are designed to be used without requiring our team to be physically present as they contain instructions for contents that may be more complex. By making these resources available online, teachers can continue incorporating them into their own classrooms, while future students can benefit from materials created during our project.

Preparing for the Future

Our education program introduces students to issues that will become increasingly important in the future, including climate change, food security, environmental stress, and sustainable agriculture. At the same time, we introduce synthetic biology as one of the possible approaches to addressing these challenges. By giving students both an understanding of these problems and an opportunity to explore scientific solutions, we hope to encourage curiosity and help them become more prepared to engage with future challenges.

Conclusion

Through our education program, we aimed to create an impact that extends beyond the lessons we conducted this year. By combining environmental awareness, accessibility, long-term learning, reusable resources, and future-oriented knowledge, we hope that the materials and ideas developed through ReLeaf can continue to be shared, adapted, and used by students and teachers alike in the future.
